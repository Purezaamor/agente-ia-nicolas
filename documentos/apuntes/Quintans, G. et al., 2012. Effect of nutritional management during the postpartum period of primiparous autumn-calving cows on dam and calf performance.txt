primiparous autumn-calving cows on dam and calf performance
under range conditions

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of different winter nutritional management
regimens in primiparous, autumn-calving heifers on dam and calf performance. In experiment
1, 41 Aberdeen Angus × Hereford fall-calving beef heifers grazed native pastures (NP) for
78 days postpartum (pp) when they were assigned to one of two treatments during 94 days
of winter: (i) grazing NP (NP; n = 21); (ii) grazing improved pastures with Lotus subbiflorus
(IP, n = 20). Calves were weaned when dams reached 203 days pp and cows were artificially
inseminated (AI) at 237 days pp. In experiment 2, 41 Aberdeen Angus × Hereford fall-calving
beef heifers grazed NP for 87 days pp, when they were assigned to one of three treatments
during 90 days of winter: (i) grazing NP (NP, n = 14); (ii) grazing improved pastures with
L. subbiflorus (IP; n = 13); (iii) grazing native pastures supplemented with a concentrate
(S, n = 14). Supplemented cows were offered a concentrate (16% CP) daily at 1% of BW. On
day 210 pp, calves were weaned and AI in cows was conducted on day 270 pp. Body weight
(BW), body condition score (BCS), milk production (MP), presence of corpus luteum (CL) and
pregnancy rate (PR) were measured during the experiment. In Experiment 1, at the end of win-
ter, cows in the IP group weighed more (P b 0.05) and had greater (P b 0.05) BCS than cows in
NP; these differences were maintained until day 270 pp. When AI commenced there were no
difference in the incidence of CL presence and final PR was similar for cows in both treatments.
MP was greater (P b 0.05) in IP than in NP cows (average: 3.8 vs. 2.4 kg/day). At weaning calves
in IP were heavier than calves in NP group (182.6 vs. 117.3 kg). In Experiment 2 from the end of
winter until AI, cows in the S group weighed more (P b 0.05) and had greater (P b 0.05) BCS than
cows in NP and IP. At the beginning of AI there was a tendency (P = 0.08) for a higher percent-
age of cows in NP with CL compared to IP (86 vs. 54%), and there was no difference compared to
S cows (71%). Final PR was similar for cows in all treatments (93, 92 and 100% for NP, IP and S,
respectively). Milk production was greater (P b 0.001) in S than in NP cows (average: 3.8 vs.
2.8 kg/day), and no differences were found with respect to IP cows (3.3 kg/day). At weaning,
calves from S were heavier than calves from IP. Both these groups of calves were heavier than
NP calves (141.5, 134.1 vs. 122.4 kg; P b 0.05). Reproductive performance in autumn-calving
cows managed during winter on native pastures was not affected during the following spring,
but calves were lighter at weaning than those kept in better winter nutritional conditions.

1. Introduction
Age at first service is considered an important issue in beef
cow–calf systems since it affects the reproductive performance

during the productive lifespan of the cow (Lesmeister et al.,
1973). In Uruguay, most beef cattle grow on native pastures
with a high variation of productivity during the year which is
also highly dependent on weather conditions. In fact, it has
been reported that those systems where native pastures are
the main component of the diet, the pattern of food intake is
not constant during the season (Wright et al., 1992). Conse-
quently, in Uruguay the growth rate of female calves is variable
during the early years where 50% of heifers are first mated at an
age of 2 years old and the rest at 3 years (DIEA, 2010).
First mating at 15–16 months old is difficult to achieve
under the conditions described previously since heifers
must receive a high and constant plane of nutrition to attain
puberty and become pregnant (Kinder et al., 1987; Short and
Bellows, 1971). Such nutritional regimens also involve care-
ful consideration of the economics of production (Patterson
et al., 1992). An alternative strategy that can be considered
is to delay the first service to the autumn season when heifers
are between 18 and 20 months old. In this system, cows
calve in late summer and they are re-bred in early summer
with the rest of the herd, but without their calves, which
are weaned in early spring at around 6–7 months of age.
This system presents the advantage that first-calving heifers
would achieve high pregnancy rates during their second
service since they are not suckling at the time of mating.
Research has established that the nutrient requirements
for first-calf heifers is greater than for mature cows, due
to continued growth in these females (Spitzer et al., 1995).
However, when cows calve in late summer, calves must
suckle during winter, when native pastures are of low quality
(Bermúdez and Ayala, 2005), thereby reducing milk produc-
tion and weaning weight of calves.
With respect to the effect of calving period on the
length of postpartum anoestrus, the results are contradictory.
Montgomery et al. (1980) found that autumn-calving cows
presented shorter postpartum intervals compared to spring-
calving cows. However, the effects of nutrition and season
are confounded. On the other hand, Thibault et al. (1966)
observed that spring-calving cows had shorter postpartum in-
tervals compared to autumn-calving cows. Moreover, White
et al. (2002) concluded that season alters behavior of oestrus
and cows needed fewer mounts per oestrus in spring com-
pared to winter.
Information of this nature is limited under rangeland
conditions within Uruguay. Our objective was to evaluate dif-
ferent nutritional management regimens during winter in
primiparous, autumn-calving heifers, and to determine its
effect on cow and calf performance. In the first experiment,
cow performance on improved and native pastures was com-
pared. In the second experiment, winter supplementation on
native pastures was incorporated as a third treatment.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Location and general herd management
Experiments were carried out at Palo a Pique Experimen-
tal Unit of the National Institute of Agricultural Research
(INIA), Treinta y Tres, Uruguay (33°S and 56°W) from late
summer to the end spring (March–November). First calving
beef cows had previously been mated at 18–20 months old
and calved during early autumn. All cows grazed together
on native pastures both before and after the application of
treatments (winter). Cows were weaned between August and
September and inseminated thereafter. These experiments
were conducted in accordance with the Experimental Unit
directive concerning the use of animals for experimentation.
2.2. Experiment 1
This experiment involved 41 Aberdeen Angus × Hereford
fall-calving beef heifers that were previously inseminated at
18–20 months of age in winter. The mean age at calving was
28 months of age in early autumn with a BW (mean ± SEM.)
of 390 ± 5.9 kg and BCS of 4.0 ± 0.1 units (1 = thin, 8 = fat,
Vizcarra et al., 1986). They grazed native pastures for 78.4 ±
2.1 days postpartum (pp) when they were assigned to one
of two treatments for 94 days of winter: (i) grazing native
pastures (NP; n = 21); (ii) grazing improved pastures with
Lotus subbiflorus (IP, n = 20). During winter, when the differ-
ent treatments were applied, both groups of cows were of-
fered 7 kg DM/100 kg LW by regulating pasture availability
and the area of the paddock. At the end of the treatment
period (day 172 ± 2.0 pp), all cows were managed together
on native pastures until calf weaning (day 203 ± 2.1 pp).
Approximately one month later, cows were artificially insem-
inated (day 237 ± 2.2) (involved 6 days of oestrus detection
and insemination, then synchronized with a single injection
of 150 μgofD-cloprostenol (Celovet-Prost, VETCOM Lab.,
Montevideo, Uruguay), and the following 6 days the same
procedure was applied). Ten days after AI finished, all cows
were exposed to a single bull of proven fertility for 21 days.
2.3. Experiment 2
Forty one first calving heifers (Aberdeen Angus × Hereford)
were used for this experiment. They calved in early autumn,
were 28 months of age and had a mean LW of 330 ± 3.1 kg
and BCS of 3.8 ± 0.07 units. They were managed together on
NP until day 87 ± 4.0 pp, when they were assigned to one
of three treatments for 90 days of winter: (i) grazing native
pastures (NP, n = 14); (ii) grazing improved pastures with
L. subbiflorus (IP; n = 13); (iii) grazing native pastures and
supplemented with a concentrate (S, n = 14). Supplemented
cows were offered a concentrate (16% CP) daily at 1% of
BW. During winter, when the different treatments were
applied, all groups of cows were offered 7 kg DM/100 kg LW.
When the treatments finished (day 179 ± 3.8 pp), all cows
were managed together on native pastures until weaning
(day 210 ± 3.8 pp). Approximately two months later, cows
were artificially inseminated (day 270 ± 3.8 pp) (as before
involved 6 days of oestrus detection and insemination, then
synchronized with a single injection of 150 μgofD-cloprostenol
(Celovet-Prost, VETCOM Lab., Montevideo, Uruguay), and
the following 6 days the same procedure was applied). Ten
days after AI finished, all cows were exposed to a single bull
of proven fertility for 21 days.
2.4. Pasture measurements, body weight and body condition
Dry matter availability (kg/ha), percentage of protein,
neutral and acid detergent fiber (NDF and ADF, respectively),

organic matter digestibility (OMD), and pasture height were
recorded monthly. BW and BCS were recorded at calving
and every 28 days until weaning. Calf BW was also measured
at birth and every 28 days until weaning. The percentage of
dry matter offered every 100 kg of LW was adjusted every
28 days, taking into account forage availability, the cow BW
and the area of the paddock.
2.5. Presence of corpus luteum and pregnancy rate
Presence of a corpus luteum (CL) was measured by ovar-
ian ultrasonic examination using a 5 MHz transducer
(Aloka SSD 500 Echo camera, Overseas Monitor Corp. Ltd.,
Richmond, BC) at the onset of treatments (day 78 pp), at
weaning (day 203 pp) and at the onset of AI (day 237) in
Experiment 1. Final pregnancy rate was determined by ultra-
sound 40 days after the end of the mating period. In Experi-
ment 2, the presence of a CL was recorded at the onset of
treatments (day 87 pp), end of treatments (day 179 pp) and
at the beginning of AI (day 270). Final pregnancy rate was
determined by ultrasound 40 days after the end of the mating
period.
2.6. Milk production and composition
In Experiment 1, 40% of the cows (n = 8/treatment) were
milked at day 20 and every 30 days until 170 day postpartum
and in Experiment 2 all cows (n = 41) were milked at days
40, 60 and every 30 days thereafter until weaning. Milk
production was assessed using the following procedure. At
approximately 18:00 h cows were separated from their
calves and each cow was injected intramuscularly with 20
international units of oxytocin (Hipofamina® Laboratorio
Dispert S.A. Uruguay) to facilitate milk letdown. Two minutes
after injection, time was recorded and cows were milked
using a portable milking machine. After milk flow had ceased,
the machine was removed. Calves were fitted with strong
nose plates and remained with their dams in the same
paddock. The last procedure was done to minimize stress of
the dam managed under range conditions and also avoid
suckling of the calves. The following day, at approximately
06:00 h, cows were milked again with the same protocol. At
that moment, milk obtained was weighed and a sample was
taken for analysis of composition and quality. Nose plates
were removed from calves after milking and cows and calves
returned to the paddock. The samples of milk were kept fresh
and delivered to the Dairy Laboratory (INIA La Estanzuela)
for determinations of percentage of protein, fat and lactose,
using a Bently 2001(Bently Instruments, Chaska, MN, USA).
Milk production is expressed as kg/animal/day.
2.7. Statistical analyses
Data for animal BW, BCS, milk yield and quality, were an-
alyzed using the PROC MIXED procedure with the REPEATED
option of SAS software (SAS, 2003) and data are presented
as means ± SEM. Means were compared using Tukey's test.
The proportion of cows with the presence of a CL and the
proportion pregnant were analyzed using the PROC GENMOD
procedure with a logit link function and a binomial distribu-
tion (SAS).
3. Results
3.1. Experiment 1
3.1.1. Pasture characteristics, body live weight and body condition
score
From calving to the onset of treatments, cows grazed na-
tive pastures with an average availability of 1260 kg DM/ha,
8.3% CP, 69% of NDF, 39% of ADF, 42% of OMD, and 4.7 cm of
height. During the winter, the forage protein level was higher
in IP than in NP (11.5 vs. 7.6%), but DM availability was sim-
ilar between groups (1300 kg DM/ha) and all animals were
offered 7 kg DM/100 kg LW. Also the percentage of NDF was
similar between NP and IP (69%) but ADF was higher in NP
(51.3%) than in IP (45.1%). The OMD was higher in IP (48%)
than in NP (37.5%). The grass height was 5.4 and 5.7 cm for
NP and IP respectively. After the end of winter treatments,
all animals grazed together on NP and were offered 8 kg
DM/100 kg LW. Grass had 7.8% of CP, 68% of NDF, 47% of
ADF and 45% of OMD.
There was a significant interaction between treatment
and time for BW and BCS (Fig. 1). At calving and at the begin-
ning of winter, cows had similar LW (391 ± 8.7 and 380 ±
8.7 kg at calving and onset of treatments, respectively). At
the end of the winter, cows in the IP group weighed more
(P b 0.05) and had greater (P b 0.05) BCS than cows on NP
(413 ± 8.8 vs. 371 ± 8.6 kg and 4.3 ± 0.1 vs. 3.7 ± 0.1 units,
for IP and NP cows respectively). Similarly, at weaning,
cows in the IP group (425 ± 8.8 kg) weighed more (P b 0.05)
than NP cows (390 ± 8.6 kg) and had greater (P b 0.05) BCS
than NP cows (4.2 ± 0.1 vs. 3.7 ± 0.1 units). This difference
was maintained until AI (day 237 pp) when cows on IP
weighed more (P b 0.05) and had a greater (P b 0.001) BCS
than NP cows (442 ± 8.9 vs. 412 ± 8.16 kg and 4.5 ± 0.1 vs.
3.9 ± 0.1 units, for IP and NP, respectively).
3.1.2. Presence of a corpus luteum and pregnancy rate
At the beginning of treatments there was no difference
in the incidence of ovarian activity between cows of NP and
IP groups (9.5 vs. 0% for NP and IP, respectively). At calf
weaning, more (P b 0.05) cows on IP had CL than NP cows
(80.0 vs. 28.6%); however 35 days later when AI started
no differences were detected (95.0 vs. 85.7% for IP and NP,
respectively). Final pregnancy rate was similar for cows in
both treatments (100%).
3.1.3. Milk production and composition
Milk yield was greater (P b 0.05) in IP than in NP cows (av-
erages for the whole period: 3.8 ± 0.29 vs. 2.4 ± 0.29 kg/day;
Table 1). Also, there was a significant interaction between
treatment and time. Before treatments started cows from
both groups produced similar amounts of milk (3.6 ± 0.40
and 2.9 ± 0.40 kg/day at 20 and 50 days postpartum, respec-
tively). However, during the treatment period and until calf
weaning, cows in the IP group produced more (P b 0.05)
milk than NP cows.
The percentage of milk fat was similar between cows from
both groups (2.5 ± 0.2 vs. 2.4 ± 0.2% for NP and IP, respective-
ly; Table 1) and declined significantly from day 22 to day 143.
Cows in the IP group had greater (P b 0.05) percentage of milk
protein than cows in NP (3.2 ± 0.05 vs. 3.0 ± 0.05%) and there

was an interaction between treatment and time. The percent-
age milk protein was similar between groups until day 110;
after that period there was an increase in this constituent in
IP cows only until the end of the experimental period. There
was a tendency (P = 0.06) for an interaction between per-
centage milk lactose and time, since the values were similar
for the whole period between both groups but at the end
(day 164) cows in the IP had a higher (P b 0.05) percentage
lactose than NP cows (5.0 vs. 4.7%).
3.1.4. Calf performance
There was an interaction in calf BW between treatment and
time (Table 1). At birth, calves from both groups had a similar
weight of 32.3 ± 4.4 kg. Also, at the onset of treatments calves
weighed 68.9 ± 4.4 kg. By the end of the treatment period,
however, calves from IP group weighed more (P b 0.05) than
calves from NP treatment (154.2 ± 4.4 vs. 96.1 ± 4.4 kg for IP
and NP, respectively). Similarly, at weaning, this difference
was maintained (182.6 ± 4.4 vs. 117.3 ± 4.4 kg for IP and NP
calves, respectively). The average BW for the whole period is
presented in Table 1.
Average daily BW gain (ADG) was 0.470 kg/day between
birth and day 78 postpartum for all calves. During the
winter period, when the nutritional treatments were applied,
calves on IP had greater (P b 0.05) ADG than calves on NP
(0.881 ± 0.03 vs. 0.316 ± 0.03 kg/animal/day). From the end
of this period to weaning, calves on IP still had a greater
(P b 0.05) ADG compared to NP calves (0.946 ± 0.03 vs.
0.706 ± 0.03 kg/animal/day).
3.2. Experiment 2
3.2.1. Pasture characteristics, body live weight and body condition
score
From calving to the onset of treatments, cows were of-
fered 12 kg DM/100 kg LW of native pastures with an average
availability of 1400 kg DM/ha, 11.1% CP, 67% of NDF, 39%
of ADF, 58% of OMD and 4.2 cm of height. During winter
all animals were offered 9 kg DM/100 kg LW and the forage
protein level was similar between IP and NP (9.0 vs. 8.0%).
Also the percentage of NDF, ADF and OMD were similar
between treatments (65, 35 and 63% for NDF, ADF and
OMD, respectively). Grass height during the winter was 3.2
and 4.2 cm for NP and IP respectively. At the end of the
winter treatments, all animals grazed together on native
pastures and were offered 7 kg DM/100 kg LW, and pastures
had 9% of CP, 61% of NDF, 34% of ADF and 64% of ODM.
There was a significant treatment by time interaction
for BW and BCS (Fig. 2). At calving and at the beginning of
winter, cows had similar BW (330 ± 3.1 and 373 ± 3.1 kg at
calving and at the onset of treatments, respectively) and a
similar BCS (3.8 ± 0.07 units at calving and at the onset of
treatments, respectively). From the end of the winter until
the AI, cows in S group weighed more (P b 0.05) and had
greater (P b 0.05) BCS than cows in NP and IP.
3.2.2. Presence of corpus luteum and pregnancy rate
At the beginning of treatments no cows were cycling. By the
end of winter treatments there were no differences in ovarian
cyclicity among cows from different groups (7, 0 and 7% for
NP, IP and S, respectively). By the beginning of AI there was a
tendency (P= 0.08) for a higher percentage of cows with CL
in NP compared to IP groups (86 vs. 54%), but not with respect
to S cows (71%). However, final pregnancy rate was similar
for cows on all treatments (93, 92 and 100% for NP, IP and S,
respectively).

3.2.3. Milk production and composition
Milk yield was greater (P b 0.001) in S than in NP cows (av-
erage for the entire period: 3.8 ± 0.30 vs. 2.8 ± 0.30 kg/day;
Table 2), but not with respect to IP cows (3.4 ± 0.30 kg/day).
However, there was a significant interaction (P b 0.0001) be-
tween treatment and time. Prior to the commencement of
treatments all cows produced similar amounts of milk (3.8,
3.6 and 3.2 ± 0.30 kg/day at 40, 60 and 90 days postpartum,
respectively). During winter, however, differences in milk
production among cows from different treatments were
evident. In fact, by the end of winter S cows produced more
(Pb 0.0001) milk than IP cows (4.4 ± 0.3 vs. 2.8 ± 0.3 kg/day)
and IP cows produced more (P b 0.05) milk than NP cows
(2.0 ± 0.3 kg/day). However, when all cows were managed
together on native pastures, milk production (day 210) was
similar among cows from the three treatments (2.4, 2.6 and
2.6 kg/day for NP, IP and S cows, respectively).
The percentage of milk fat was similar among cows
from different groups (Table 2) but declined until day 150
and from there increased until the end of the experimental
period. Milk protein percentage was also similar among
cows from different treatments and there was a significant
effect of time, since protein increased until day 150 and
then decreased on day 180. The percentage of milk lactose
did not differ among cows from the three treatment groups
and it declined significantly during the postpartum period.
3.2.4. Calf performance
There was an interaction in calf BW between treatment
and time. At birth, calves from all groups had similar weights
(30.2 ± 1.2 kg). Also, at the onset of treatments, calves had a
similar weight (85.9 ± 4.0 kg). However, by the end of the dif-
ferential winter management, calves from S group weighed
more (P b 0.05) than calves from NP treatment (127.5 ± 4.0
vs. 113.0 ± 4.0 kg), and calves from IP (123.1 ± 4.1 kg) tended
(P = 0.08) to be heavier than claves from NP. At weaning,
calves from S were heavier than calves from the IP group
and these calves were heavier than those from the NP group
(141.5 ± 4.0, 134.1 ± 4.1 and 122.4 ± 4.0 kg for S, IP and NP,
respectively; P b 0.05). The average BW for the whole period
is presented in Table 2.
4. Discussion
The reproductive performance in spring of first-calving
heifers managed during the previous winter on NP was high
and similar than those managed with better nutrition regi-
mens during the same period. Although BCS and LW were
lower during winter in first-calving heifers grazing NP com-
pared to those on IP or S, when they were weaned and bred
thereafter, they achieved high pregnancy rates. However, in
both experiments, calf weaning weight was significantly
lower in those cows that were managed on NP during winter
compared to the other groups, despite the differences ob-
served between years.
Weather was notably different from year to year over
the experimental period; thus, this could have affected the
production and quality of pastures, with experiments being
done in identical paddocks each year. The maximum differ-
ence in pasture production and quality was observed in
improved pastures, since native pastures had similar and
more stable parameters in both years. In fact, the level of pro-
tein was higher and its accessibility greater in the first than
in the second year since pasture height average was higher.

The length of the postpartum anoestrous interval in pri-
miparous cows is longer than in multiparous cows (Tervit
et al., 1977). Under similar conditions to the present experi-
ment, postpartum anoestrous intervals from 90 to 110 days
for multiparous cows (Quintans et al., 2010) and more than
120 days for primiparous cows (Quintans and Vázquez,
2002) have been reported. However, in those specific exper-
iments, all cows calved in the spring, while in the present
experiments heifers calved in the autumn. There is evidence
to suggest that seasonal reproductive mechanisms are pre-
sent in cattle even though this species ovulates and breeds
throughout the year. Seasonal and nutritional effects might
be confounded in some studies. For example, Montgomery
et al. (1980) observed that cows calving in autumn had
shorter postpartum intervals compared to those calving in
spring. Indeed, the pattern of body weight between herds
was different. Autumn-calving heifers had an extra 85 kg at
the onset of the calving season compared to those calving in
the spring. The authors suggested that the shorter postpar-
tum intervals observed in the autumn-calving cows may be
related to differences in nutrition and photoperiod. Harrison
et al. (1982) reported a significant reduction of LH activity
in beef cows during winter months compared to spring. It
is interesting to observe that the length of the anoestrous
period in heifers under the conditions of the present
study was longer than those reported in other experiments
with young females of similar characteristics (62 to 85 days,
Montgomery et al., 1985). Although this period was not
strictly evaluated, the percentage of cows with CL at different
intervals during the experimental period was measured. In
Experiment 1, by day 203 postpartum, almost 50% remained
anoestrous whereas in Experiment 2, by 179 days postpar-
tum, 95% remained anoestrous. Heifer age, photoperiod
and nutrition may account for these differences compared
to other experiments.
In Experiment 1 cows began to lose body weight at 60 days
postpartum while cows in Experiment 2 did so around 90 days
postpartum. The effect of nutrition on the re-initiation of
ovarian activity is well documented (Randel, 1990;
Wetteman et al., 1982; Short et al., 1990) and it can be specu-
lated that the main reason of the low postpartum ovarian cy-
clicity in these young females was an interaction between
nutrition and season. However, in the system evaluated, the
rapid re-initiation of ovarian cyclicity after calving loses impor-
tance as heifers were served again in the following spring, one
or two months after weaning.
Once the calves were weaned cows started cycling within
a short period of time. The effect of weaning on postpartum
ovulation is well documented (Parfet et al., 1986; Bishop
et al., 1994). Although most studies focused on evaluating
the effect of early or temporary weaning on the re-initiation
of ovulation after calving, even in these situations when
cows are between 50 and 80 days postpartum, the effect of
weaning in advancing ovulation is consistent (Oxenreider,
1968; Lusby et al., 1981; Parfet et al., 1986; Williams, 1990;
Bishop et al., 1994; Quintans et al., 2009; de Castro et al.,
2011). In the present experiments cows were at an advanced
stage of the postpartum period when calves were weaned,
so the response in ovulation would be expected to be more
rapid. At weaning only 28.6% of cows that grazed on native
pastures were cycling (Experiment 1), but one month later
86% of cows were cycling and all became pregnant. In Exper-
iment 2 only 7% of cows that grazed on native pastures
were cycling at the end of winter (day 179) but 93% became
pregnant thereafter. In the conditions of the present study,
weaning time coincided with the spring growth of native
pastures, and consequently with the increase in the availabil-
ity and quality of the cows' diet.
The winter management of cows on improved pastures in-
creased BW and BCS during that period and the difference
with cows on native pastures was maintained until the end
of the Experiment (1). In Experiment 2, only supplemented
cows had greater LW and BCS compared to the other two nu-
tritional treatments. However, in both years, the management
of cows on improved pastures and concentrate supplementa-
tion both led to a significant increase in milk production. In
Experiment 1 cows on IP produced almost twice as much
milk as cows on NP and with a higher percentage of protein.
Similar results were observed in Experiment 2 where supple-
mented cows had the highest milk production followed by
cows on IP. The increase in milk production when energy
intake improved is well documented (Jenkins and Ferrel,
1992; Lalman et al., 2000; Perry et al., 1991). In the present
study the increase of milk production when nutritional level
improved indicates that significantly more nutrients were
used for milk production, compared to other physiological
functions like growing or fat deposition. Sinclair et al.
(1998) reported that those breeds with high milk potential
had reduced quantities of subcutaneous fat and produced
higher yields of milk when fed high energy diets compared
to low milk potential cows. The increase in milk production
and protein percentage in cows on IP in Experiment 1 was
highly expressed in live body weight gain and weaning weight
of their calves. In Experiment 2 these differences were less
important but still significant and supplemented cows weaned
the heaviest calves. Similarly Perry et al. (1991) and Spitzer
et al. (1995) found that feed intake from parturition affects
milk production and consequently weaning weight of calves.
In conclusion, autumn calving beef heifers which were sub-
sequently managed during winter on native pastures did not
encounter reproductive problems during the following spring;
however calf performance was negatively affected. When they
were managed on improved pastures or when they were sup-
plemented with concentrates, body weight of calves increased
but no change in reproductive performance was observed.
These results indicate that under range conditions this system
in another alternative to achieve high pregnancy rate in first
calving heifers and, depending on economical scenario, either
improved pastures or supplementation can be used to increase
calf weaning weight.
