High perennial ryegrass seeding rates reduce plant
size and survival during the ﬁrst year after sowing:
does this have implications for pasture sward
persistence?
J. M. Lee*, E. R. Thom*, K. Wynn†,1, D. Waugh*, L. Rossi‡ and D. F. Chapman‡
*DairyNZ, Hamilton, New Zealand, †DairyNZ, Whangarei, New Zealand, ‡DairyNZ, Lincoln University,
Lincoln, New Zealand
Abstract
Failure of perennial ryegrass swards to persist is a key
issue on dairy farms in many areas of the world. This
study describes an experiment conducted to test the
hypothesis that high ryegrass seeding rates
(>18 kg seed ha/C01) reduce plant size and physical sur-
vival during the ﬁrst year after sowing, with negative
implications for population persistence. Four cultivars
representing four functional types of perennial rye-
grass were sown at ﬁve seeding rates (equivalent to 6,
12, 18, 24 and 30 kg seed ha/C01) with white clover in
three dairying regions of New Zealand. Plant establish-
ment rates, size and survival were measured for the
ﬁrst 13 months after sowing. Herbage accumulation,
botanical composition and perennial ryegrass tiller
density were also monitored. Increasing seeding rate
reduced plant survival in the seven weeks after sow-
ing, reﬂecting likely differences in germination and
very early mortality of small seedlings. Thereafter,
plant survival was relatively high and consistent across
seeding rates at two sites but was consistently greater
in the 6 kg ha/C01 treatment compared with the 24 and
30 kg ha/C01 treatments at one site. Higher seeding rates
also increased ryegrass tiller density and the contribu-
tion of ryegrass towards total herbage biomass but
reduced the contribution of white clover. Very few
cultivar 9 seeding rate interactions were detected for
any of the measured variables. Many of the seeding
rate effects dissipated by the end of the ﬁrst year after
sowing, indicating that high seeding rates did not
predispose swards to poor persistence in the longer
term, irrespective of ryegrass functional type.
Keywords: pasture sward persistence, sowing rate,
sward establishment
Introduction
Persistence of perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne L.)
swards is a key issue on dairy farms in many areas of
the world, particularly where low summer rainfall and
insect pest attack are more prevalent (Mercer, 2011;
Tozer et al., 2014). Although ‘persistence’ is a relative
term that invokes expectations of how a sown sward
should perform over time (Tozer et al., 2014), the
overall outcome desired by farmers is that the dry-
matter (DM) yield advantage of the new sward com-
pared with the previous sward persists for as many
years as possible. Thus, ‘persistence’ is deﬁned here as
the persistence of yield. The failure of a new sward to
maintain its yield advantage could be due to a loss of
sown ryegrass plants or a loss of the overall yielding
ability of the sown plants because yield-related traits
are not expressed, or are lost altogether (Parsons et al.,
2011). Consequently, to fully understand persistence,
information is required at the levels of pasture sward
community (the presence and abundance of different
species), population (the density of plants/tillers of the
sown species) and individual plants (the phenotype
and genotype). Few long-term studies have followed
trends in herbage accumulation in different environ-
ments after sowing, let alone collected detailed infor-
mation on plant and sward structure at the same time.
Thus, it is not known whether or not variation in
plant structure/morphology in the early life of the
sward is important in inﬂuencing long-term productiv-
ity. Systematic studies that include these detailed mea-
surements are, therefore, crucial for helping to

identify the mechanisms associated with a lack of per-

Recommended seeding rates for renewal of pere-
nnial ryegrass swards in New Zealand are currently
20–25 kg seed ha/C01 for diploid cultivars and 25–30
kg ha/C01 for tetraploids. In Ireland, the recommended
rates are even higher (35 kg ha/C01; TEAGASC, 2014). A
review of the literature covering the previous 60 years
indicates that ryegrass seeding rates <18 kg ha/C01 were
often sufﬁcient to establish swards as productive as
those sown at higher seeding rates (e.g. Sears and
Brougham, 1963; Frame and Boyd, 1986; Praat et al.,
1996). In some cases, swards sown at lower seeding
rates yielded less initially, but this effect dissipated
resulting in similar annual herbage accumulation in the
ﬁrst year after sowing (Brougham, 1952; Cullen, 1958;
Praat et al., 1996), or by year two (Culleton et al.,
1986). This equilibrium was largely the result of greater
tillering in plants sown at lower seeding rates compen-
sating for the lower plant numbers. Plant mortality was
also reduced at lower seeding rates (Brougham, 1952;
Heddle and Herriott, 1954), and the beneﬁcial effect of
plant size on survival has been tracked over 18 months
(Heddle and Herriott, 1954).
One possible implication of these trends is that the
denser population of smaller plants generated from
high seeding rates could predispose the population to
high rates of plant mortality if environmental stresses
are encountered before plants have reached a certain
size and built a sufﬁciently robust root system (Hoen,
1968). This could occur in, for example, temperate
regions where new pastures are sown in autumn and
then encounter drought conditions the following spring
or summer. The resulting depleted population of sown
plants may be less able to resist invasion by volunteer
weed species leading to loss of yield potential and fail-
ure of the new sward to meet farmer expectations for
yield and persistence in the long term. This is the gap in
the aforementioned studies; while there are many
detailed measurements conducted within the ﬁrst year
of sowing, none of the studies go beyond three years,
which is arguably too short a time period to make con-
clusions regarding sward persistence.
The hypothesis of the study reported here was
that high perennial ryegrass seeding rates (>18 kg
seed ha/C01) reduce plant size and physical plant sur-
vival during the ﬁrst year after sowing, which might
have negative implications for long-term population
persistence. To test the hypothesis, the morphological
development and survival of plants of four perennial
ryegrass cultivars sown at ﬁve different seeding rates
were monitored intensively for 13 months after sow-
ing within the same experimental design established
in three different environments. Monitoring of this
experiment is continuing beyond the period reported
here to collect the data required to conﬁrm whether
or not the hypothesis holds for long-term persistence
of pasture sward yield. This information will be
reported in subsequent publications.
Materials and methods
Experimental sites
The experiment was conducted in three dairying
regions in New Zealand: Northland and Waikato in
the North Island (5 and 23% of total New Zealand
milk solids (fat + protein; MS) in 2013/2014, respec-
tively) and Canterbury in the South Island (19% of
total milk solids) (DairyNZ, 2014). The experimental
sites were located at Fonterra’s Jordan Valley Farm in
Northland (35/C1612 S, 174/C1262 E; 96 m a.s.l.),
DairyNZ’s Scott Farm in the Waikato (37/C1772 S,
175/C1378 E; 40 m a.s.l.) and the Lincoln University
Research Dairy Farm in Canterbury (43/C1638 S,
172/C1462 E; 10 m a.s.l.). The soil types at the three
sites, respectively, were Wairua clay, Matangi silt loam
and Wakanui silt loam over a mottled sandy loam
phase. These are classiﬁed as an Orthic Gley, a Typic
Sandy Gley and a Mottled Immature Pallic soil respec-
tively (Hewitt, 1998). Both the Northland and Wai-
kato sites were dryland, while the Canterbury site was
irrigated (standard for dairy farms in each region).
Experimental design and treatments
At each of the three sites described above, four peren-
nial ryegrass cultivars, each representing different
functional types within the species, were sown in plots
at ﬁve nominal seeding rate treatments (6, 12, 18, 24
and 30 kg seed ha/C01). Main treatment plots (cultivar,
540 m2) were divided into subplots (seeding rate,
108 m2), arranged in a randomized split-plot design
with ﬁve replicates.
The perennial ryegrass cultivars were ‘Grasslands
Nui’, ‘Commando’, ‘Alto’ and ‘Halo’. In terms of their
functional types, Nui is a diploid cultivar with a mid-
season ﬂowering date of approximately 22 October
(Lee et al., 2012). Nui was selected from plants col-
lected from 40-year-old sward at a dairy farm in south
Auckland in the 1960s and released commercially in
1976 (Armstrong, 1977). The other three cultivars
were released commercially after 2000. Commando is
a diploid with a similar ﬂowering date to Nui that was
bred from New Zealand ecotypes. Alto is a diploid that
ﬂowers 14 d later than Nui and is based on late-head-
ing material from Nui and material from north-west
Spain. Halo is a tetraploid classiﬁed as very late season
ﬂowering (25 d later than Nui; Lee et al., 2012). The
four cultivars of differing functional types were

All cultivars were infected with the endophytic

tems are described by Thom et al. (2012). Pre-sowing
‘grow-out’ tests for endophyte viability conﬁrmed
endophyte infection frequencies of 87, 74, 84 and
78% of seed for the Nui, Commando, Alto and Halo
seed lines respectively.
Seed germination percentages and 1000 seed
weights measured for each cultivar were used to
ensure a similar number of viable seeds were sown
per hectare for all cultivars. This resulted in the
6kgha/C01 treatments being sown at 6/C10, 7/C13, 8/C15 and
6/C17 kg seed ha/C01 for Alto, Commando, Halo and Nui,
respectively, with the remaining seeding rate treat-
ments increasing proportionately.
Site preparation and establishment
At all sites, the existing sward was sprayed in March
2011 with glyphosate herbicide (2/C17, 3/C12 and
2/C12 kg a.i. ha/C01 in Northland, Waikato and Canter-
bury, respectively) plus Pulse/C226 organosilicone pene-
trant Nufarm, Auckland, New Zealand) at 100 mL/
100 L water. In Northland and Canterbury, the exist-
ing sward also received broadleaf-weed herbicide (30
and 16/C15gha/C01 of tribenuron-methyl respectively). In
Northland and Waikato, a second application of gly-
phosate herbicide (2/C17 and 2/C12 kg a.i. ha/C01, respec-
tively) and Pulse/C226 was applied three to four weeks
later, with perennial ryegrass seed direct-drilled in late
March/early April using a Renovator MK3 drill (Dun-
can Ag, Timaru, New Zealand) or a John Deere 750A
drill (John Deere International GmbH, Schaffhausen,
Switzerland) respectively. In Canterbury, the site was
surface rotary hoed and harrowed, before seedbed
preparation by ploughing, harrowing and rolling. Seed
was drilled using a cone seeder in late March. All sites
received slug bait (0/C14 kg metaldehyde ha/C01) in the
week before sowing. Ryegrass seed was Ultrastrike/C226-
treated (PGG Wrightson Seeds Ltd., Christchurch, New
Zealand) providing a fungicide to prevent damping-off
disease and systemic insecticides to deter insect attack
on seedlings before endophyte alkaloid production
commenced. All plots were over-sown with 8 kg ha/C01
of Superstrike/C226-coated (PGG Wrightson Seeds Ltd.,
Christchurch, New Zealand) Tribute white clover seed
(Trifolium repens L.; equivalent to about 5 kg ha/C01 of
bare seed). Post-emergence weed control within eight
weeks of sowing was achieved at all sites by the applica-
tion of Preside/C226 herbicide (40–52 g ﬂumetsulam ha/C01)
plus Uptake
TM
surfactant (500 mL/100 L of water).
Grazing management
Plots were rotationally stocked by dairy cows at all
sites, with all plots at a site grazed at the same time.
The timing of the ﬁrst grazing after sowing was deter-
mined by the ‘pluck’ test, which involves grasping
ryegrass seedlings ﬁrmly between the thumb and fore-
ﬁnger and tugging in a single quick movement to
mimic the biting action of a cow. If the seedling is
uprooted from the ground, the sward is not considered
to be ready for grazing. If the leaves break off and the
roots remain in the ground, the sward is considered to
be ready for grazing. The ﬁrst grazings took place 96
(20 July 2011), 57 (25 May 2011) and 158 d (4
September 2011) after sowing at the Northland, Wai-
kato and Canterbury sites respectively. Subsequently,
grazing took place when herbage biomass reached
2500–3500 kg DM ha/C01, with a target post-grazing
residual of 1600 kg DM ha/C01 (approximately 4 to
5 cm residual stubble height). This resulted in 10, 11
and 12 grazings at the Northland, Waikato and Can-
terbury sites, respectively, with an average (/C6standard
deviation) grazing interval of 30 /C6 11, 33 /C6 8 and
24 /C6 5 d. At each grazing, a discrete group of cows
were allocated to each main plot; the number of cows
in each group was determined by herbage availability
above the desired residual and expected cow intake.
During the peak of seedhead production (late
spring/early summer) when clumps of herbage were
left ungrazed around dung pats and urine-affected
areas, plots were mown to a stubble height of 7 cm
after grazing. This occurred once at the Canterbury
site (January 2012), twice at the Northland site
(November 2011 and January 2012) and four times at
the Waikato site (October 2011, November, December
and January 2012).
Fertilizer management
Before sowing, soil cores (25 mm diameter) were
taken across each replicate at each site to 150 mm
depth and analysed for pH, phosphorus (P), potassium
(K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na) and
sulphur (S). This revealed suboptimal K and S concen-
trations for herbage growth at the Waikato site. To
rectify deﬁciencies, 8, 50, 9, 3 and 19 kg ha/C01 of P, K,
S, Mg and Ca, respectively, was applied on 23 March
2011. Additional K was also applied in September
2011 (50 kg K ha/C01). Following annual soil nutrient

to the Waikato site in March 2012 (40, 49, 25, 97 and
20 kg ha/C01 of P, S, Mg, Ca and Na, respectively)

Weather conditions and irrigation
Weather records were obtained from Fonterra’s Kauri
processing plant 4 km from the Northland site (rainfall
only), the Ruakura climatological station 5 km from
the Waikato site and the Lincoln climatological station
2 km from the Canterbury site (Figure 1). No temper-
ature gauge exists near the Northland site; therefore,
National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research
Ltd. (NIWA) virtual climate station temperature data
for the nearest station to the site were used. These
estimates are produced on a daily basis, based on the
spatial interpolation of actual data observations made
at climate stations located around New Zealand.
At the Canterbury site only, water was applied on
twenty occasions using a travelling irrigator. A total of
92 mm was applied over three applications in March
2011 before sowing. A further 232 mm was then
applied on 17 irrigation days from 29 November 2011
to 6 April 2012, with an average application rate of
14 mm d/C01 at approximately 5-d intervals during
summer (Figure 1).
Plant size and survival
Ten perennial ryegrass seedlings were selected at
intervals on a single transect across each subplot seven
weeks after sowing (May 2011, before the ﬁrst graz-
ing). In total, 1000 seedlings were tagged at each site.
A string line stretching from one corner of each sub-
plot to the diagonally opposite corner, with coloured
tape attached at deﬁned intervals, was used to deter-
mine the position of the nearest plant that was to be
tagged. The last tape position at either end of the tran-
sect was located at least 1/C15 m from the plot edge, and
no positions were chosen in the central region of the
plots where the two drill passes forming each plot
occasionally overlapped. The selected plants were
tagged at ground level using plastic-coated wire with
the ends twisted together and inserted into the ground
to hold the ring in place. Later, steel wire loops were
inserted over the wire and a hand-held metal detector
was used to help locate the position of tagged plants.
The number of tillers on each tagged plant was
counted in May 2011 at tagging, and during spring,
summer and autumn (September 2011, December
2011, and May 2012, respectively) to assess changes
in plant size. The status of the tagged plants (alive,
dead, buried under dung, ‘pulled’, severely grazed,
subjected to treading damage, scorched by urine) was
checked on ﬁve occasions in Northland (July, Septem-
ber, October, December 2011 and May 2012), six
occasions in Waikato (June (29), August, October,
December 2011 and May 2012) and three occasions in
Canterbury (September, December 2011 and May
2012). Tagged plants that were not able to be located
were classiﬁed as ‘missing’; if a plant was still missing
in May 2012, it was excluded from analyses. This was
the case for 201, 64 and 33 of the 1000 tagged plants
at the Northland, Waikato and Canterbury sites
respectively.
The density of perennial ryegrass plants per plot
was estimated using perennial ryegrass tiller density
data (description of the method for tiller density
below) and the average number of tillers per tagged
plant seven weeks after sowing (May 2011). This
enabled calculation of the apparent percentage of the
sown viable seed that resulted in a seedling plant.
Apparent survival of these seedling plants into spring,
summer and autumn (September 2011, December
2011 and May 2012, respectively) was then calculated
using survival rate data from the tagged plants.
In May 2012 after the ﬁnal tiller count, the surviv-
ing tagged plants in each subplot in three of the ﬁve
replicates were cut at ground level using a scalpel.
This herbage was oven-dried at 95°C to constant
weight (approximately 48 h) to provide an estimate of
plant size on a dry weight basis.
Plant morphology and orientation
At the Waikato site, measurements were taken one
month after sowing (autumn; May 2011) to character-
ize the effect of ryegrass seeding rate on early plant
development. Measurements were restricted to the
diploid cultivar Alto and the tetraploid cultivar Halo.
Ten ryegrass tillers per seeding rate treatment were
randomly selected, and the following measurements
were made on intact tillers: undisturbed tiller height
(the maximum vertical height above ground of the tip
of the tiller leaf), undisturbed pseudostem height (the
maximum vertical height above ground of the ligule
of the oldest expanded leaf) and total tiller length
(length of the tiller from its attachment point at
ground level to the tip of the longest leaf). Tiller angle
was then calculated as the angle between the lower
part of the tiller and ground level (Thomas, 1980).

Plant development
At the Canterbury site during late winter and late
spring 2011 (August and November 2011, respec-
tively), ten soil cores (5 cm diameter 9 12 cm depth)
were removed per subplot in three of the ﬁve repli-
cates with each core containing the root system of a
randomly selected, single ryegrass plant. The shoot
material of each plant was cut to ground level and
retained before the core was removed. In the labora-
tory, the soil was washed from the roots and the num-
ber of tillers and lateral roots per plant counted, and
the maximum root length measured. The shoots and
roots from each plant were then re-paired and oven-
dried at 95°C for 48 h to determine the dry weight of
above- and below-ground material.

Herbage accumulation
Herbage accumulation was estimated by quadrat cuts
on the day before grazing at each site. This com-
menced from the second grazing at the Northland and
Waikato sites (September and June 2011, respec-
tively), and from the third grazing at the Canterbury
site (October 2011). Herbage samples (n = 2) were cut
to the approximate post-grazing height (4 cm) within
square quadrats (0/C12m2) placed randomly within each
subplot in three of the ﬁve replicates. Samples were
washed and oven-dried at 95°C to constant weight
(approximately 48 h) to allow calculation of herbage
biomass and herbage accumulation rate (HAR) per
hectare. Seasonal herbage accumulation for each sub-
plot was calculated from HAR data for individual har-
vests partitioned within the following periods: winter,
1 June to 31 August; spring, 1 September to 30
November; summer, 1 December to 28/29 February;
and autumn, 1 March to 31 May. Total annual her-
bage accumulation was also calculated for the period 1
June to 31 May.
Botanical composition
At all sites, representative samples of herbage from
each subplot were collected the day before grazing in
late winter/early spring, late spring, summer and
autumn (August/September 2011, November, January
2012 and April respectively). Hand shears were used
to cut six 500 9 10 mm strips per subplot to the
approximate post-grazing height (4 cm). All ﬁve repli-
cates were sampled in Northland and Canterbury,
while only three replicates were sampled at the Wai-
kato site. In the laboratory, samples for each subplot
were homogenized and a subsample was dissected into
the following categories: perennial ryegrass leaf,
perennial ryegrass reproductive stem (including seed-
head and ﬂowers), white clover, unsown species and
dead material. Dissected subsamples were oven-dried
at 95°C to constant weight (approximately 48 h) and
weighed to determine botanical composition as a pro-
portion of herbage biomass above 4 cm on a DM basis.
At the Northland site in late winter, volunteer ryegrass
plants were identiﬁed that appeared visually different
from sown perennial ryegrass plants. Tillers from a
number of these plants were genotyped using SSR
markers (Moon et al., 1999) and a large proportion
identiﬁed as being infected with Neotyphodium occul-
tans, the endophyte that frequently infects annual rye-
grass species, indicating the volunteers were annual
ryegrass types. Therefore, samples from Northland also
included the category, annual ryegrass, as identiﬁed
by the following criteria: leaves ≥6 mm wide, large
clasping auricles and a rolled emerging leaf. Seedheads
with awns were also included in this category.
Perennial ryegrass tiller density
Perennial ryegrass tiller population densities were cal-
culated from the total number of ryegrass tillers
counted in ﬁve randomly placed tiller frames
(5 9 20 cm) per subplot in autumn 2011, spring,
summer and autumn 2012 (May 2011, September,
December and April 2012 respectively).
Statistical analysis
Botanical composition, endophyte presence and peren-
nial ryegrass tiller density data were analysed using
REML in GenStat 14.1 (VSN International Ltd., 2011)
with cultivar (main plot), seeding rate (subplot), site
and their interactions as ﬁxed effects, and block, main
plot within block and subplot within main plot as ran-
dom effects. For botanical composition data, both
untransformed and angular transformed data were
analysed; similar conclusions were drawn from both
analyses, and therefore, untransformed data are pre-
sented here. Data collected at only one site were anal-
ysed using REML in GenStat 14.1 with cultivar (main
plot), seeding rate (subplot) and their interactions as
ﬁxed effects, and block, main plot within block and
subplot within main plot as random effects. Herbage
accumulation data were also analysed using this
method for each site individually due to the differing
length of measurement. Fisher’s protected least signiﬁ-
cant difference test was used to identify signiﬁcant dif-
ferences between treatment means.

Rainfall and temperatures
At the Northland site, rainfall during autumn 2011
was 60% greater than the 25-year mean (614 vs.
385 mm respectively; Figure 1). Annual rainfall from
June 2011 to May 2012 was also greater than the 25-
year mean (1833 vs. 1506 mm, respectively), due to
greater summer and autumn rainfall (+161 and
262 mm respectively). Average monthly maximum
and minimum temperatures were generally within
1°C of the 25-year mean. At the Waikato site, rainfall
during autumn 2011 was greater than the 30-year
mean (395 vs. 269 mm respectively). Annual rainfall
from June 2011 to May 2012 was similar to the 30-
year mean (1193 vs. 1135 mm, respectively), although
the distribution was altered (less during spring/more

during summer). Temperatures during the experiment
were generally cooler (by /C01°C on average) than the
30-year mean, with the greatest difference occurring
during summer and autumn 2012 (maximum temper-

site, total rainfall over the experiment was similar to
the 30-year mean. The distribution was different how-

seeding rate-by-cultivar-by-site interactions are shown

Plant morphology and development
Plant morphology
At the Waikato site one month after sowing, ryegrass
tillers in the 6 kg ha/C01 treatment were more prostrate
(i.e. smaller tiller angle) than in the 18–30 kg ha/C01
treatments (Table 2). There was no signiﬁcant differ-
ence between Alto and Halo plants, nor any signiﬁ-
cant interactions between seeding rate and cultivar for
the four measured variables.
Plant development
There were no signiﬁcant seeding rate 9 cultivar
interactions evident in the plant development data
from the Canterbury site (P > 0/C105). In late winter
(10 weeks after sowing), ryegrass plants in the 6 and
12 kg ha/C01 treatments were larger than plants in the
18–30 kg ha/C01 treatments, in terms of both the num-
ber of tillers and lateral roots per plant, and above-
and below-ground biomass (data not shown). These
effects were still evident in late spring (eight months
after sowing; Table 3). Differences in plant biomass
were entirely due to differences in the number of til-
lers per plant: there was no effect of seeding rate on
the biomass of individual tillers (Table 3). Mean tiller
numbers per plant at this time were very similar to
the tiller numbers recorded on the tagged plants at the
same stage (as described below).
In late winter, Commando plants had more lateral
roots than the other three cultivars (P < 0/C105; 32 vs.
26 lateral roots per plant; standard error of the differ-
ence (s.e.d.) = 1/C17), while the maximum root length
of Alto and Nui plants was greater than Commando
and Halo (P < 0/C1001; 90 vs. 81 mm; s.e.d. = 3). By late
spring, the number of lateral roots, maximum root
length and the above- and below-ground plant bio-
mass were similar across all cultivars.
Tiller accumulation per plant
There were no signiﬁcant seeding rate 9 cultivar
interactions evident in the tiller accumulation data
(P > 0/C105). As ryegrass seeding rate increased, the
number of tillers on tagged ryegrass plants decreased.
This trend was ﬁrst identiﬁed in autumn 2011 at the
Waikato site (P < 0/C1001) and spring 2011 at the
Northland and Canterbury sites (P < 0/C1001), and
remained evident through the entire establishment
year at all sites (P < 0/C101; Figure 2).
Across the three sites, Commando plants initially
had 20% more tillers per tagged plant than Halo and
Nui plants (P < 0/C1001; data not shown). These cultivar
differences were still evident in spring 2011
(P < 0/C1001) and summer of 2011–2012 (P < 0/C105), but
had dissipated by autumn 2012 (P > 0/C105) when
tagged plants had an average of 18 tillers per plant.
Plant size at 13 months after sowing
There were no signiﬁcant seeding rate 9 cultivar
interactions evident in the plant size data (P > 0/C105).
The above-ground dry weight of the surviving tagged
plants in autumn 2012 was greater in the 6 kg ha/C01
treatment compared with 18–30 kg ha/C01 treatments
(P < 0/C101; 1/C11, 1/C10, 0/C18, 0/C18 and 0/C17 g DM per plant for
the 6, 12, 18 24 and 30 kg ha/C01 treatments, respec-
tively; s.e.d. = 0/C112). The dry weight of the surviving
tagged plants in autumn 2012 was similar across culti-
vars (P>0/C105).

viable seeds sown that resulted in a seedling plant (in
May 2011) or an established plant (after May 2011).
Data for percent survival in May 2011 reﬂect differ-

bly, very early mortality of small seedlings. Data after
May 2011 reﬂect differences in physical survival of
plants as they developed beyond the seedling stage
and as the sward density increased. These two phases
are described separately for each site below. No

reached this threshold did so as a result of higher tiller

the 13-month survivors.

It is not surprising that plants sown at lower seeding
rates are larger because interplant competition for
resources such as light, nutrients and water is reduced
(Donald, 1963) and plants are able to produce more
tillers and therefore occupy more space, compensating,
at least in part, for the lower plant density. The cur-
rent study also demonstrates that tillers on plants
sown at lower seeding rates are more prostrate, at
least in the early stages of development (Table 2), a
possible response to increase light interception on a
per plant basis and/or residual leaf area remaining to
improve rapid recovery following grazing (Rhodes,
1971).
The data summarized in Table 3 show clearly that
the plant size effect is driven entirely by tiller number;
no effect of seeding rate on tiller biomass was
observed at either sampling, indicating a high level of
intraplant regulation of resource allocation. At low ini-
tial plant density (low seeding rate), resources are
preferentially used to produce more tillers to increase
resource capture, whereas at high plant density (high
seeding rate), tillering is constrained. The net result
for the 12–30 kg ha/C01 treatments at the site where
this sampling was conducted (Canterbury) was a
more-or-less similar total density (number per m2)of
similar-size tillers distributed along a continuum of
plant density from around 500 to 750 plants m/C02.
Plants in the 6 kg ha/C01 treatment, however, were not
able to fully compensate through tillering to achieve
the same tiller density as the other treatments
(Figure 6). This treatment was included in the experi-
mental design to provide a wide range in plant
morphology so that the hypothesis could be tested
rigorously rather than with an expectation that it
would be a practical option for farmers.
With regard to the plant survival component of the
hypothesis, acceptance of the hypothesis is contingent
on the deﬁnition of ‘survival’ including the period
from sowing to the seedling stage (day 0 to around
day 49) when plants mainly supported between two
(Canterbury site, Figure 2c) and six (Northland and
Waikato sites, Figure 2a and 2b) tillers, on average.

in this phase of early sward establishment than it was
from seven weeks onwards. High seeding rates

and day 49 includes germination, which in this study
was not measured directly. Therefore, we cannot sepa-
rate treatment effects on germination vs. survival of
very young seedlings. Both processes are likely to be
operating. Brougham (1952) reported improved emer-
gence of short-rotation ryegrass seedlings one month
after sowing from a low seeding rate compared with
higher seeding rates (70% vs. 63–65% from swards
sown at 11 kg ha/C01 compared with 17 kg ha/C01 or
more), supporting the idea that some of the difference
recorded here was likely due to differences in germi-
nation. Increased seedling mortality at the cotyledon
and radical stage in the higher seeding rate treatments
is likely due to the greater interplant competition for
resources such as light, nutrients and water (Donald,
1963).
Heddle and Herriott (1954) also reported that the
percentage of viable perennial ryegrass seed sown that
germinated and survived to 8 weeks after sowing was
negatively related to seeding rate (58, 53 and 48% on
average across two cultivars for 11, 22 and
34 kg ha/C01, respectively), a trend that continued to
the ﬁnal measurement 18 months after sowing (45,
34 and 25%). Similar to the current study and that of
Charles (1961), they reported that a high proportion
of the original viable seed population (and therefore
of the genetic diversity within the original seed line) is
eliminated very early in the life of the pasture sward.
In the current study, the rate of plant mortality after
seven weeks post-sowing was much lower than in the
ﬁrst phase from day 0 to day 49 (Figure 3), which also
accords with Heddle and Herriott (1954). Both studies
provide some evidence for higher mortality during this
phase in higher seeding rate treatments as well. In
Heddle and Herriott (1954), about half the plants pre-
sent at eight weeks post-sowing had died after
18 months in the 34 kg seed ha/C01 treatment, com-
pared with one-third of plants in the lowest seeding
rate treatment. Here, plant survival between seven
weeks and nine months after sowing was higher in
the 6 kg ha/C01 treatment at the Waikato site – but not
at the other two sites.
Retrospective analysis of the current study data
indicated that there appeared to be a size threshold for
survival of plants throughout the ﬁrst year after sow-
ing; those that had accumulated at least ﬁfteen tillers
by late winter/early spring (ﬁve to six months after
sowing) were more likely to survive than those with
fewer tillers. These ﬁgures are similar to those in
Thom et al. (1986), who noted that large Nui peren-
nial ryegrass plants (seventeen tillers) had a higher
probability of surviving their ﬁrst summer than small
plants (ten tillers) growing in paspalum (Paspalum
dilatatum)-dominant swards. This information regard-
ing a threshold number of tillers per plant attained
within the ﬁrst few months of sward life may be a
useful benchmark for evaluating establishment options
for perennial ryegrass swards in the future. In a plant
population undergoing self-thinning (such as an estab-
lishing sward), smaller plants are more likely to be
eliminated (Harper and White, 1970), as was the case
in the aforementioned higher seeding rate treatments.
The denser swards that resulted from high seeding
rates had greater light interception during early stages
of regrowth in winter and spring at the Waikato site
(e.g. Figure 5); this reduction in the amount of light
penetrating into the canopy to reach the smaller
plants in the population likely contributed towards the
greater plant mortality in the high seeding rate treat-
ments.
It is acknowledged that the current study was sown
during a single year at each site. Roche et al. (2009)
demonstrated that a number of climatic variables
which strongly inﬂuence plant development and sur-
vival are highly variable across years within a site.
Thus, results from the current study likely represent
close to the best-case scenario for each site as climatic
conditions post-sowing were favourable in all cases.
Implications for herbage accumulation and
persistence
One thought was that the depleted population of
sown plants resulting from high seeding rates may be
less able to resist invasion by volunteer weed species
leading to loss of yield potential and failure of the
new sward to persist as expected. In the current study,
although there was greater plant mortality in the high
seeding rate treatments, the swards still maintained a
greater tiller density than the 6 kg ha/C01 treatment for
the majority (Northland and Waikato), or for the
whole of (Canterbury), the ﬁrst year after sowing. The
high seeding rate treatment swards also had a lower
initial weed population through winter and spring
than other treatments, although differences were rela-
tively small (average /C6 standard deviation 5 /C6 2%
DM). This weed suppression was presumably due to
the greater initial ground cover in the high seeding
rate treatments, an effect that has also been reported
by Brougham (1952) and Culleton and Murphy
(1987). The importance of post-emergence herbicide
application to the results from the current study
should be noted here. In this study, all sites received
post-emergence herbicide within eight weeks of sow-
ing; if herbicides had not been applied, weed ingres-
sion would likely have been much greater,

particularly in the low seeding rate treatments which
had less initial ground cover. It is extremely important
that farmers pay diligence to herbicide timing and
application to reduce weed ingression and improve
persistence, particularly when sowing swards at lower
seeding rates.
While reduced initial weed content may be one
beneﬁt to higher seeding rates (depending on herbi-
cide use), the downside resulting from the greater ini-
tial ground cover and increased interplant competition
was that establishment and growth of the slower
establishing, less competitive species, white clover,
was negatively affected. By late spring, swards in the
6kgha/C01 treatment contained 18% white clover on
average across the three sites compared with between
5 and 8% in the high seeding rate (18–30 kg ha/C01)
treatments. These differences between seeding rate
treatments were evident throughout the remainder of
the establishment year and are consistent with reports
from Brougham (1952), Cullen (1958) and Black et al.
(2006) that higher grass seeding rates suppressed
white clover growth in the ﬁrst year after sowing.
White clover is a beneﬁcial component of swards in
many temperate regions because it is able to ﬁx atmo-
spheric N2, complements seasonal growth patterns of
commonly used pasture grasses, has high nutritive
value for ruminants and improves herbage intake and
utilization (Caradus et al., 1996; Harris et al., 1998).
While the difference in white clover content reported
here is likely less than that required to inﬂuence ani-
mal production (Harris et al., 1998; Lee et al., 2004), it
is not known what the effect may be, if any, on long-
term sward persistence. The botanical composition, til-
ler density and herbage accumulation of the seeding
rate treatments will continue to be monitored for at
least another four years.
The effects of seeding rate on total herbage accu-
mulation in the ﬁrst year after sowing differed across
the three sites, with greater herbage accumulation
from the 30 kg ha/C01 treatment at the Northland site
compared with all other treatments, greater herbage
accumulation from the 24–30 kg ha/C01 treatments at
the Canterbury site compared with the 6–12 kg ha/C01
treatments and no effect of seeding rate at the Wai-
kato site. Results of previous studies investigating the
impact of ryegrass seeding rate on herbage accumula-
tion have also been equivocal; some studies report
greater herbage accumulation from higher seeding
rates for two years (Culleton and Murphy, 1987;
Venuto et al., 2004; Creighton et al., 2012), whereas
other studies report initially improved herbage accu-
mulation from greater ryegrass seeding rates which
dissipate after the ﬁrst year (Keane, 1980; Culleton
et al., 1986; Quigley, 2000) and others report similar
herbage accumulation across a range of seeding rates
over one to three years (Brougham, 1952; Heddle and
Herriott, 1954; Frame and Boyd, 1986). Thus, while
economically useful short-term improvements in her-
bage accumulation can be achieved sometimes using a
higher seeding rate, results will be variable in space
and time and hard to predict.
It is logical that swards sown at higher seeding rates
initially have greater yielding ability; they have more
plants in a denser sward which is able to intercept more
light. Over time the greater mortality of smaller plants
in swards sown at high seeding rates and the greater til-
lering ability of plants sown at low seeding rates (Hed-
dle and Herriott, 1954, 1955) bring the swards to a
common tiller density. The length of time that this pro-
cess takes depends on many factors (e.g. environmental
conditions, grass species/cultivar) and is one possible
reason for the differences in results in the previous
studies (i.e. studies reporting differences in herbage
accumulation had not yet attained a common tiller den-
sity across treatments; Culleton and Murphy, 1987;
Venuto et al., 2004). In the current study, it appeared to
be more complex, at least at the Northland and Waikato
sites; at the Northland site, tiller density was often simi-
lar across treatments from spring onwards, but herbage
accumulation remained greater from the 30 kg ha/C01
treatment, whereas at the Waikato site the seeding rate-
induced differences on tiller density were evident for
longer, but herbage accumulation remained similar.
It was not clear what was driving these site differ-
ences. Temperature plays a major role in the rate of til-
ler formation through its effects on leaf appearance rate
and, therefore, the production of meristematic bud sites
that can develop into new tillers (Davies and Thomas,
1983). Thus, it could be hypothesized that seeding rate
differences in herbage accumulation may dissipate
more rapidly in warmer regions as swards may reach a
common tiller density more quickly. This was not the
case in the current study, however. Herbage accumula-
tion at the Waikato site was similar across seeding rates
from the ﬁrst harvest in mid-June 2011 (three months
after sowing; 1031 growing degree-days (GDD) using a
base temperature of 0°C), while at the Northland and
Canterbury sites seeding rate differences were evident
throughout the year (accumulation of 7239 and 4808
GDD respectively). At these latter sites, 1031 GDD was
reached by early May and mid-July, respectively, at
which point the effect of seeding rate on herbage accu-
mulation was still evident.
Perennial ryegrass functional type
No seeding rate 9 cultivar interactions were evident
in the plant size or survival data, indicating that that
the conclusions from this study are valid for the range
of ryegrass functional types currently available.

Conclusion
The hypothesis that high perennial ryegrass seeding
rates (>18 kg seed ha/C01) reduce plant size and sur-
vival during the ﬁrst year after sowing was accepted.
However, these effects did not appear to predispose
swards to greater risk of physical depletion of plant
populations and, therefore, of persistence failure as,
within sites, all swards attained a similar equilibrium
tiller density which was well within the range of tiller
densities commonly reported for swards of productive
dairy pastures. This was true for all four of the rye-
grass functional types included in the experiment.
Monitoring of this experiment is continuing beyond
the period reported here to collect the data required to
conﬁrm whether or not this conclusion holds for long-
term persistence of pasture sward yield. This informa-
tion will be reported in subsequent publications.
