Department of Animal Science, University of California, Davis 95616
ABSTRACT: Beef steers were fed in two phases 1)
to determine the relative importance of changes in
DMI, gastrointestinal tract fill, energy expenditures,
and composition of gain in the compensatory growth
phenomenon, 2) to compare the effects of growth
restriction due to ad libitum consumption of a low-
energy (low-concentrate) diet to those of limited
intake of a high-energy (high-concentrate) feed, and
3) to examine changes in carcass composition and
quality resulting from different types of growth
restriction. During the growing phase (237 to 327 kg),
steers were fed either a high- ( C ) or low- ( F )
concentrate diet. Diet F was available for ad libitum
consumption (FA) and diet C was available either for
ad libitum consumption (CA) or on a limited basis
(CL) to match the live weight gains by the FA group.
During the finishing phase (327 to 481 kg), all steers
received diet C, either for ad libitum consumption
(CA) or restricted (CL) to 70% of the intake by the
corresponding CA steers. Backfat thickness was mar-
kedly reduced ( P < .001) by final feed restriction (7.4
and 6.9 mm for CL-CL and FA-CL respectively),
compared with CA-CA (12.6 mm). Backfat also was
lower in CL-CA (11.6 mm, P < .10) and FA-CA (9.9
mm, P < .05) than in CA-CA steers. Conversely,
marbling scores were similar among groups, except for
the FA-CL steers, which had lower marbling scores
than FA-CA and CL-CA steers ( P < .05). Higher DMI
following growth restriction were accompanied by
increased rates of live weight (+54 and +27%) and
empty body weight (EBW; +57 and +43%) gain for
CL-CA and FA-CA steers, respectively, compared with
CA-CA steers. Gain:feed (EBW basis) were improved
in some restricted/refed groups (+30, +13, and +10%,
for CL-CA, CL-CL, and FA-CA, respectively) relative
to CA-CA. Increased DMI played a major role in the
compensatory gain response in both CL-CA and FA-
CA groups. Maintenance requirement was reduced
( −17%) in CL-CA and increased in the FA-CA group
(+21%); both changes affected the magnitude of
compensatory gain in those animals. In contrast,
composition of gain had little or no effect on the
compensatory gain response. Programmed feeding can
be used to manipulate carcass quality, but low-
concentrate feeding during the growing phase may
impair overall feedlot performance.
Key Words: Restricted Feeding, Growth, Carcass Traits, Beef Cattle

Introduction
A growing phase for cattle is usually imposed for a
period between weaning and finishing in a feedlot.
The growing phase allows body development before
fattening, which in turn permits cattle to attain
slaughter finish at desirable carcass weights. Mar-
bling and quality grade are thus improved by allowing
the animals to reach greater maturity (Thompson and
O’Mary, 1983). Cattle are often under some nutri-
tional stress during the growing phase and exhibit
compensatory gain when placed on full feed. Compen-
satory gain, the more rapid and efficient growth of
animals following a period of feed restriction, plays an
important role in beef production. Animals entering
the finishing phase attract unit price premiums if
buyers perceive that they have undergone a period of
nutritional stress, because their subsequent perfor-
mance is expected to be superior to that of well-fed
animals. Although intensive research has shed some
light on the mechanisms of compensatory gain and its
consequences for body and carcass composition,
several aspects of these phenomena remain poorly
understood. In particular, the roles of increased feed
intake, altered composition of gain, and possibly
altered patterns of energy utilization have not been
fully elucidated.

Results from experiments using different planes of
nutrition have given conflicting results regarding
animal performance, feed intake, and carcass composi-
tion. Differences in severity, nature, and duration of
the growth restriction, as well as in the genetic
background of the animals, undoubtedly have con-
tributed to disparities among previous research
results. An experiment was therefore conducted with
several objectives: 1) to determine the relative impor-
tance of changes in feed intake, gastrointestinal tract
fill, energy expenditures, and composition of gain in
the compensatory growth phenomenon; 2) to compare
the effects of growth restriction due to ad libitum
consumption of a low-energy (forage) diet to those of
limited intake of a high-energy (concentrate) feed;
and 3) to examine changes in carcass composition and
quality resulting from different types of growth
restriction.
Materials and Methods
Animals and Treatments
One hundred thirty-five medium-framed British
breed steer calves were subjected to a 3-wk period of
adaptation to a low-concentrate diet (Table 1) before
commencing the trial. Steers were housed in in-
dividual pens and fed once daily at approximately
0800 h. Calves were stratified by weight and 15 calves
were assigned to each of nine slaughter groups. These
included an initial slaughter group, three slaughter
groups following the growing phase, and five slaughter
groups at the end of the finishing phase (Table 2).
Treatments were applied during two phases, based on
average weights of steers within each group: from 237
to 327 kg (growing phase) and from 327 to 481 kg
(finishing phase). During the growing phase, steers
were fed one of two diets (high- or low-concentrate;
Table 1). The low-concentrate diet was available
continuously ( FA) and the high-concentrate diet was
either available continuously ( CA) or was limited
( CL) to match the weight gains by the FA group
(Table 2 and Figure 1). During the finishing phase,
steers were fed the high-concentrate diet, either for ad
libitum consumption (CA) or restricted to 70% of the
intake by the corresponding CA steers (CL).

Results and Discussion
Carcass Characteristics
Growing Phase. At the end of the growing phase,
EBW were lower ( P < .001) in FA than in CA steers,
even after covariance adjustment for differences in
fasted body weight, whereas CL steers were inter-
mediate (Table 4). Gut fill (GI tract contents) was
highest in FA and lowest in CA steers; that of the CL
steers was intermediate. After covariance adjustment
to a common EBW (293 kg), there were no treatment
differences in carcass weight. Several measures of
fatness, including backfat, KPH, abdominal fat, mar-
bling score, and carcass fat content (estimated based
on density), were lowest in FA, highest in CA, and
intermediate in CL steers. In contrast, longissimus
muscle areas did not differ among groups. Therefore,
growth retardation during the growing phase seemed
to have greatest impact on fat accretion, without
impairing muscle development (at equal EBW). In
fact, total empty body protein was higher in CL and
FA than in CA steers, at equal EBW (Table 4).
Finishing Phase. After the finishing phase, GI tract
contents were similar in groups given ad libitum
access to feed (CA-CA, CL-CA, and FA-CA) but were
increased in groups that were limit-fed (CL-CL and
FA-CL). As a consequence, EBW were lower in
restricted groups than in steers with ad libitum intake
(Table 5). The observation that gut fill was increased
in intake-restricted animals was unexpected but was
consistent with data obtained after the growing phase.
Even so, the possibility of an experimental artifact
(e.g., due to differences in length of fast, etc.) cannot
be excluded. Unfortunately, in the absence of informa-
tion on dry matter contents of the digesta, rates of
passage, and so on these data are difficult to interpret
and must await confirmation. That being the case,
analysis of all slaughter data on an equal EBW basis
seemed the best approach for the present experiment.
Of the steers finished with ad libitum intakes, FA-
CA steers had lower carcass weights (adjusted for
differences in EBW) than CA-CA or CL-CA steers,
reflecting increased weights of non-carcass tissues
(data not shown). In this experiment, longissimus
muscle areas showed no clear pattern among groups.
Longissimus muscle areas were reduced in CL-CL and
FA-CA, compared with CA-CA and CL-CA; FA-CL
showed intermediate longissimus muscle areas. Calcu-
lated USDA yield grades were lower ( P < .001) in FA-
CL steers than in CA-CA and FA-CA steers (Table 5).

Marbling scores were similar among groups, except for
the FA-CL steers, which had lower marbling scores
than FA-CA and CL-CA steers ( P < .05). Quality
grades ranged from high Standard to low Choice, but
means were similar for all groups.
Backfat thickness over the 12th−13th ribs was
markedly reduced ( P < .001) by final feed restriction
( −41 and −45% for CL-CL and FA-CL, respectively)
compared with CA-CA. Similar differences were found
in abdominal fat weights, carcass fat content, and
empty body fat weight. These results support several
previous studies in which the proportion of fat
deposited in the carcass was directly related to energy
intake during the finishing phase (see Byers, 1982 for
review). Effects of dietary restriction on subsequent
body composition are less clear. Steers fed a low-
concentrate diet in the growing phase and finished
with ad libitum access to feed (FA-CA) had reduced
backfat ( −21%, P < .05) compared with full-fed
controls (CA-CA). Steers that were limit-fed during
the growing phase and finished ad libitum (CL-CA)
also tended to have lower backfat ( −8%, P < .10) than
CA-CA. Internal fat depots varied; whereas abdominal
fat was reduced in groups finished at restricted
intakes (CL-CL and FA-CL), it was similar for all
groups finished with ad libitum access to feed (CA-
CA, CL-CA, and FA-CA). In contrast, KPH was
similar in all groups except FA-CL, which had less
KPH relative to FA-CA (Table 5). This discrepancy
may be due to the fact that abdominal fat was
dissected and measured, but KPH was based on a
visual assessment. Overall carcass fat concentration
and empty body fat mass followed a pattern resem-
bling that of abdominal fat: these were similar for
groups finished with ad libitum intake (CA-CA, CL-
CA, and FA-CA) and were reduced in FA-CL and CL-
CL compared with full-fed controls (CA-CA). Differ-
ent workers have found that the empty body and(or)
carcass fat contents of steers that experienced compen-
satory gains were unaffected (Fox et al., 1972;
Rompala et al., 1985), were lower (Smith et al., 1977;
Mader et al., 1989), or were higher (Tudor et al.,
1980; Abdalla et al., 1988) than those of controls.
These differences could be due to differences in the
genotypes, as well as in degree of nutritional restric-
tion. Moreover, the timing of measurement could
change the conclusions, because the composition of
compensatory gains changes over time from leaner to
fatter than full-fed controls (Fox et al., 1972; Rompala
et al., 1985; Wright and Russel, 1991). In those
reports, final empty body and(or) carcass composition
was unaffected by restriction and refeeding, in agree-
ment with the results of the present study (Table 5).
Despite the similarity in total carcass fat contents,
backfat was reduced and KPH was increased in FA-
CA steers compared with the CA-CA group. Although
not conclusive, the present results suggest that there
may be differential changes in different fat depots that
may be related to the timing of the growth restriction.
Limited intake of dietary energy during the period of
subcutaneous adipose tissue development (Robelin,
1986) could have lasting effects on deposition of lipid
in this depot without impairing fat accretion in later-
developing sites (e.g., intramuscular fat). Alterna-
tively, the differential effects on adipose depots may
reflect differences in their sensitivities to nutritional
controls. These hypotheses could have important
implications for dietary manipulation of carcass qual-
ity, but further work will be required to confirm or
refute them.

Feed Intakes and Growth Performance
Growing phase. Average DMI during the growing
phase were identical for steers fed the high-concen-
trate and those fed the low-concentrate diets for ad
libitum intake (Table 6), consistent with the hypothe-
sis that feed bulk limited DMI in the growing phase.
The NRC (1984) feed intake equation, which predicts
higher energy intakes for diets with increasing energy
content, under predicted DMI for both CA (7.15 v 8.41
kg/d) and FA (6.69 v 8.41 kg/d) steers. Similar total
intakes of these two diets clearly resulted in reduced
ME intakes for the FA group, as reflected in their
reduced gains (Table 6). Limit-feeding of CL steers
resulted (by design) in ADG similar to those of FA
steers (.73 and .76 kg/d for CL and FA steers,
respectively, compared with 1.99 kg/d for CA), but
slightly lower ( P > .05) rates of EBW gain (due to
differences in GI tract fill). Rates of EBW gain were
more reflective than ADG of ME intake, which was
lower in CL than in FA steers (Table 7). Probably,
ME, and not protein intake, limited growth in the
growing phase, because the reductions (relative to
CA) in EBW gain ( −65 and −61%) and ME intake

( −46 and −39%) were more similar than the reduc-
tions in protein intake ( −39 and −9%) in CL and FA,
respectively. Feed efficiencies during the growing
phase were different among groups, but these were
confounded by differences in energy densities of the
diets and intakes. Expression of feed efficiencies on a
ME basis (i.e., grams of EBW gain:megacalories of
ME intake) shows the highest efficiency for CA (78)
and the lowest for FA (39). The CL (48) steers were
intermediate (SE 1.5; P < .001). One would expect
gross efficiency to decline at lower intakes, but the
difference between CL and FA confirms the well-
known reduced efficiency of utilization of roughages
relative to concentrates. The CL steers were fatter
than the FA steers at the end of the growing phase
because of their higher (+57%) rates of fat gain, not
because of lower protein gains ( −4%). This may have
been partly due to the different patterns of absorbed
nutrients, including amino acids and VFA, as well as
possible endocrine changes. In addition, FA steers had
higher (+23%) rates of heat production than CL
steers, so less energy was available for fat deposition.
Finishing Phase. Growth restriction during the
growing phase clearly resulted in compensatory gains
in the finishing period, demonstrated by increased
(relative to CA-CA) rates of empty body weight gain
in CL-CA and FA-CA and improved feed efficiency in
CL-CA, CL-CL, and FA-CA (Table 6). Feed efficiency
during the finishing phase was improved by early
limit-feeding, regardless of DMI in the finishing
phase, as shown by higher gain:feed for CL-CA and
CL-CL compared with CA-CA steers (Table 6).
Overall rates of gain were reduced by growth restric-
tion during the growing phase (Table 6), but total
feed requirements were not changed by limit-feeding
in the growing phase (i.e., gain:feed was not changed
in CL-CA and CL-CL groups relative to CA-CA).
Overall feed efficiency was impaired only in the FA-
CA and FA-CL groups, due to very low gain:feed in the
growing phase, both on a weight basis and on a ME
basis.
The remainder of this discussion will focus on the
mechanisms responsible for increased gains and(or)
feed efficiency in these groups. Carstens et al. (1991)
concluded that changes in gut fill and energy content
of gain accounted for much of the compensatory
growth response but also suggested that changes in
feed intake and maintenance energy requirements
might be involved. The present study was designed to
enable estimation of the relative contributions of DMI,
gut fill, maintenance energy requirement, protein:fat
ratio (as related to their respective partial efficiencies
of deposition), and energy content of gain in the
compensatory gain response.
Upon refeeding, DMI of CL-CA and FA-CA steers
were 21 and 30% greater, respectively, than those of
CA-CA steers. In fact, DMI for the CA-CA group (9.04
kg/d) were very similar to those predicted by NRC
(1984) equations (9.06 kg/d), but intakes by CL-CA
(10.98 kg/d) and FA-CA (11.73 kg/d) steers were
higher than predicted, even after adjusting (+10%) for
increased age. Whereas some authors have reported
that cattle placed on full feed after a period of
restriction have increased feed intakes (Fox et al.,
1972; Mader et al., 1989; Ryan et al., 1993), others
have seen no change (Rompala et al., 1985; Coleman
and Evans, 1986; Carstens et al., 1991). In his review
of compensatory growth, Hogg (1991) pointed out the
great variability in refeeding intakes, adding that this
might be partly explained by differences in diet energy
content. Clearly, animals that consume more feed
would be expected to grow more rapidly and effi-
ciently, but the relative importance of DMI in the
compensatory gain phenomenon remains unknown.
As seen in Table 6, EBW gains were increased in
CL-CA and FA-CA steers, relative to CA-CA, so that
the compensatory growth response was independent of
changes in gut fill. Maintenance energy requirements
for three groups (CA-CA; CL-CA and CL-CL; and FA-
CA and FA-CL) were estimated as the intercepts of
the respective regressions of ME intake against
retained energy (Sainz and Oltjen, 1994). Steers fed
the low-concentrate diet during the growing phase
(FA-CA and FA-CL) had increased maintenance
energy requirements during the finishing phase
(+21%, P < .001) compared with steers finished on the
high-concentrate diet with ad libitum intake (CA-
CA). In contrast, limit-fed steers (CL-CA and CL-CL)
had reduced maintenance requirements ( −17%, P <
.001) compared with CA-CA. Finally, proportions of
protein:fat in the weight gains were numerically
increased in CL-CA (+5%) and decreased ( −14%) in
FA-CA, relative to CA-CA steers, although these
changes were not statistically significant ( P > .05).
The effects of these changes on the compensatory
growth response were assessed by making successive
modifications to the standard equations for predicting
DMI, maintenance energy requirement, net energy for
gain, and EBW gain (NRC, 1984). For example, the
effect of increased DMI was estimated by calculation
of NEg values and EBW gains based on actual DMI for
each animal. To assess the potential impact of
observed changes in maintenance requirement, the
standard factor for calculation of maintenance (.077
Mcal/kg.75) was multiplied by 1 (CA-CA), .83 (CL-
CA), or 1.21 (FA-CA). Similarly, the effects of

fourth the energy required for accretion of fat (Pullar
and Webster, 1977). Therefore, the EBW gains
estimated from NEg using the NRC (1984) equation
were multiplied by an adjustment factor, calculated as

The proportions of protein and fat in the gain are
thought to influence the efficiency of growth. Several
studies with nonruminants indicate that accretion of
lean is four times as efficient (on a kilocalories/gram
basis) as deposition of fat (Pullar and Webster,
1977). The same proportion (i.e., the relative efficien-
cies of lean to fat accretion) in ruminants has been
lower and more variable, ranging from .8 to 1.8 in one
review (Rattray and Joyce, 1976) but reaching 2.7 in
a study with growing lambs (Sainz et al., 1990). In
this study, the proportions of protein and fat in the
gain were not significantly altered, although there
were numerical differences. Several studies have
shown that gains during early compensation contain
more protein, but later gains contain more fat, relative
to full-fed controls (Fox et al., 1972; Rompala et al.,
1985; Wright and Russel, 1991). These reciprocal
changes result in nearly equal body compositions at
slaughter, in agreement with the present results in
steers finished with ad libitum access to feed.
Nevertheless, changes in composition of gain may
have played a role in the compensatory gain response,
particularly in CL-CA steers. Changes in the composi-
tion (and energy content) of gain had minor effects in
FA-CA but may have contributed up to 14% of the
response in CL-CA. This estimate includes changes in
hydration as well as protein:fat in gain. Due to the
high value assigned to the relative efficiencies of lean
and fat gain, this is likely an overestimate of the
impact of the composition of gain.

compensatory gain response in either CL-CA or FA-
CA. In contrast, increased DMI play a major role in

Implications
Steers recovering from growth restriction exhibited
more rapid (compensatory) growth upon refeeding.
For both low-concentrate and limit-fed steers this was
mainly due to increased dry matter intake; steers
limit-fed concentrate also converted feed more effi-
ciently during the finishing period than those previ-
ously fed a low-concentrate diet. Carcass fat content
was unaffected, but fat distribution was altered so
that growth-restricted/refed steers had less subcutane-
ous and more internal fat. Programmed feeding can be
used to alter carcass quality, but low-concentrate
feeding during the growing phase may impair overall
feedlot performance.
