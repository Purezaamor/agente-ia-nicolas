Summary 
This paper is a review of the non-nutritive, 
exogenous substances which improve animal 
growth and feed conversion. These agents are 
categorized according to the methods through 
which they improve livestock performance. 
Categories defined and discussed include anti­
microbial production improvers, rumen addi­
tives, anabolic growth promotants and "other" 
production improvers. The exogenous sub­
stances covered include primarily drugs with an 
approved New Animal Drug Application 
(NADA) with the U.S. Food and Drug Adminis­
tration. Two exceptions are thiopeptin, an anti­
biotic, and trenbolone acetate, a synthetic 
androgen. These compounds are discussed be­
cause they work through modes of action 
where approved drugs are not available. Agents 
for the control of parasitic diseases will not be 
covered. The modes of action through which 
these substances act are examined in detail. 
Some recent studies involving the effects of 
exogenous hormones on the growth, feed con­
version and carcass composition of normal, 
growing lambs are presented. The hormones dis­
cussed include growth hormone, prolactin and 
thyrotrophin-releasing hormone. Also, a study 
on the effects of exogenous GH on milk pro­
duction is included. Finally, the future of pro­
duction improvers for livestock is discussed. 
(Key Words: Livestock, Production Improvers, 
Antimicrobial Agents, Rumen Additives, 
Anabolic Growth Promotants, Exogenous 
Hormones.)

In the past, any drug which improved animal 
performance was called a growth promotant.

This generalization has caused some confusion 
concerning how these agents work. The purpose 
of this paper is to categorize non-nutritive, 
exogenous substances which improve livestock 
production according to their activity and to 
examine the modes of action through which 
these substances act. These categories have been 
described previously (Muir et al., 1977), but are 
not widely known. The exogenous substances 
covered in this discussion will be primarily 
drugs with an approved New Animal Drug Ap­
plication (NADA) with the U.S. Food and Drug 
Administration (FDA). Two exceptions are 
thiopeptin, an antibiotic, and trenbolone ace­
tate, a synthetic androgen. These compounds 
are discussed because they work through modes 
of action where approved drugs are not avail­
able. These agents are usually administered as 
feed additives or implants. Drugs for the con­
trol of parasitic diseases will not be covered in 
order to limit the scope of this presentation. 
Next, some recent studies on the effects of 
growth hormone (GH), prolactin (Prl) and 
thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) on 
animal growth and carcass composition will be 
presented. Finally, the future role of non-
nutritive, exogenous substances to improve 
livestock production will be discussed. 
Non-nutritive, exogenous substances which 
improve animal performance will be referred to 
as animal production improvers. Animal pro­
duction includes body weight gain, feed con­
version, milk production and litter size for 
swine. Feed conversion is the units of feed re­
quired to produce a unit of body weight gain. 
Feed efficiency, the reciprocal of feed conver­
sion, is the units of gain per unit of feed. 
Categories of production improvers are anti­
microbial production improvers, rumen addi­
tives, anabolic growth promotants and "other" 
production improvers. Antimicrobial produc­
tion improvers include antibiotics, antibacterial 
agents and antifungal agents which improve 
animal performance by killing or inhibiting the

growth of microorganisms. Antibiotics are sub­
stances produced by microorganisms which kill 
or inhibit the growth of other microorganisms. 
Antibacterial agents are synthetic substances 
which kill or inhibit the growth of micro­
organisms. Antifungal agents are natural or 
synthetic substances which inhibit the growth 
of fungi. The category of antimicrobial produc­
tion improvers consists of two classes: growth 
permittants and growth effectors. Growth per-
mittants allow animals, presumably depressed 
in growth rate and feed efficiency by the action 
of intestinal microflora, to grow at their normal 
potential; while growth effectors improve 
growth and feed efficiency by controlling clini­
cal and subclinical infectious diseases. Rumen 
additives are substances which improve rumi­
nant growth or feed efficiency by altering 
rumen fermentation. Rumen additives may or 
may not be antimicrobial agents. Anabolic 
growth promotants are substances which im­
prove growth rate or feed efficiency through 
systemic action on the hormonal, biochemical 
or metabolic processes of the animal. "Other" 
production improvers is a general category, 
including substances which improve animal per­
formance by means such as metabolic disease 
control. 
Antimicrobial Production Improvers 
Growth Permittants. The discovery that 
chtortetracycline improved the growth and feed 
efficiency of apparently healthy poultry (Stok-
stad and Jukes, 1950) and swine (Jukes et al., 
1950) stimulated considerable research on the 
role of antibiotics in animal growth. Matherson 
and Singsen (1951) observed that antibiotics 
did not have to be absorbed to be potent 
growth stimulators. Gordon (1952) reported 
that penicillin fed to chicks decreased intestinal 
weight. Stokstad et al. (1953) found that anti­
biotic growth response in chicks is enhanced 
when animals are fed a sucrose diet. 
A clear concept of growth permittancy 
emerged from studies that followed these initial 
observations. In disease-free, conventionally-
reared young animals, growth rate can be in­
creased approximately 20% by feeding a 
growth-permittant antibiotic (Forbes and Park, 
1959; Eyssen and De Somer, 1967). Young 
germ-free animals grow approximately 20% 
faster than similar animals reared conven­
tionally, but the germ-free animals do not 
respond to growth-permittant antibiotics 
(Forbes and Park, 1959; Eyssen and De Somer, 
1967). Germ-free animals, inoculated with 
microorganisms common to the intestinal track, 
grow slower than germ-free controls and at a 
rate similar to conventionally reared animals 
(Lev and Forbes, 1959; Eyssen and De Somer, 
1967). Feeding growth-permittant antibiotics 
to these inoculated germ-free animals restores 
growth rate to the level of the germ-free animal 
(Lev and Forbes, 1959; Eyssen and De Somer, 
1967). Thus growth permittants are antimicro­
bial agents which allow animals, presumably 
depressed in growth rate and feed efficiency by 
the action of intestinal bacteria, to grow to 
their normal potential. 
Growth permittants are effective in rumi­
nants as well as swine and poultry (Wallace, 
1970). The growth and feed conversion re­
sponses by cattle to growth-permittant anti­
biotics are inversely related to the age of the 
animal (table 1). Newborn calves gave responses 
of 18.7 and 7.8% for growth and feed conver­
sion, respectively, while responses in finisher 
cattle were 6.7 and 4.9% for growth and feed 
conversion, respectively. Growth permittants 
do not appeat to improve the performance of 
mature cattle (Wallace, 1970).

The mode of action of growth permittants 
involves the inhibition of some "normal" non­
pathogenic intestinal microflora. Not all "nor­
mal" intestinal microbes have a negative effect 
on growth (Lev and Forbes, 1959). The micro­
bes which have been implicated include S. 
faecalis (Eyssen and De Somer, 1967), S. face-
ium (Coates, 1980) and C. perfringens (Lev and 
Forbes, 1959; Stutz et al., 1982a,b). A signifi­
cant correlation has been observed between the 
dietary level of the growth permittant, thio­
peptin, the improvement in weight gain and 
feed conversion, and the decrease in C. per­
fringens found in the ileal contents of chicks 
(table 2) supporting results obtained with 
monocontamination of germ-free chicks with 
this microorganism. 
How altering intestinal microflora improves 
animal performance is not fully understood. 
There are significant correlations between the 
dietary level of a growth permittant, such as 
bacitracin, and both body weight (positive) and 
weight of the small intestine (negative, table 3). 
Additional studies indicate the decrease in 
intestinal weight is due to reduced length and 
thickness of the small intestine (Jukes et al., 
1956; Stutz et al., 1982b). These data suggest a 
major decrease in number, and possibly turn­
over rate, of mucosal ceEs. Although the effects 
of growth permittants on intestinal mucosal

A decrease in number and turn-over rate of 
intestinal mucosal cells could partially explain 
the improved performance obtained with 
growth permittants. Webster (1980) studied the 
contribution of the major body tissues to pro­
tein synthesis in young rats. His data suggest 
that, although intestinal tissue contains less 
than 10% of total body protein, approximately 
50% of the protein synthesis in the body occurs 
in intestinal tissue (table 4). Thus, a small de­
crease in protein synthesis by intestinal tissue 
would be expected to greatly lower the animal's 
protein and energy maintenance requirements. 
The spared nutrients and energy should then be 
available for body growth. 
Other proposed modes of action of growth 
permittants include increased efficiency of 
nutrient absorption (March and Biely, 1967), 
inhibition of toxin production (Visek, 1978) 
and nutrient-sparing action (Jukes, 1971). The 
thinner intestinal wall, obtained when growth 
permittants are given, may allow better absorp­
tion of nutrients from the digesta. Growth per­
mittants may inhibit intestinal microbes which 
produce toxins that restrict growth (Visek, 
1978). These toxins could be exotoxins, nor­
mal microbial metabolites, such as ammonia, or 
food components converted to toxins by micro­
bial action. Finally, growth permittants may 
inhibit intestinal microbes which compete with 
the host for food nutrients. Additional research 
is required to determine which mode of action 
or combination of actions are responsible for 
the improved animal production observed with 
growth permittants. 
Antimicrobial agents approved by the U.S. 
FDA for use in animal feeds and their mode of 
production improvement are presented in tables 
5 (antibiotics) and 6 (antibacterial and antifun­
gal agents). Approximately 70% of the ap­
proved antibiotics have growth permittant 
activity, while only one of the antibacterial 
agents, carbadox, has growth permittant 
activity. 
Growth Effectors. Infectious diseases have a 
negative impact on the growth and feed conver­
sion of animals. Antimicrobial agents with 
growth-effector activity inhibit the microorga­
nisms responsible for a clinical or subclinical 
infectious disease and restore animal perfor­
mance. An example of growth effector activity 
is ptesented in table 7. Young pigs were in­
fected with Vibrio coli and Treponema hyo-
dysenteriae to induce hemorrhagic dysentery, 
an enteric disease. Half of the pigs were treated 
with tylosin to cure the disease. Pigs given 
tylosin gained 119% more weight and their 
feed conversion was improved 86% compared 
to infected controls. Since tylosin does not 
have growth-permittant activity (table 5), the 
improved performance resulted from control­
ling the infectious disease.

Rumen Additives 
VFA Shifting and Methane Inhibition. 
Ruminant nutritionists have searched for a long 
time to find agents which would improve the 
efficiency of rumen fermentation. Antibiotics, 
such as penicillin, chlortetracycline and bacitra­
cin, were studied as potential manipulators of 
rumen fermentation. At practical levels, such as 
those approved by the U.S. FDA for use in 
cattle, these antibiotics do not greatly alter 
rumen fermentation. At higher levels these anti­
biotics can profoundly alter rumen fermenta­
tion, but none of these changes were associated 
with improved animal performance. Halo-
genated methane analogs were found to inhibit 
methane production; however, the efficacy of 
methane inhibitors for improving ruminant per­
formance has been minimal and highly variable 
(Chalupa, 1980). 
Monensin (Rumensin®), an ionophore and 
antibiotic with narrow-spectrum activity, was 
approved in 1975 by the U.S. FDA as the first 
rumen additive for improving the feed effi­
ciency of cattle. When monensin is fed to feed-
lot cattle receiving typical feedlot diets, there is 
a dose-related decrease in feed consumption, a 
slight to no increase in gain and an improve­
ment in feed conversion (Rumensin Technical 
Manual, 1975). If monensin is fed above use 
level, feed intake declines further and improve­
ments in feed conversion are lost. Later, 
monensin was approved by the U.S. FDA for 
increasing the weight gain of pastured cattle. 
Doses of monensin from 50 to 200 mg/d cause 
dose-related improvements in rate of gain of 
pastured cattle (Rumensin Technical Manual,

Studies by Potter et al. (1976) and Daenicke 
et al. (1982) indicate that cattle fed monensin 
retain a greater percentage of dietary energy in 
their carcasses (table 10). Potter et al. (1976), 
using corn and corn cob diets fed ad libitum to 
fattening cattle, estimated the digestible energy 
(DE) consumed in the feed and the gain in 
carcass energy. Expressing the gain in carcass 
energy as a percentage of dietary energy, they 
found that monensin at 22 and 33 ppm in­
creased energy retention 13.8 and 8.8%, respec­
tively. More recently, Daenicke et al. (1982) 
pair-fed bulls a maize silage and cereal grain 
concentrate diet at 90 to 95% of ad libitum 
with and without monensin at 30 ppm from 
weaning to 500 kg body weight. Feed energy 
was the same for both treatments and esti­
mated to be 16.5 Meal of metabolizable energy 
(ME) per d. Gains in carcass energy were differ­
ent (P<.01); 3.15 and 3.66 Meal for control 
and monensin-fed bulls, respectively. Express­
ing the gain in carcass energy as a percentage of 
dietary energy, a 16.3% increase with monensin 
is realized. These data indicate that the im­
provements in weight gain and feed conversion 
observed with monensin in the live animal are 
realized in the edible carcass and that the effect 
of monensin is not due to a restriction of feed 
intake. These data do not indicate whether the 
increased retention of dietary energy is a func­
tion of improved efficiency of fermentation, 
decreased maintenance requirements or a shift 
in carcass composition. 
Monensin alters rumen fermentation causing 
dose-related decreases in acetate and increases 
in propionate in cattle fed either low- or high-
roughage diets (table 11). This shift in volatile 
fatty acids (VFA) is associated with a decrease 
in methane production of approximately 30%, 
and no accumulation of gaseous hydrogen 
(Broome, 1980; Chalupa, 1980). In vitro 
studies with monensin and pure cultures of 
rumen bacteria suggest that the decrease in 
methane and acetate and the increase in pro­
pionate production may result from the selec­
tive effects of monensin against acetate- and 
hydrogen-producers, such as Ruminococcus 
albus, R. flavefaciens and Butyrivibrio fibrisol-
vens, and for propionate producers, such as 
Selenomonas ruminantium (Chen and Wolin, 
1978).

Increased propionate production at the ex­
pense of acetate and methane should improve 
the efficiency of rumen fermentation, since 
propionate recovers 109% of the energy of glu­
cose compared to only 62% for acetate (figure 
1). Theoretical estimates (R. L. Baldwin, per­
sonal communication) indicate that a 30% 
reduction in methane, with complete recovery 
of the energy, would improve efficiency ap­
proximately 2.5% for roughage diets and less 
for concentrate diets. The VFA shifts and 
reduction in methane, observed with monensin 
feeding, appear to account for some, but not 
all, of the 9 to 16% improvement in retention 
of dietary energy described previously. 
Other mode(s) of action for monensin must 
be involved. Broome (1980) discussed three 
additional proposals, including improved 
protein or amino acid output by the rumen, 
increased efficiency of VFA utilization by the 
animal and improved efficiency of nitrogen re­
tention. However, evidence to support these 
postulated modes of action as important con­
tributors to the efficacy of monensin is not 
available. More research is required to fully 
understand monensin's mode(s) of action. The 
effects of monensin on maintenance energy 
requirements of the animal and on the compo­
sition of gain are areas which appear worthy of 
further study.

Prevention of  Lactic Acidosis. Lactic acido­
sis is a  common metabolic disorder that occurs 
in ruminants following  the  overeating of highly 
digestable carbohydrate (Baldwin, 1967). 
Streptococcus bovis,  a  gram-positive microbe 
which is  normally present  in the  rumen, initia­
tes the  disorder  by  converting dietary starches 
and sugars  to  lactic acid (Chaplin  and  Jones, 
1973). Lactic acid production dominates rumen 
fermentation to the  exclusion of VFA,  causing 
ruminal acidosis  and absorption of  large quanti­
ties of  lactic acid. Blood lactic acid increases 
and blood base excess decreases causing syste­
mic acidosis (Muir  et al.,  1981). Reduced ani­

Anabolic Growth Promotants 
Estrogenic Agents. Estrogens are the major 
class of anabolic growth promotants for rumi­
nants. Estrogens are not anabolic for swine and 
cause increased fattening in poultry (Trenkle, 
1969). Diethylstilbestrol (DES), a synthetic 
estrogen, was first shown to increase the gain of 
implanted heifers (Dinusson et al., 1948) and, 
by 1954, had been shown to be orally active in 
both sheep and cattle (Burroughs et al., 1954; 
Dinusson et al., 1950). Although DES is no 
longer approved by the U.S. FDA for cattle use, 
other estrogenic agents are available as ear im­
plants including Compudose® (17|3-estradiol) 
for steers, Ralgro® (zeranol) for steers, heifers 
and lambs, Synovex-H® (estradiol benzoate 
with testosterone propionate) for heifers and 
Synovex-S® (estradiol benzoate with proges­
terone) for steers (table 13). Data collected on 
estrogenic agents over the past 30 yr indicate 
that estrogens are most effective in castrated 
male ruminants, where improvements in gain 
and feed conversion of 10 to 20% can be ob­
tained without altering feed intake (table 14). 
Estrogenic agents are less effective in females 
(Clegg and Cole, 1954) and do not appear to 
improve the performance of intact males (Baker 
and Arthaud, 1982). Recent reviews on estro­
genic agents for cattle include Heitzman (1980) 
and Preston (1975).

carcasses of wether lambs (Muir et al., 1982)

genic agents increase GH secretion which, in 
turn, promotes growth, feed conversion and 
carcass protein accretion. 
The concept that DES works through in­
creased GH secretion was supported by the 
studies of Struempler and Burroughs (1957, 
1959). They reported that nitrogen balance of 
lambs fed high or low protein and energy diets 
was increased by both DES and injected bovine 
GH (bGH). In addition, they demonstrated that 
cattle fed DES had larger pituitaries with more 
GH. Davis et al. (1970a,b) also reported that 
DES and injected ovine GH (oGH) increased 
nitrogen retention by lambs. However, L. A. 
Muir (unpublished data) was unable to demon­
strate increased nitrogen retention in lambs 
given daily injections of bGH at 5, 10 or 20 
mg/d (table 15). The effects of DES on plasma 
GH levels of ruminants have been examined 
with varying results. Trenkle saw no effect of 
DES on plasma GH of lambs (Trenkle, 1969), 
but observed an increase in plasma GH of cat­
tle (Trenkle, 1970). Swan et al. (1972) found 
no increase in plasma GH of cattle given DES. 
Davis et al. (1977) examined the effects of DES 
on the secretory patterns of plasma oGH in 
wether lambs. They injected DES at 2 mg/d and 
collected blood samples every 15 min for 12 h 
on d 23 of treatment. In their study, DES 
doubled overall means and base-line values of 
plasma oGH compared to controls and ap­
peared to increase the amplitude of the secre­
tory spikes. However, the period of treatment 
and dose of DES required to obtain this oGH 
response was not investigated. In a recent 
study, growing lambs were injected daily with 
oGH and compared to control and DES-treated 
lambs for effects on performance, carcass com­
position and plasma GH levels (Muir et al., 
1982). The results of this study, which will be 
discussed in a later section, do not support the 
concept that the total mode of action of DES 
is through increased GH secretion.

improves ruminant performance through in­
creased adrenal androgen production caused by 
a direct stimulation of ACTH. However, growth 
responses to testosterone or adrenal androgens 
have not been on a par with DES (Andrews et 
al., 1956; Dinusson et al., 1950; Preston, 1975).

Trenkle (1969, 1970) found that DES 
increased plasma insulin in cattle. L. A. Muir 
observed similar effects with DES in lambs 
(unpublished data). It has been suggested that 
this increase in insulin might be responsible for 
the anabolic action of estrogens by stimulating 
protein synthesis (Preston, 1975). However, 
elevated insulin levels, in addition to causing 
increased protein synthesis, would be expected 
to cause enhanced lipogenesis. Possibly, DES 
causes a diabetogenic effect that favors protein 
synthesis over lipogenesis (Y. T. Yang, personal 
communication). Further studies regarding the 
role of insulin in the mode of action of DES are 
required. 
Another possibility is that estrogens may act 
directly on skeletal muscle cells to cause pro­
tein accretion. Snochowski et al. (1981) indi­
cated that estrogen receptors have been found 
in rat skeletal muscle, but the supporting data 
have not been published. Skeletal muscle cells 
have androgen (Michel and Baulieu, 1980) and 
glucocorticoid receptors (Ballard et al., 1974), 
but estrogens do not compete for these recep­
tors (Snochowski et al., 1981). Thus, estrogens 
do not appear to act through these receptors 
to block the catabolic action of glucocorticoids 
or to activate the androgen receptor. More 
work is needed to determine if estrogens have 
a direct effect on skeletal muscle cells. 
Progestogenic Agents. Progestogenic agents 
improve the growth and feed conversion of 
cyclic heifers. Melengestrol acetate is a synthe­
tic progestogen with oral activity that is 
approximately 100 times more active than 
progesterone. As a feed additive for heifers 
(table 16), melengestrol acetate improves gain 
and feed conversion by as much as 10% without 
altering carcass composition (Davis, 1969). The 
mode of action of melengestrol acetate appears 
to be through estrus suppression and reduction 
of estrus related problems, such as reduced feed 
consumption and hyperactivity, since the drug 
works in cyclic heifers, but not pregnant or 
spayed heifers or steers (Davis, 1969). 
Androgenic Agents. There are no androgenic 
agents approved for livestock use as anabolic 
promotants by the U.S. FDA. Nevertheless, 
androgenic agents do improve the growth, feed 
conversion and carcass protein content of cattle 
and swine, and are most effective in females 
(Heitzman, 1980). In Europe the anabolic 
androgen, trenbolone acetate, is used to im­
prove the performance of beef heifers and 
steers. The mechanism of action of androgenic 
agents is not fully understood, but is thought to 
be a direct action on skeletal muscle cells, 
which, as mentioned previously, have androgen 
and glucocorticoid receptors. Methyl tren­
bolone, a derivative of trenbolone acetate, 
binds to the skeletal muscle androgen receptor 
(Tremblay et al., 1977). Thus, trenbolone ace­
tate and other androgens could act directly 
through the androgen receptor to increase pro­
tein synthesis. Another possibility is that andro­
genic agents compete for or "down regulate" 
muscle glucocorticoid receptors, and thereby 
reduce the catabolic effects of glucocorticoids 
on muscle protein metabolism (Mayer and 
Rosen, 1975, 1978). Studies by Vernon and 
Buttery (1976, 1978) suggest that in the rat, 
trenbolone acetate acts on skeletal muscle 
to reduce the rates of both synthesis and 
degradation of protein while increasing growth, 
efficiency of feed utilization and nitrogen 
retention. The resulting decrease in protein 
turnover could account for the observed im­
provement in efficiency of feed utilization. The 
increased nitrogen retention suggests a net 
change in protein metabolism favoring protein 
accretion.

Dichlorvos is an antiparasitic agent which im­

Poloxalene, a polyoxypropylene-polyoxy-

(Bartley et al., 1965), but is only partially

Bovine ketosis, acetonemia, is a major meta­
bolic problem in dairy cows which occurs pri­
marily during the first 6 wk of lactation, when 
milk production is maximal. During early lac­
tation, high-producing dairy cows are often in 
negative energy balance and the demand for 
glucose to meet body needs and milk lactose 
synthesis exceeds the capacity of the liver to 
synthesize glucose. The cow must metabolize 
body lipids to provide energy needs not sup­
plied through the feed and excessive amounts 
of ketones are produced. Under these condi­
tions both hypoglycemia and hyperketonemia 
may develop. The resulting illness, acetonemia, 
is characterized by such clinical symptoms as 
reduced feed consumption and milk produc­
tion. Propylene glycol, given orally, is quickly 
absorbed and taken up by the liver, where it 
serves as a substrate for gluconeogenesis. This 
glucose reduces the rate of ketone formation, 
allowing the cow to recover and, subsequently, 
milk production to improve (Sauer et al., 
1973). 
Thyroprotein (iodinated casein) is an orally 
active agent with thyroid hormone-like activity, 
cleared as a feed additive for dairy cows to in­
crease milk production. Thyroprotein feeding, 
initiated approximately 7 wk into lactation, 
causes a temporary increase in milk production 
which is maximal during the first 2 wk of treat­
ment. The effect on milk production gradually 
decreases and milk production over the entire 
lactation does not appear to be greatly altered. 
This temporary increase in milk production 
caused by thyroprotein is probably mediated 
through its effects on basal metabolic rate 
(Schmidt et al., 1971). 
Recent Studies on Exogenous Hormones 
and Livestock Production 
Growth Hormone. Unquestionably GH is 
required for normal animal growth. However, 
the value of providing normal, growing animals 
with additional GH has not been fully deter­
mined. Exogenous GH, given by injection, has 
been reported to improve the growth rate and 
feed conversion of normal, growing pigs (Mach-
lin, 1972), calves (Brumby, 1959) and lambs 
(Wagner and Veenhuizen, 1978) and to increase 
efficiency of milk production in dairy cows 
(Machlin, 1973; Peel et al., 1981). In addition, 
GH administration has been reported to shift 
carcass composition from fat toward protein 
and moisture (Machlin, 1972; Wagner and Veen­
huizen, 1978). However, because of the short­
age of purified GH, most studies did not use 
enough animals and(or) sufficient duration of 
treatment to define the magnitude of the re­
sponses to exogenous GH. In view of recent 
progress in producing GH through recombi­
nant DNA techniques, the need to accurately 
assess improvements in livestock performance 
due to exogenous GH treatment has become 
more important. 
Recently, Muir et al. (1983) concluded an 
8-wk growth trial to assess the effects of exo­
genous ovine GH (oGH; 7 mg/d sc) on growth 
performance and carcass composition of nor­
mal, growing lambs. Diethylstilbestrol (.1 
mg/d sc) treated and control lambs were in­
cluded for comparisons. The study involved 16 
wether lambs/treatment with four lambs/ 
treatment designated to provide blood samples 
to monitor hormonal changes during the trial.

at 15 mg/d for 98 to 112 d were 3.93 vs 6.19 
kg fat and 1.28 vs 1.02 kg protein for treated vs 
control sheep, respectively. Also, Machlin 
(1972) gave pigs exogenous GH at approxi­
mately 6 mg/d and reported that GH decreased 
the percentage of fat 35% and increased the 
percentage of protein 8% and moisture 10% in 
the ham. Thus, although GH administration 
appears to increase carcass protein, the greater 
effect is to reduce carcass fat. 
Recent studies by Peel et al. (1981) demon­
strated that exogenous bGH (44 mg/d sc) can 
increase milk production in high-producing 
dairy cows (34.4 kg milk/d) during an 11-d 
trial. Injections of bGH increased plasma GH 
from approximately 5 ng/ml during the pre-
injection period to an average of 40 ng/ml at 3 
h post-injection after the third injection. With 
five cows/treatment bGH increased (P<.05) 
milk secretion 9.5% with no significant effect 
on feed intake (—4.3%). Mammary secretion of 
fat, lactose and energy were increased (P<.01) 
22.7, 14.6 and  17.1%,  respectively, by exo­
genous bGH. However, mammary secretion of 
protein was not altered. Milk composition was 
shifted with an increase (P<.05) of 12.9% in fat 
percentage and a decrease (P<.05) of 4.6% in 
protein percentage. Energy balance data, col­
lected during the last 5 d of the pre-injection 
and the injection period, indicate that bGH did 
not alter the intake, fecal excretion, digestibil­
ity or urinary excretion of energy (table 19). 
Thus, with increased (P<.01) secretion of milk 
energy, DE balance was decreased (P<.01). 
Nitrogen balance data, collected during the 
same period, indicate that bGH did not signifi­
cantly alter intake, fecal excretion, absorption, 
urinary excretion or milk secretion of nitrogen 
(table 20).

It is well known that hypophysectomy pre­
vents normal protein accretion in the skeletal 
muscle of young animals, and that GH replace­
ment therapy restores this process through 
increased protein synthesis (Goldberg, 1969; 
Goldberg et al., 1980). The effects of GH on 
lipid metabolism are also well established 
(Goodman and Schwartz, 1974), but perhaps 
not as well recognized. Studies in vitro with rat 
adipocytes (Goodman and Coiro, 1981; Good­
man and Kostyo, 1981) and in vivo with sheep 
(Bassett and Wallace, 1966) have shown that 
GH has an immediate, short-term insulin-like 
effect on adipocytes which is followed by a 
prolonged, lipolytic response. In sheep the in­
sulin-like effect of oGH lasts about 1 h, during 
which time plasma glucose, FFA and ketones 
decline. The insulin-like effect is followed by a 
lipolytic response which lasts about 8 h. During 
this time plasma FFA, glucose and ketones are 
increased (Bassett and Wallace, 1966). Appar­
ently, GH produces adipose tissue refractori­
ness to the insulin-like effects of GH (Goodman 
and Coiro, 1981). Thus, the lipolytic effects of 
GH would be expected to predominate when 
GH is chronically elevated. This probably ac­
counts for the reduced carcass fat of lambs ob­
served by Muir et al. (1982) and the increased 
milk fat secretion by dairy cows (Peel et al., 
1981). 
Prolactin. The role of prolactin (Prl) in 
growth is not well established. Some literature 
suggests that Prl is associated with growth. Exo­
genous ovine Prl (oPrl) has been reported to in­
crease body weight gain of mice (Wallis and 
Dew, 1976) and rats (Reisfeld et al., 1961). 
Chronic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) 
injections have been reported to increase 
plasma Prl, feed intake and growth rate of 
wether lambs (Davis et al., 1976, 1977a) and 
heifers (Davis et al., 1977b; McGuffey et al., 
1977). Finally, extended daily photoperiod has 
been associated with increased plasma Prl, 
feed intake and rate of gain in lambs (Forbes 
et al, 1979) and heifers (Peters et al., 
1978).

Future of Production Improvers 
Animal production improvers are extremely 
important to both producers and consumers. 
For example, the value of a 10% feed savings to 
the U.S. feedlot, finisher hog and broiler in­
dustries is estimated to total 8.8 million t of 
feed and 1.3 billion dollar/yr based on 1979 
USDA data (table 25). If similar estimates are 
extended to all major livestock industries 
worldwide, the savings are enormous. With the 
population and the per capita consumption of 
livestock products both increasing while land 
available for feedstuff production is dwindling, 
the importance of animal production improvers 
will continue to increase. Therefore, safe, effec­
tive production improvers will remain an inte­
gral part of the livestock industries. 
Nevertheless, there are significant problems 
to be overcome. Since the introduction of anti­
biotics more than 25 yr ago, various groups 
have been concerned with the possible develop­
ment of antibiotic resistance by bacteria and 
transfer of these "resistent organisms" to man. 
The concerns have centered on 1) human health 
hazards, 2) animal health hazards, and 3) anti­
biotic effectiveness in animals. The facts remain 
that definitive data, concerning the effects of 
low-level feeding of antibiotics to livestock on 
human health, are not available. Animals fed 
low levels of antibiotics have remained respon­
sive to therapeutic levels of the same drug, and 
the growth and feed conversion responses from 
antibiotics have been documented to be the 
same today as they were 25 yr ago. Neverthe­
less, the FDA has proposed to ban subtherapeu­
tic use of penicillin and to "severely restrict" 
the low-level use of tetracyclines in animal 
feeds, pending the outcome of government-
funded studies now in progress. Animal scien­
tists should start now to develop new anti­
microbial agents which are specific for animal 
use and not cross-resistant with antibiotics used 
in human medicine.

to which animal science research should be
