Introduction
Silage is the predominant form of ruminant feed on
many farms during winter (Wilkinson 2005). It is the
product formed when forages of sufﬁciently high mois-
ture content and liable to spoilage by aerobic micro-
organisms are stored anaerobically (Whittenbury 1968).
Thus, once a crop is sealed from the air, plant and micro-
bial respiration consumes the trapped oxygen (McDonald
et al. 1991) and the subsequent anaerobic environment
terminates plant respiration, inhibits the growth of aero-
bic micro-organisms and creates favourable conditions for
the growth and development of epiphytic lactic acid
bacteria (Pitt et al. 1985), which convert sugars into
mainly lactic acid, thereby reducing pH (McDonald et al.
1991). The conditions created are inhibitory to the sub-
sequent activity of deleterious micro-organisms such as
Clostridia and Enterobacteria.
The two most commonly employed methods of ensil-
ing are conventional precision-chop silage stored in large
silos and individual bales of silage (300–800 kg) wrapped
in polythene stretch-ﬁlm (O’Kiely et al. 2000). Although
the principles controlling conservation and nutritive value
are the same for both systems, baled silage composition
frequently differs from that of comparable conventional
silage (Ohlsson 1998; Fychan et al. 2002). Consequently,
the environment created within the bale may be less
inhibitory to the activities of undesirable micro-organisms

Abstract
Aims: Grass silage is the product formed by a natural lactic acid bacterial fer-
mentation when grass is stored under anaerobic conditions, and represents an
important ruminant feedstuff on farms during winter. Of the two commonly
employed methods of ensiling forage, baled silage composition frequently dif-
fers from that of comparable precision-chop silage reﬂecting a different ensiling
environment. The aim of this study was to investigate the dynamics of the
silage fermentation in wilted grass and between ensiling systems.
Methods and Results: Fermentation dynamics were examined using traditional
methods of silage analyses, including microbial enumeration and analysis of
fermentation products, and culture-independent terminal restriction fragment
length polymorphism (T-RFLP). A successful fermentation was achieved in
both systems, with the fermentation (increase in lactic acid bacteria and lactic
acid concentration, decrease in pH) proceeding rapidly once the herbage was
ensiled.
Conclusions: Under controlled conditions, little difference in silage quality and
microbial composition were observed between ensiling systems and this was
further reﬂected in the T-RFLP community analysis.
Signiﬁcance and Impact of the Study: T-RFLP proved a potentially useful tool
to study the ensilage process and could provide valid support to traditional
methods, or a viable alternative to these methods, for investigating the dynam-
ics of the bacterial community over the course of the fermentation.

such as Clostridia, Enterobacteria, Bacilli, yeast and mould
(O’Brien et al. 2005; McEniry et al. 2006), leading to
losses in nutritive value and animal production potential.
Of the many factors that can affect the silage fermenta-
tion, the type of micro-organisms that dominate the pro-
cess often dictates the quality of the silage produced
(Ennahar et al. 2003). Hence, the ensilage process has
been investigated by measuring changes in the chemical
and microbiological composition of the forage while in
the silo. However, traditional, culture-based microbiology
assays can be biased by the growth characteristics of the
microbes studied and detect culturable cells only (Giraffa
and Neviani 2001). Furthermore, it has been estimated
that in microbial ecology studies considerably <10% of
the microbial diversity present is culturable and as a
result, the use of selective media can lead to a nonrepre-
sentative assessment of the extent of the microbial com-
munity structure (Liu et al. 1997; Ranjard et al. 2000).
Ampe et al. (1999) reported that this was also true for
fermented foods where most micro-organisms were
thought to be culturable.
Advances in molecular biology have led to the develop-
ment of DNA-based community proﬁling techniques [e.g.
denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE), auto-
mated ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis and terminal
restriction fragment length polymorphism (T-RFLP)] that
remove the biases associated with culturing. These tech-
niques have been widely applied across a range of systems
including soil (Kennedy et al. 2004), bioreactors (Fernan-
dez et al. 2000), salt marsh (Buchan et al. 2003), marine
plankton (Moeseneder et al. 1999) and cheese (Rade-
maker et al. 2005). However, few studies to date have
employed these approaches to study the ensilage process.
Brusetti et al. (2006) described the use of length hetero-
geneity PCR to monitor lactic acid bacteria succession
during the ensilage of maize. May et al. (2001) and Yang
et al. (2006) employed DGGE to investigate the fungal
communities in whole plant corn silage and the bacterial
communities in wheat straw silage, respectively. Rade-
maker et al. (2002) employed T-RFLP to demonstrate the
relative dominance of Lactobacillus-based silage inoculants
during ensiling.
This study describes the bacterial proﬁle of wilted
herbage ensiled as baled and precision-chop silage using
traditional culture-based techniques and culture-indepen-
dent T-RFLP combined with robust statistical analysis.
T-RFLP is a rapid and sensitive molecular approach that
can provide an insight into the structure of microbial
communities (Marsh 1999), combining selective PCR
ampliﬁcation of target genes with restriction enzyme
digestion, high resolution electrophoresis and ﬂuorescent
detection (Rademaker et al. 2006). This enables the char-
acterization of complex microbial communities and
comparative community analysis (Liu et al. 1997). The
results of this study may not only provide information
concerning the bacterial community dynamics of the
fermentation process but may also aid in our under-
standing of why the conservation characteristics of
individually wrapped bales often differ from those of
conventional silages.
Materials and methods
The forage treatments were organized in a 2 (ensiling
system) · 6 (stage of ensiling) factorial arrangement.
Herbage (303 ± 30Æ2 dry matter kg)1) was ensiled after a
24 h wilting period in cylindrical bales or as precision-
chopped grass in laboratory silos. Three replicates of each
treatment were sampled after 0, 2, 6, 14, 35 and 98 days
of ensiling. Speciﬁc bacteria and yeast were enumerated
and fermentation variables were assessed at each sampling
time. Total silage bacterial DNA was extracted and
T-RFLP was applied to the herbage samples to generate a
community ﬁngerprint.
Ensiling
An homogenous plot of Lolium perenne (inﬂorescence
emergence growth stage of the primary growth) was
mown, wilted in the ﬁeld for 24 h, following which rep-
resentative samples of the herbage were baled with a
round baler (Claas 250 Rotacut, chopping knifes disen-
gaged) into 15 1Æ2 m wide · 1Æ2 m diameter cylindrical
bales and wrapped in six layers of polythene stretch-ﬁlm
(750 mm wide, 0Æ025 mm thickness prestretching, 1Æ70
stretch). The wrapped bales were stored outdoors under
protection from wildlife. At the time of baling, 15 repre-
sentative samples of wilted herbage were obtained from
across the ﬁeld, from the same swaths used for baling.
These samples were precision-chopped (Mex VI, Pottin-
ger; 19 mm nominal chop length) and ensiled in units
of 4Æ7 kg chopped herbage in 15 plastic pipe silos
(height = 0Æ75 m, internal diameter = 0Æ152 m, internal
volume = 13Æ6 l; O’Kiely and Wilson 1991). Laboratory
silos were stored at a room temperature of 15/C176C prior
to sampling.
Grass and silage sampling
Three representative samples of herbage were taken after
ﬁeld wilting to characterize the grass prior to ensiling (day
0). Triplicate bales and laboratory silos were destructively
sampled after 2, 6, 14, 35 and 98 days ensilage. Bales were
aseptically sampled, at eight representative points around
the bale using an electrically powered cylindrical core bit
(length = 65 cm, internal diameter = 3Æ5 cm) and these

Herbage chemical analysis
Samples were dried at 40/C176C for 48 h and milled (Wiley
mill) through a 1 mm screen prior to analysis for water-
soluble carbohydrate concentration (WSC). Aqueous
extracts from samples were used to determine pH, and
lactic acid, volatile fatty acid (acetic acid, butyric acid and
propionic acid) ethanol and ammonia-N (NH3-N) con-
centrations, as described previously by McEniry et al.
(2006).
Herbage conventional microbiological analysis
Samples were processed for microbial enumeration within
3 h of sample collection. De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe
(Oxoid, CM 361) nystatin (100 000 U l)1; Sigma, N1638)
agar, violet red bile glucose (Oxoid, CM 485) agar,
reinforced Clostridia (Oxoid, CM151) agar and malt
extract (Oxoid, CM59) agar with both streptomycin
(100 mg ml)1; Sigma) and chloramphenicol (100 mg ml)1;
Sigma) were used for the enumeration of lactic acid
bacteria, Enterobacteria, Clostridia and yeast, respectively,
as described previously by McEniry et al. (2006). The
colony forming units (CFU) on each plate were enumer-
ated and the number of micro-organisms g)1 herbage
expressed as log10.
Total herbage DNA extraction and puriﬁcation
Total DNA was extracted from herbage samples based on
the method described by Brodie et al. (2002). Brieﬂy,
0Æ3 g of aseptically chopped grass or silage (0Æ5–1 mm
length) was added to a 2 ml polyethylene microcentrifuge
tube containing 0Æ5 g of sterile 0Æ1 mm glass beads and
0Æ5 g of sterile 0Æ5 mm zirconia beads, to which 0Æ5mlof
modiﬁed hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)
extraction buffer (equal volumes of 10% CTAB in
0Æ7 mol l)1 NaCl with 240 mmol l)1 potassium phosphate
buffer, pH 8Æ0) was added. After centrifugation at 3000 g
for 10 s, the tubes were incubated at 70/C176C for 10 min.
About 0Æ5 ml of phenol : chloroform : isoamylalcohol
(25 : 24 : 1) was then added to the tubes and the tubes
were shaken in a Hybaid Ribolyser (Savant FP120 Fast-
prep) at 5Æ5ms)1 for 30 s. Following bead-beating, herb-
age refuse and beads were removed by centrifugation at
14 000 g for 5 min at 4/C176C. The supernatant was removed
and extracted twice with chloroform : isoamylalcohol
(24 : 1). The resultant nucleic acids were puriﬁed using a
High Pure PCR Product Clean Up Kit according to the
manufacturer’s instructions (Roche Diagnostics GmbH,
Penzberg, Germany) and DNA was eluted in a ﬁnal vol-
ume of 50 ll. DNA purity was assessed by UV spectro-
photometry and DNA was consistently suitable for PCR
ampliﬁcation without further treatment.
Bacterial community ﬁngerprinting by T-RFLP analysis
To investigate bacterial community dynamics during fer-
mentation, samples from days 0, 2, 6, 14, 35 and 98 of
ensiling were analysed using T-RFLP. Terminal restriction
fragment (TRFs) lengths were determined using a modiﬁ-
cation of the method of Liu et al. (1997). The bacterial 16S
small subunit rRNA genes were ampliﬁed (PX2 Thermo-
Hybaid, Thermo Scientiﬁc) from extracted DNA using
primer set F27 (5¢-AGAGTTTGATC(C ⁄ A)TGGCTCAG-3¢)
and R1492 (5¢-TACGG(C ⁄ T)TACCTTGTTACGACT-3¢,
Lane 1991). The forward primer, F27, was labelled at the 5¢
end with a Beckman Coulter D4 ﬂuorescent dye.
PCR reactions were performed in 50 ll volumes contain-
ing 5 ll of the forward and reverse primers (0Æ3 lmol l)1
50 ll)1), 5 ll10· Mg-free PCR buffer (Sigma), 5 llof
bovine serum albumin, 2Æ5 ll MgCl2 (25 mmol l)1;
Sigma), 1 ll dNTPs (10 mmol l)1 of each; Sigma), 25Æ25 ll
distilled sterile H2O, 2Æ5UTaq DNA polymerase (Sigma)
and approx. 10 ng extracted DNA. Thermocycling condi-
tions were as follows: a hot start at 94/C176C for 3 min
(1 cycle); 94/C176C for 1 min, 55/C176C for 2 min, 72/C176C for 2 min
(26 cycles) and ﬁnal annealing at 72/C176C for 5 min. PCR
products were visualized on a 1% agarose gel and subse-
quently puriﬁed using a High Pure PCR Product Cleanup
Kit as per manufacturer’s instructions (Roche Diagnostics).
Puriﬁed PCR products were quantiﬁed on a 1Æ2% aga-
rose gel with an appropriate molecular weight marker.
Approximately 50 ng of each PCR product was digested
with 20 U of the restriction endonuclease MspI (New
England Biolabs Inc., Herts, UK) and 2Æ0 ll of the corre-
sponding 10· NEBuffer 2, in a ﬁnal volume of 20 ll, at
37/C176C for 12 h. Digested products were de-salted and 1 ll
aliquots were mixed with 38Æ75 ll of sample loading solu-
tion (GenomeLab, Beckman Coulter, Galway, Ireland)
and 0Æ25 ll of size standard 600 (GenomeLab, Beckman
Coulter).
Terminal restriction fragment lengths were determined
by electrophoresis using a Beckman Coulter (CEQ 8000,
Genetic Analyser System, Beckman Coulter, Galway, Ire-
land) automated sequencer, and fragment analysis was
performed using the Beckman Coulter fragment analysis
package v.4Æ0. Only fragments with ﬂuorescence >1% of
the total ﬂuorescence were included in the analyses. A

sequences in the prokaryotic 16S rRNA database.
Cloning and sequencing
Bacterial 16S rRNA replicate PCR products obtained
from baled silage on days 2 and 6 of ensiling were
pooled for each time point and subsequently puriﬁed
using a Wizard SV PCR clean-up system (Promega,
Madison, WI, USA). Puriﬁed PCR products were then
cloned into Escherichia coli JM109 cells using the pGem
T-Easy Vector Cloning Kit (Promega). Clones were
selected randomly, on the basis of blue-white screening,
and checked for correct insert size by vector-targeted
PCR [M13f (5¢-GTTTTCCCAGTCACGAC-3¢) and M13r
(5¢-CAGGAAACAGCTATGAC-3¢) primers; Promega]
and agarose gel electrophoresis. Positive clones were
digested as above, but with 20 U of endonucleases MspI
and HhaI (New England Biolabs Inc.) included. Clones
displaying different banding patterns were selected for
sequencing by single extension using an API 9730xl
DNA analyser (Macrogen, Korea). Sequence similarity
searches were performed in the NCBI GenBank data
library using the blast programme (http://www.
ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/) and putative identities were
assigned based on sequence similarities. The New
England BioLabs NEB cutter V2Æ0 on-line DNA sequenc-
ing tool (http://tools.neb.com/NEBcutter2/index.php) was
used to analyse sequences of interest for MspI restriction
sites.
Statistical analyses
Herbage chemical composition and microbial count data
were analysed by a two-way analysis of variance (anova)
for a 2 · 6 factorial arrangement of treatments using the
general linear models procedures of SAS, Version 8.2
(SAS 2000), and the Bonferroni t-test was used to com-
pare treatments.
Multivariate statistical analyses were performed on
T-RFLP community proﬁles using Primer Software
(Primer-E Ltd, Ivybridge, UK), Genstat Version 8.0 and
Canoco for MS-Windows version 4.5. Genstat was used to
calculate the overall abundance of each individual TRF for
all samples (and within individual ensiling systems) and to
perform a two-way factorial anova for effect of ensiling
system, stage of ensiling and their interaction on bacterial
ribotypes. The TRFs were subsequently ranked according
to overall abundance and assigned rankings were used to
select TRFs for inclusion in further multivariate analyses.
The gross similarity of ribotype proﬁles for each treat-
ment was examined using the Bray–Curtis index as a
measure of similarity. Bacterial community proﬁles were
analysed using Primer software, and used to create non-
metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) plots for the data
set. MDS is an ordination technique that represents the
relationship inherent in multivariate data sets and
attempts to preserve the ranked order of the similarity of
any two communities as an inverse function of the dis-
tance between the points representing those communities
on the plot (Kruskal 1964). Therefore, sample communi-
ties with the highest similarity in the data set are repre-
sented by points on the plot that are plotted closest
together, and those with the lowest similarity with points
furthest apart (Rooney et al. 2006). The degree to which
the plot matches the similarity matrix can be judged by
examining the stress value (Kruskal’s stress). The MDS
plot described here was used to visualize the relationship
between bacterial communities (as determined by ribo-
type proﬁles) in both ensiling systems over the course of
the silage fermentation. MDS was performed using 25
random starting conﬁgurations of sample points and a
two-dimensional solution is presented.
Redundancy analysis (RDA) using Canoco, was used to
analyse the T-RFLP data after initial analysis by detrended
correspondence analysis revealed that the data exhibited
a linear, rather than a unimodal, response to ensilage
variables (e.g. stage of ensiling, ensiling system, pH). The
resulting ordination biplot approximated the weighted
average of each species (i.e. relative abundance of each
ribotype) with respect to each of the ensilage variables. A
Monte Carlo permutation test based on 199 random
permutations was used to test the null hypothesis that
bacterial proﬁles were unrelated to ensilage variables.

An MDS ordination plot was used to compare and
visualize gross differences in bacterial community struc-
ture between ensiling systems over the course of the
fermentation, for the top 20 most abundant TRFs which
represented 89Æ6% of overall abundance (Fig. 2). Similar-
ity between samples is presented as distance measures on
an MDS ordination plot, so that ordinations are typically
interpreted based on the distance between ordinate
points. Thus, where sample points or treatments (e.g.
triplicates for a particular silage sampling time) appear
close together, these can be regarded as having a similar
bacterial community composition. The stress value (0Æ11)
indicated that the MDS ordination plot was a reliable
spatial representation of the differences within the data.
The largest difference in community composition typi-
cally occurred between day 0 samples and those from day
98. However, while the plot revealed a marked shift in
community structure over the course of the fermentation
(visualized as a left to right gradation across the plot),
there was relatively little difference between the two
ensiling systems. In both ensiling systems, community
structure changed after ensiling (from day 0) as the
fermentation progressed, remaining similar for days 2, 6,
14 and 35 of ensilage, but with a ﬁnal shift in the
community structure with extended storage to day 98.
While MDS analysis gives a broad view of treatment
effects on bacterial community composition, a further
multivariate statistical approach was carried out in order
to elucidate the effects of the main ensilage factors on
community composition. As the top 20 most abundant
TRFs accounted for the majority of the variance in the
data set only these TRFs were used for subsequent anal-
yses. Figure 3 shows a RDA ordination plot of the top
20 most abundant TRFs in relation to ensiling system,
stage of ensiling, the interaction between ensiling system
(system) and stage of ensiling (stage), lactic and acetic
acid concentrations, pH, NH3-N and WSC. Axes 1
(P <0Æ01) and 2 were found to explain 35Æ3% and 6Æ6%
of the overall variance within the TRF data, respectively,
accounting for 42% of the total variance. The cumula-
tive species–environment relation for axes 1 and 2 was
85%, indicating that these axes accounted for the bulk
of the variance in the TRF data that could be attributed
to ensilage factors (e.g. stage of ensiling, ensiling system

previously. Several trends indicated by the RDA ordination

signiﬁcant positive effect on TRF 574 and a signiﬁcant

Discussion
Conventional indices of silage fermentation
A successful silage fermentation is a complex solid state
process whereby a minority community of lactic acid bac-
teria should become the predominant microbial ﬂora
(Daeschel et al. 1987). The fermentation is not a fully
controlled process under farm conditions and hence most
components of a planned ensilage contribute to stacking
the probabilities in favour of a lactic acid dominant
fermentation (O’Kiely and Muck 1998). As conditions
become favourable in the silo or bale, lactic acid bacteria
become dominant and produce acid (mainly lactic acid)
until the available sugar is exhausted or until the pH
becomes unfavourable for further growth (Langston and
Bouma 1960a; Pahlow et al. 2003).
In the current experiment, a successful lactic acid dom-
inant fermentation was achieved with both baled and pre-
cision-chop silage systems. Silage fermentation proceeded
rapidly in both ensiling systems once the herbage was
ensiled, with the greatest increase in fermentation prod-
ucts and consequently the greatest decrease in silage pH
occurring by day 2 of ensilage. At this stage of ensiling, it
is likely that anaerobic conditions prevailed in both the
bales and laboratory silos, and that plant cells had lysed
with the liberation of plant juices promoting the rapid
development of lactic acid bacteria (Greenhill 1964; Seale
et al. 1982). With the development of a lactic acid bacte-
rial fermentation an increase in lactic and acetic acid
concentrations, and a decrease in herbage WSC and pH.
Epiphytic Enterobacteria and yeast species could also
beneﬁt from the increase in readily available substrate
after ensiling and this was indicated with their numbers
peaking at day 2 of ensilage. However, the rapid develop-
ment of lactic acid bacteria and the decreasing pH
proved inhibitory to the activity of the latter undesirable

Langston et al. (1962) found that Streptococcus sp., Leuco-
nostoc sp. and Gram-negative bacteria are prominent in
the early stages of ensiling. Silages undergoing a desirable
fermentation then generally demonstrate a typical
sequence in that the latter groups give way to homofer-
mentative Lactobacilli and Pediococci sp. Heterofermenta-
tive acid-tolerant Lactobacilli sp. ﬁnally dominate under
low pH conditions (Seale 1986). However, traditional cul-
ture-based methods can prove laborious, time-consuming
and they need to be performed shortly after sampling
(Rademaker et al. 2006). Of greater importance is the fact
that these methods generally only enumerate a small
proportion of total micro-organisms (relative to some
molecular techniques), with the consequent risk of the
micro-organisms identiﬁed not being representative of the
total bacterial population (Giraffa and Neviani 2001).
This inability to cultivate more than 90% of the members
of many communities shows the need for culture-inde-
pendent analysis (Hugenholtz et al. 1998).
Although some molecular-based methods have been
applied to study different aspects of the ensilage process,
to the best of the author’s knowledge, the current study is
one of the ﬁrst applications of a sophisticated molecular
community approach to study bacterial community
dynamics during a grass silage fermentation. This study
has demonstrated that it is possible to follow the changes
in bacterial community composition over the course of
the fermentation using culture-independent T-RFLP.
Apart from monitoring the presence or absence of mem-
bers of the population, shifts in the relative presence of
members at different time points during ensiling could be
followed (Osborn et al. 2000). T-RFLP is a rapid and
sensitive method ideal for characterization and compari-
son of microbial community structure, and has a number
of advantages over other techniques including high
resolution, a digital output and the ability to reference a
database allowing tentative identiﬁcation (Marsh 1999;
Rademaker et al. 2005).
The substantial change in bacterial community compo-
sition during ensiling was evident on the MDS ordination
plot, with a left-to-right gradation across the plot regard-
less of ensiling system. This coincided with the develop-
ment of the lactic acid bacterial population once
anaerobic conditions were established and substrate
became available, resulting in changes in pH, fermenta-
tion products and WSC. These vast changes in the envi-
ronment within the silos and bales probably resulted in
the inhibition of undesirable aerobic and anaerobic
micro-organisms with all these factors contributing to
determining community composition. With extended
storage (day 98), a ﬁnal shift in community structure
was observed corresponding to the end of the primary
fermentation and a decline in the numbers of the micro-
organisms responsible for that fermentation under acidic
conditions.
A total of 78 distinct TRFs were detected across all
herbage samples, with TRF 554 representing 29Æ3% of the
abundance. These results were further supported by a
total of 58 distinct TRFs being detected for the same
samples when restriction endonuclease HhaI was used to
generate a T-RFLP proﬁle (data not shown). Within the
HhaI T-RFLP proﬁle the dominant fragment represented
28Æ6% of the total abundance. In general, T-RFLP data
reﬂected the changes in microbial numbers, measured by
plate counting, during ensiling. As numbers of lactic acid
bacteria increased rapidly after ensiling so too did the
relative abundance of TRF 554, and to a lesser extent
TRFs 577, 581, 580 and 586 (all putatively identiﬁed as
lactic acid bacteria). The numbers of lactic acid bacteria,
as measured by the standard plate count, peaked on day
14 of ensiling and then gradually declined under acidic
conditions, and this was generally reﬂected in the changes
in the relative abundance of TRF 554, in particular. It is
possible that the most abundant TRF (TRF 554) could
represent a number of related lactic acid bacteria species,
with the species clustering due to the same restriction site
(Buchan et al. 2003). The decline in Enterobacteria num-
bers with decreasing pH could also be reﬂected in the
decrease in the abundance of TRF 493. TRF 493 was the
dominant ribotype at the onset of ensiling but declined
over the course of the fermentation and was not detected
after 98 days. In addition, some TRFs such as TRF 574
and 584 only appeared late in the storage period when
acid conditions prevailed and possibly reﬂects the consen-
sus that acid-tolerant Lactobacilli sp. ﬁnally dominate
under low pH conditions (Seale 1986). This is in agree-
ment with Rademaker et al. (2006) who reported that
T-RFLP analysis of yoghurt was partly supported by plate
counting. Cocolin et al. (2000) also reported an excellent
agreement between plating data and the presence or
absence of a cognate DGGE band when proﬁling yeast
dynamics in wine fermentations.
The general changes in T-RFLP proﬁles were reﬂected
in the cloning data where the bacteria identiﬁed from day
2 and day 6 of ensilage had terminal fragments of 555,
576, 578 and 612 bp, respectively. The distribution of
strains of Lactobacillus sp. and Leuconostoc sp., identiﬁed
by sequencing, in fermenting plant material has been
well-documented (Daeschel et al. 1987; Pahlow et al.
2003). The presence of Leuconostoc sp. in the early stages
of ensilage was previously reported by Langston et al.
(1962). Furthermore, Ennahar et al. (2003) reported that
most of the lactic acid bacteria characterized from paddy
rice silage belonged to the genera Lactobacillus, Lactococ-
cus and Leuconostoc. While this study presents a broad

Although T-RFLP has great potential in microbial com-
munity analysis, as with any community ﬁngerprinting
method problems exist in the introduction of experimen-
tal biases and the interpretation of the complex data
generated. Community ﬁngerprints generated from this
method must be interpreted with caution, because of
species differences in rRNA gene copy number, biases
resulting from extraction and PCR ampliﬁcation, and the
difﬁculty in standardizing the amount of DNA analysed
in each replicate (Crosby and Criddle 2003; Egert and
Friedrich 2003). However, as all samples in this study
were subject to the same biases throughout all experi-
mental procedures it is still possible to compare between
samples on a relative basis. Furthermore, data generated
in this study were analysed and interpreted using a
number of robust multivariate approaches to explore the
relationship between community structure and ensilage
parameters.
While RDA revealed that no one environmental ⁄ ensi-
lage factor alone inﬂuenced the bacterial community,
stage of ensiling (based on anova) appeared to be the
dominant factor affecting bacterial community structure.
This is probably because, overall, it represents the signiﬁ-
cant chemical changes occurring in the silo during
fermentation. Numerous TRFs were negatively correlated
with increasing lactic acid concentration and decreasing
pH, perhaps being indicative of the decrease in acid sensi-
tive, undesirable aerobic and anaerobic micro-organisms,
with the development of lactic acid bacteria. For example,
TRFs 493, 480, 500 and 520 were all negatively correlated
with increasing lactic acid concentration and decreasing
pH. In contrast, TRFs 574, 586 and 584 were all positively
correlated with stage of ensiling, with their abundances
generally increasing as the ensilage progressed, reﬂecting
the changes in the microbial community in response to
chemical changes in the environment in the silo or bale.
With the exception of four TRFs (TRF 580, 586, 581
and 75), ensiling system had little effect on the overall
community composition, although small differences in
the most abundant fragments were detected. TRF 493 was
more abundant in the baled silage system and TRF 574
became the dominant TRF after 98 days ensilage in the
precision-chop silage. Tentatively, taking TRF 493 to
represent Enterobacteria sp., this is in accord with
McEniry et al. (2007) who reported that the decrease in
Enterobacteria numbers in baled silage is slower than that
in precision-chop silage. The dominance of TRF 574 at
the end of ensilage could agree with Seale (1986) who
suggested that heterofermentative acid-tolerant Lacto-
bacilli sp. dominate the ﬁnal stages of ensiling.
In this experiment, good quality silages were produced
under controlled conditions and little difference in silage
quality and microbial composition were observed between
ensilage systems. T-RFLP proved a potentially useful tool
to study the ensilage process and for following the
dynamics of bacterial community changes over the course
of the fermentation, and generally agreed with ﬁndings
from conventional analyses. In such a copiotrophic envi-
ronment, where a restricted group of micro-organisms
are responsible for community changes, it is possible that
culture-based approaches are adequate tools to study the
changes in microbial composition. However, the use of
culture-independent approaches could potentially result
in the analysis of a greater extent of the community (e.g.
uncultured Lactobacillus sp. were detected by cloning).
Allied to this the range of methods available and the abil-
ity to target different microbial groups using speciﬁc
primers, shows the great potential of DNA-based methods
in the analysis of complex microbial systems. Further-
more, a signiﬁcant beneﬁt of the T-RFLP method is that
an overview of the whole bacterial population can be
obtained in one assay. As a result, T-RFLP offers a new
method to study the ensilage process and offers a viable
alternative to culture-dependent methods.
