Abstract
Overseeding cool-season annual legumes into bermudagrass 
[Cynodon dactylon (L.) Pers.] offers an economical solution to pas-
ture shortage experienced by beef cattle (Bos spp.) farmers during 
winter on the southeastern Coastal Plain of the USA. Using Austrian 
winter peas [ Pisum sativum var. arvense  (L.) Poir.], ‘Dixie’ crimson 
clover (Trifolium incarnatum L.), ‘Yucchi’ and ‘Apache’ arrowleaf clo-
ver ( T. vesiculosum  Savi), and ‘AU Merit’ hairy vetch (Vicia villosa 
Roth.) in replicated complete blocks (four replicates in each of two 
growing seasons), our objectives were to (i) determine the nutritive 
value of forage provided by several legumes as they mature; (ii) 
evaluate legume impact on the nutritive value of subsequent ber-
mudagrass hay harvests; (iii) obtain estimates of the quantity of N 
they potentially add to the system; and (iv) evaluate the preferences 
of grazing cattle for the various legumes. All legumes improved 
early-spring dry matter (DM) yield compared with control plots 
without overseeding (3310 vs. 1120 lb/acre for legumes and con-
trol; P < 0.01). Legumes increased the crude protein (CP) content 
of bermudagrass hay harvested during the subsequent growing 
season (14.1, 14.8, 13.7, 14.8, and 12.0% for crimson, vetch, peas, 
arrowleaf, and control, respectively; P < 0.01); however, peas and 
arrowleaf reduced hay DM yield (P = 0.04). Across species, legumes 
added approximately 89 lb/acre N to the system by fixation, and 
overseeding offered a potential addition of 64 grazing days/acre 
(1200-lb cows). Legumes improved the potential removal of both N 
and P from the system, and cattle readily consumed the legumes. 
Overseeding may have the potential to reduce N fertilization and 
lengthen grazing season; however, with added seed costs, it may 
not improve profitability.

ways to feed their cattle so they can maintain a profit mar-
gin. One feed that has received increased attention is pasture.

of the USA, where sandy soils are not conducive to growing 
tall fescue ([Schedonorus arundinaceus (Schreb.) Dumort. nom. 
cons.]; Hannaway et al., 2009) or other cool-season perennial 
grasses can be a challenge. Bermudagrass, a warm-season 
perennial grass, often constitutes the majority of pastures

One method for shortening the duration of pasture 
scarcity is overseeding cool-season annual grasses into 
the bermudagrass sod (Hoveland et al., 1978; Hill et al., 
1985). Overseeding with cool-season annual legumes 
would also offer the potential benefit of reducing the 
need for chemical N sources (Evers, 1985). Modeling 
has suggested that crimson clover can supply the 
equivalent of 107 lb/acre N when overseeded into ber-
mudagrass and that clover growth reduces the winter 
feeding period for stored forage (Overman et al., 1992). 
Rao et al. (2007) demonstrated both improved forage 
quality and increased duration of forage availability 
with legumes overseeded into bermudagrass pad-
docks. Mullen et al. (2000) found improved forage 
quality when clover was overseeded into bermudag-
rass compared with a bermudagrass monoculture and 
suggested that overseeding might be an efficient way 
to reduce the need for inorganic N application.
There are numerous legumes that thrive in the coastal 
plain available to beef farmers (Chamblee and Mueller, 
1999). Using Austrian winter peas, Dixie crimson clo-
ver, Yuchi and Apache arrowleaf clover, and AU Merit 
hairy vetch, our objectives were to (i) determine the 
nutritive value of forage provided by the legumes as 
they mature; (ii) evaluate legume impact on the nutri-
tive value of subsequent bermudagrass hay harvests; 
(iii) obtain estimates of the quantity of N added to the 
system; and (iv) evaluate the preferences of cattle for 
the various legumes.
Site, Legumes, and Data Collection  
and Analysis
This research was conducted over the course of two 
growing seasons (2009–2010 and 2010–2011) in three 
seeded bermudagrass pastures at Cherry Research 
Farm (Goldsboro, NC; 35°22¢41.7² N, 78°2¢44.13 ² W; 75 
feet above sea level). One pasture had ‘Cheyenne’ and 
one had ‘Ranchero Frio’ in the first growing season. 
Both cultivars yielded similar results (P > 0.20), so the 
overall means are presented. The pasture used in the 
second growing season was ‘Cheyenne’. The establish-
ment methods for the bermudagrass and legumes have 
previously been described in detail (Freeman et al., 2014). 
As a reminder, crimson clover was seeded at the rate of 
15 lb pure live seed (PLS)/acre, arrowleaf clover at 10 lb 
PLS/acre, vetch at 20 lb PLS/acre, and winter peas at 12 
lb PLS/acre. The soil was Johns sandy loam or Lakeland 
sand with the division between soil types bisecting the 
field into halves so that each type was equally repre-
sented across replicates. Plots (10 ft wide separated by 
5-ft buffers) were 200 ft long in the 2009–2010 growing 
season or 155 ft long in the 2010–2011 growing season 
and were randomly assigned to legume treatments in 
a complete block design with four replicates per year. 
Replicates were subdivided into sections by temporary 
fences for sequential grazing (preliminary or sequence 
1, 2, or 3) and sequencing order was randomly assigned 
(Fig. 1). Grazing sequence was considered the experi-
mental unit.
When the legumes reached approximately 8 inches in 
height (mid-April), preliminary areas were grazed off 
by available cattle (25 developing heifers, 838 ± 44 lb, 
in 2010, or 9 steers, 750 ± 61 lb, in 2011). No measure-
ments were made during preliminary grazing, and 
animals remained in the preliminary section of the 
pasture until the residual forage was about 3 inches tall. 
Sequence 1 plots were grazed 2 weeks later, with the 
cattle being allowed to graze one of the four replicates 
per day across 4 days (Tuesday to Friday). Cattle were 
moved from replicate to replicate at 0800 each morning 
and allowed to choose between legumes within matu-
rity area. They had free-choice access to water and 
trace mineralized salt. Observations of the number of 
cattle grazing in each legume treatment were made at 
15-min intervals for the first 2 h and then 30-min inter-
vals for the third hour of each day to determine animal 
preference for the legume treatments. Sequences 2 and 
3 followed at 2-week intervals.
After Sequence 3, the bermudagrass in the entire pas-
ture area was fertilized with 40 lb/acre N, allowed 
to grow to 12 to 15 inches, and then harvested as 
hay. Nitrogen was applied at the same rate after hay 
harvest, and regrowth was harvested as often as the

On Monday, before grazing each maturity, forage from 
five randomly selected locations (2.7 ft2) in each legume 
treatment strip was clipped to 2 inches stubble height, 
pooled, weighed, and subsampled. Subsamples for cal-
culation of available forage mass were weighed, dried 
(48 h at 140°F), and reweighed, and DM was used to 
calculate forage mass per acre. Subsamples for nutrient 
analysis were stored in zippered plastic bags, placed 
immediately on ice, and transported to the North 
Carolina Department of Agriculture and Consumer 
Services forage testing laboratory (Raleigh, NC), where 
they were analyzed for DM according to Shreve et al. 
(2006; NFTA procedure 2.1.4), neutral detergent fiber 
(NDF) according to Van Soest et al. (1991) modified for 
Ankom Method 13, acid detergent fiber (ADF, Ankom 
Method 12), total digestible nutrients (TDN), and min-
eral concentrations (AOAC, 2010).
At the peak of legume growth in each growing sea-
son (Sequence 2), samples of legumes were harvested 
along with broadleaf weed samples from control strips. 
Samples were dried and ground (1 mm, Cyclotec 1093 
mill, Foss Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden), and 0.1 g was 
packaged in tinfoil cups (LECO #502–186–200, St. Joseph, 
MI). The foil cups were shipped to the University of 
California at Davis Analytical Laboratory for 15N deter-
mination using an elemental analyzer (PDZ Europa 
ANCA-GSL, Dayton, OH) interfaced with a continu-
ous flow isotope ratio mass spectrophotometer (Sercon, 
Cheshire, UK). The percentage of N resulting from fixa-
tion was calculated by the natural abundance method 
(Unkovich et al., 1997). This method assumes that the 
15N level in leguminous forage is between the atmo-
spheric and soil 15N levels since both sources contribute

All data were analyzed with the mixed procedure of 
SAS (version 9.2; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) with replicate 
within year as the random effect and, where applicable, 
hay cutting number or grazing time interval as repeated 
effects. Main effects were legume treatment, grazing 
sequence, and their interaction. When the main effects 
were significant (P £ 0.05), means were separated and 
considered different when Pr > |t| £ 0.05. Apache and 
Yuchi arrowleaf clovers gave similar results (P > 0.20) 
for all parameters reported and will be presented as 
means (arrowleaf).
Forage Yield, Nutritive Value,  
and Nutrient Harvest
Legumes
All the legumes grew well, resulting in legume 
plots yielding more DM than controls. At Sequence 
2, legume yield was similar between treatments (P > 
0.20) and averaged 3853 lb/acre compared with 1395 lb/
acre for controls (SEM = 298). Legume yield was also 
greater than controls across maturities (3310 vs.1120 
lb/acre for legumes and control, respectively; P < 0.01). 
Similar legume yields were described by Chamblee 
and Mueller (1999). Assuming a standard cow of 1200 
lb, daily DM requirements per head would be 30 lb 
(2.5% BW). At peak forage DM production, there would 
have been 64 animal grazing days per acre of available 
forage about 4 weeks before the bermudagrass was 
ready to harvest, assuming a 50% forage utilization. 
Extension estimates of 4–12 weeks are found in the lit-
erature (Knight, 1970; Hoveland et al., 1978). Extending 
the grazing season usually results in a reduced need 
for feeding hay and in lower feed costs. Whether a 
producer purchases all of their hay or makes it from 
surplus forage on location, hay feeding represents a 
significant cost. Estimates from farms using stockpiled 
forage to replace hay suggest savings of $1 per head per 
day in the grazing system (Poore, 2013); however, when 
seeding costs are considered, savings from overseed-
ing may be less than with stockpiled forage.

TDN for 1200-lb lactating beef cows with moderate 
milk production. Forage from all treatments would 
meet these needs (Table 1); however, forage in the con-
trol strips was largely winter annual weeds—henbit

Sequence × treatment interactions were significant (P 
< 0.01, Table 1). The interaction of these main effects is 
probably due to the variation in rate of maturation and 
senescence between legume treatments. Crimson clo-
ver matured the most quickly, had begun to set flowers 
by the onset of Sequence 1, and was blooming dur-
ing Sequence 2. Nutritive value declined during this 
same period and continued downward at Sequence 3, 
as indicated by decreases in both CP and TDN and an 
increase in ADF. Arrowleaf clover followed a similar 
pattern; however, it did not reach bloom until Sequence 
3. Winter peas were still slower to bloom, resulting in 
similar CP at Sequences 1 and 2 followed by reduced 
CP at Sequence 3. Despite the reduction in CP concen-
tration and increase in ADF concentration between 
Sequences 1 and 2, winter peas maintained their TDN 
concentration over the course of the harvest period. 
Vetch maintained its CP concentration over the course 
of grazing; however, its TDN began declining after 
Sequence 2 and was accompanied by an increase in 
ADF. The forage available for grazing in the control 
plots followed the same pattern as the vetch. This simi-
larity may have been the result of the combination of 
the weeds senescing while the bermudagrass began to 
break dormancy.

Cumulative bermudagrass hay harvested from the 
plots averaged 5832 lb per acre per year (Table 2) and 
was reduced by both vetch (22%) and arrowleaf clover 
(25%) compared with the control. Muir and Bow (2011) 
reported reduction in hay DM as a result of overseeding. 
The nutrient content of all hay harvested was adequate 
to meet the needs of lactating beef cows; however, over-
seeding legumes increased the CP content of the hay 
over that of control (P < 0.01). There was no harvest × 
treatment interaction.

the control (P < 0.05). McLaughlin et al. (2005) reported 
that controls ranked lowest in DM and nutrient yields 
than other treatments but the differences were not sig-
nificant. Muir and Bow (2011) found improved N yield 
but no consistent effect on P yield when legumes were 
seeded into bermudagrass. Results are inconsistent 
and probably vary with the growing season and cul-
tural practices applied.
Contribution	of	Nitrogen	Fixation
One potential advantage of using overseeded legumes 
as opposed to non-N-fixing species is the capture 
of atmospheric N to replace chemical N fertilizer. 
Legumes in this trial captured similar proportions of 
N by fixation (86, 79, 86, and 89% for crimson, vetch, 
peas, and arrowleaf, respectively; P = 0.13) and fixed 
similar quantities of N (Fig. 2). All quantities fixed were 
within the range given by Sarrantonio (2007), and the 
proportions of fixed N were similar to those reported 
by Peoples et al. (2001). Since the CP concentration of 
hay harvested from legume plots was greater than that 
of hay from the control, it may have been possible to 
reduce N applications following legume overseeding 
and obtain comparable quality grass hay. Such a reduc-
tion might improve farm profitability. Biermacher et 
al. (2012) and Smith et al. (2014) conducted economic 
analyses of overseeding legumes into bermudagrass. 
Both sets of researchers found that the cost of N fer-
tilizer would have to increase greatly for legumes to 
have an economic advantage over traditional applica-
tions of N fertilizer. Biermacher et al. (2012) pointed out 
that legume seed tends to increase in cost as fertilizer 
cost rises, so that it is difficult for legumes to become 
cost effective. The ability of the legumes to reseed was 
not mentioned in either paper. Choosing a legume 
with good reseeding capability and managing the

Animal Preferences for Legumes
The cattle readily consumed all the legumes while 
they were vegetative (Fig. 3). Arrowleaf clover was 
the preferred forage, and a greater proportion of 
the cattle were observed in arrowleaf strips than in 
other treatments across time and grazing sequence 
(P < 0.01). Once the legumes began to flower and set 
seed, however, stronger differences in grazing devel-
oped, especially an avoidance of crimson, which was 
in full bloom by Sequence 2 (26 Apr. 2010 and 9 May 
2011). The proportion of cattle observed in the crimson 
strips declined between Sequences 1 and 2 (8.7 and 
3.3%) while the proportion of cattle observed in peas 
increased (6.9 and 13.0% for Sequences 1 and 2; P < 0.01). 
Proportions remained constant for the other treat-
ments and between Sequences 2 and 3.
As the favorite, arrowleaf clover, was consumed 
and became unavailable, the proportion of animals 
selecting less favored species increased during the 
observation period (Fig. 4). A large proportion of the 
animals ceased grazing and began ruminating between 
the 150- and 180-min observations. At the end of each 
24-h grazing period, most forage in all the strips was 
largely consumed, with the exception of crimson after 
it had begun to set seed (Fig. 5), suggesting that any of 
the legumes tested would be a viable source of forage if 
managed properly.
Implications for Producers
Winter annual legumes overseeded into bermu-
dagrass produced forage that was very palatable to 
cattle and provided high-quality feed. Overseeding 
resulted in greater DM production from the system, 
which could have provided as much as 9 weeks of 
additional grazing or which could have been har-
vested as high-quality hay to remove nutrients from 
the fields. Nitrogen fixation resulted in an average 
addition across legume species of about 89 lb/acre N 
to the pastures that could potentially reduce the quan-
tity of commercial N fertilizer needed. Depending on 
legume seed prices and the cost of N fertilizer, the 
potential reduction in fertilizer application coupled 
with potential savings from grazing as opposed to 
feeding hay could result in improved profit margins 
for beef producers who adopt this practice.
