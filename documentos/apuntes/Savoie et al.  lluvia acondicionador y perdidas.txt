Loss AND DRYING CHARACTERISTICS OF FORAGE 
MATS AFTER RAINFALL 
p. Savoie, R. Chabot, D. Tremblay 
MEMBER STUDENT MEMBER MEMBER 
ASAE ASAE ASAE 
ABSTRACT, Forage mats were made in the field with a mower-macerator prototype and subjected to 6 mm of rainfall. 
Drying coefficients of mats were 111% and 71% greater than drying coefficients of conventionally conditioned windrows 
before and after rain, respectively. Dry matter losses were estimated on the basis of the change in acid detergent fiber 
concentration during drying. After rain, losses were equal in timothy mats and timothy windrows (8%) but were higher in 
alfalfa mats (20%) than in alfalfa windrows (13%). When no rain occurred, mats had less respiration loss (3%) than 
windrows (7%). Keywords. Alfalfa, Timothy, Windrows, Conditioning, Harvest. 
Field losses during hay making have been estimated to vary between 18% and 30% of initial dry matter (Rees, 1982). The three main mechanisms of forage field losses are fragmentation, leaching after 
rainfall and respiration. Weather, crop species, and the type 
of mechanical treatment used to handle hay dictate the 
proportion of each type of loss. In general, however, losses 
are roughly proportional to the amount of time the forage 
remains in the field; Gabon (1982) estimated average daily 
losses of 4 to 5% in alfalfa and clover. A logical way to 
reduce field losses is to reduce the amount of time the 
forage remains in the field. 
Conventional methods of mowing-conditioning and 
leaving a wide swath in the field require three to four days 
of good drying weather (Savoie et al., 1984). This time is 
required for hay to reach a low enough moisture content, 
below 20% on a wet basis, for baling and safe storage 
without the risk of spoilage due to fungal growth 
(Rotz et al., 1990). To reduce the field drying time, several 
machines that manipulate windrows have been proposed: 
hay tedders, windrow inverters, rakes. However, these 
machines have two disadvantages: they mechanically 
impact the forage which may cause substantial leaf loss 
due to shatter, and they represent additional costs for labor, 
tractor, and machine use. Rotz and Savoie (1991) estimated 
that, at least for alfalfa, windrow manipulation could save 
several hours of drying and reduce respiration losses, 
although it sometimes increased mechanical losses.

Meanwhile, forage mat-making promises to sub­
stantially reduce the field drying time without requiring 
additional passes in the field between mowing and pickup. 
Also known as forage superconditioning, mat-making is a 
technology that integrates intensive conditioning at 
mowing. It involves macerating the freshly-cut forage and 
compressing it into a thin mat before it is deposited on the 
stubble (Koegel et al., 1988). Initial data on alfalfa mats 
indicated drying rates twice or even three times as high as 
drying rates of conventional windrows. Alfalfa mats dried 
to baling moisture within a 6-h period under good weather 
conditions (Shinners et al., 1987a). 
Although mat making considerably reduces the drying 
period, concerns have been raised about the fragility of the 
mat and its susceptibility to loss if rainfall occurs. 
Rotz et al. (1991) estimated that loss in conventional 
windrows was about 0.1% dry matter per millimeter of 
rainfall; dry matter loss in alfalfa mats made in the 
laboratory with a stationary macerator-press was three 
times higher, about 0.3%/mm of rain. These authors also 
measured an increase of the fiber content after rainfall due 
to leaching of more soluble nutrients. 
Forage mat-makers have not yet been marketed 
commercially, in part because of the need to simplify the 
mechanisms and to improve the flexibility of the machine 
to operate in more types of crops. Shinners et al. (1992) 
reported on a new large-scale prototype that includes a 
simple impact macerator and a beltless press. Savoie et al. 
(1993) also built a large scale prototype evaluated in both 
alfalfa and timothy grass. As discussed above, another 
hurdle that needs to be crossed is better understanding of 
mat response to unfavorable weather conditions. 
The objective of the present study was to evaluate the 
loss and redrying characteristics of forage mats after 
rainfall. Specific objectives were to: 
• Measure drying rates in mats and conventional 
windrows with and without rain. 
• Measure rain absorption. 
• Estimate dry matter and nutrient loss with and 
without rain.

METHODS 
FIELD CONDITIONING TREATMENTS 
Established alfalfa and timothy stands at the 
Deschambault Research Station, Quebec, were mowed 
during the first growth cycle in 1992. Mowing was done 
either with a commercial 3.0-m-wide disk mower with a 
rubber roll conditioner (New Holland model 411) or with a 
2.1-m-wide experimental mower mat-maker described in 
detail by Savoie et al. (1993). The first machine produced 
conventional fluffy windrows; the second machine 
produced macerated and compressed mats. 
Mowing was done during the weeks of 1 June, 8 June, 
and 15 June 1992, and repeated twice within each week on 
non-rainy days. On each of the six dates, mowing started 
around 9:00 A.M. with either the conventional mower or the 
mat maker. Machine order was chosen randomly. Each 
machine mowed and conditioned four adjacent 150-m long 
strips of alfalfa or timothy each mowing day. Crop order 
was also randomly selected. Samples of the fresh crop were 
taken at the time of mowing to estimate initial moisture 
content by oven drying at 60° C for 72 h (ASAE, 1993, 
Standard S358.2). 
Around 1:00 P.M. of the same day, four samples of 
alfalfa mats, four samples of alfalfa windrows, four 
samples of timothy mats, and four samples of timothy 
windrows were lifted gently, placed in wire mesh trays, 
and brought to the laboratory. The trays made of 25-mm 
wire mesh measured 0.45 m x 0.35 m with sides 0.15-m 
high; a fine screen was placed at the bottom to retain small 
particles. 
SIMULATED RAINFALL AND DRYING TREATMENTS 
In the laboratory, half the trays (two of each collected 
treatment) were subjected to two artificial rainfalls of 
3 mm each falling at a rate of 18 mm/h. Rainfall was 
applied manually with a sprayer; a calibrated quantity of 
water was sprayed uniformly over eight trays in 10 min. 
The first rainfall was applied at 3:00 P.M. the first day and 
the second rainfall was applied at 8:00 A.M. the second day. 
The proportion of rainfall retained or absorbed was 
measured by weighing trays immediately before and 
immediately after the rainfall treatment. All 16 trays were 
kept uncovered in a shaded area where evaporation could 
occur at a low rate (equivalent to 0.07 mm pan water 
evaporation/h) overnight until they were placed in a drying 
environment after both rainfalls. 
After the second simulated rainfall, the 16 trays were 
split in two groups of same treatments and placed in two 
drying environments. Environment number 1 was similar 
to the wind tunnel used by Savoie and Beauregard (1991). 
Air flowed over eight trays at an average air velocity of 
0.7 m/s; radiation from infrared lamps impinged the trays 
at the rate of 125 W/m^ (measured with a LI 200SB 
pyranometer sensor, Li-Cor, Inc.). Environment number 1 
provided energy at the surface of the forage equivalent to a 
pan water evaporation rate of 0.56 nmi/h which is typical 
of evaporation in the middle of a sunny day. Environment 
number 2 was a shaded area with natural convection of air 
at 24° C and 50% relative humidity. It provided energy 
equivalent to an average pan water evaporation of 
0.07 mm/h which is typical of nighttime drying or slow 
drying on an overcast and humid day. 
Once placed in the drying environments, the 16 trays 
were weighed hourly during 8 h to measure water 
evaporation from the forage. Final moisture content was 
obtained by removing all the forage from each tray at the 
end of the drying period and oven drying it at 60° C for 
72 h (ASAE, 1993, Standard S358.2). Moisture content at 
each weighing interval was calculated from the dry matter 
content based on the final moisture content measured. The 
rate of moisture evaporation from the forage was estimated 
from the time-related exponential function:

Other physical characteristics measured included yield 
of the standing crop, yield in the windrow and yield in 
mats. Eight additional mat samples (four from each forage 
species) were taken from the field to the laboratory each 
mowing day to measure mat thickness and cohesion. Half 
of these additional mat samples (two alfalfa, two timothy) 
received rain as described above; the other half was dried 
in ambient air without exposure to rain. Mat thickness was 
taken at eight points from each mat sample. Cohesion was 
measured by gradually increasing the free span distance of 
the mat sample on an apparatus similar to the one described 
by Shinners et al. (1987b) except that mats were inclined at 
45° from the horizontal plane to better simulate actual 
deposition along an oblique path. Cohesion was expressed 
as the length at which the mat started to break, defined by a 
tear of over 1/3 the mat width. Cohesion is an important 
measure in designing the height of the mat forming and 
pickup mechanisms with respect to ground or stubble 
height. The design goal is to maintain mat integrity by 
using a free span distance shorter than cohesion length. 
Loss MEASUREMENTS 
Some researchers have attempted to measure dry matter 
(DM) loss by estimating initial and final dry matter of 
forage in a tray of fixed quantity (Rotz et al., 1991). A 
major problem with this approach is that initial DM can 
only be estimated on the basis of moisture content taken 
from several samples which are similar but never identical 
to the experimental sample. In very wet forage, a small 
error in moisture estimation can cause a large difference in 
dry matter estimation. For example, at a typical initial 
moisture content of 83%, a 1% error in moisture estimation 
will cause a 6% error in dry matter estimation. This is often 
of the same order of magnitude as the loss measured. For

Five chemical constituents were measured in the fresh 
crop and after drying to estimate any change during 
wilting. They are: crude protein (CP), acid detergent fiber 
(ADF), calcium (Ca), phosphorous (P), and magnesium 
(Mg). CP and ADF were measured by methods described 
by Goering and Van Soest (1970). Calcium was measured 
by flame emission (Technicon IV photometer); 
phosphorous was measured by colorimetry (Tecator 5323 
U.V. spectrophotometer); magnesium was measured by 
atomic absorption (Perkin Elmer 460 atomic absorption 
spectrophotometer). ADF was considered a suitable 
(non water soluble, non respirable) constituent for 
estimating dry matter loss (eq. 2) during laboratory 
simulation of raining and drying. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
PHYSICAL CHARACTERISTICS 
Forage was mowed in the field during the first growth 
cycle in 1992 on six dates when no rained occurred: 2, 4,9, 
11,15, and 17 June. Initial moisture declined with maturity 
from 83 to 78% for timothy, and from 85 to 80% for alfalfa 
over the three-week period (table 1). Acid detergent fiber 
(ADF) of the fresh crop increased while crude protein (CP) 
decreased with maturity which is typical for forage crops 
(table 1). Moisture contents at the time of rain represent 
moisture after the crop had dried a short time in the field 
(1 to 4 h) and 16 h later after drying in a shaded area in the 
laboratory. Mats were generally drier than conventional 
windrows, in spite of the fact that mats were sometimes 
made 2 h after windrows because of delays in adjusting the 
mat maker prototype in the field. Thus, during the same 
mowing day, mats could dry only 1 h in the field while 
windrows dried 3 or 4 h in the field prior to being brought 
in the laboratory for simulated rainfall. 
Actual standing crop yield varied between 3.03 and 
5.15 t DM/ha for timothy and between 3.59 and 
5.48 t DM/ha for alfalfa over the three week period 
(table 2). Yield in windrows and yield in mats were two to 
three times greater than yield in the standing crop because 
of the narrowing action of the mower-conditioner and the 
mat-maker. 
Mat thickness was greater than expected, averaging 
53 mm for timothy and 47 mm for alfalfa in 1992 (table 2). 
The difference between timothy and alfalfa mat thickness 
was not statistically significant (P - 0.28). In 1991 
(Savoie et al., 1993), the same prototype compressed mats 
to an average thickness of 26 mm while laboratory 
experiments produced mats of 16-mm thickness. Because 
of difficulty in maintaining good tracking of the 
compression belts on the prototype in 1992, a lower tension 
was applied to the belts which resulted in mats of low 
volumetric density. For example, Sundberg and Lundvall 
(1991) obtained in laboratory conditions volumetric 
densities above 100 kg DM/m^ which is five times greater 
than densities obtained in the field in 1992. However, the 
drying rate improvement of mats compared to conventional 
windrows was still considerable (as seen later in table 4). 
Cohesion was measured on mats that had partially dried 
and that had either received or not received two 3-mm 
rainfalls. The moisture content at the time of the second 
rainfall is given in table 1. Figure 1 illustrates mat cohesion 
averaged for both crop as a function of maturity. The 
cohesion of non-rained-on mats increased from about 150 
to 3(X) mm with maturity over the three week period of the 
experiment. The cohesion of rained-on mats did not 
increase with maturity and ranged from 130 to 200 mm.

Mats generally absorbed more rainfall than did the 
conventional windrows (table 3). During the first spraying 
of 3 mm of rain, mats on average absorbed 64% of rainfall 
while windrows absorbed 57% of rainfall. When a second 
spraying of 3 mm was applied 16 h after the first rain, mats 
absorbed 55% and windrows 51% of this second rain. 
Results from the two sequential rainfalls indicate that 
forage in general absorbs a smaller proportion of rain as the 
forage becomes wetter.

mats and windrows were left to dry in two environments. 
Mats always had a higher drying rate than conventional 
windrows in the same drying environment (table 4).

than that of conventional windrows (P « 0.0001). However, 
there were several important interactions indicating that 
mat making was more beneficial in a sunny environment 
after rain than in a shaded or cloudy environment after rain.

high drying rate increases with mats will occur more 
consistently in areas with frequent periods of sunshine, 
even if these periods of sunshine are interrupted by some 
rainfall.

(2.7%) than in windrows (6.8%). Mats dried faster than 
windrows under favorable weather and dryness inhibited 
respiration. In a previous field experiment without rain 
(Savoie et al., 1993), alfalfa mats had 8% visible DM loss 
(broken particles on the ground) after 28 h of wilting and 
pick up with a baler. In the current experiment, 5% of 
invisible loss (respiration) was estimated over a similar 
wilting period. So total field losses in alfalfa mats without 
rain are estimated at 13%. Conventional alfalfa hay harvest 
without rain usually results, at best, in 18 to 20% DM loss 
(8% visible, 12% respiration). 
However, under rainy conditions, mats remained as 
humid as windrows. Mats, averaged over alfalfa and 
timothy, had more respiration and leaching losses (14.1%) 
than windrows (10.2%) because prior maceration made 
soluble constituents more accessible for leaching and 
respiration. Because mats are also susceptible to 
crumbling, total DM losses of mats after rainfall are 
difficult to estimate precisely but would certainly be higher 
than losses in windrows. 
Rotz et al. (1991) came to a similar conclusion: mats 
had greater DM losses than conventional windrows after 
rain. However, their absolute loss estimate was lower than 
the present data. They reported losses of 0.5, 14.5, and 
26.8% in macerated alfalfa, and 1.2, 3.5, and 6.9% in 
conventional windrows subjected to 0, 18, and 62 mm of 
rain, respectively. The present data show losses of 5.4 and 
19.7% for alfalfa mats and 11.8 and 13.0% for con­
ventional windrows under 0 and 6 mm of rain, 
respectively. Both the non-rain and rain-induced losses 
were higher in the present study. However the trends were 
identical in both studies: mats had less loss without rain but 
had more loss after rain than windrows. Absolute loss 
values should be used with caution but the relative effect of 
rainfall on mats and windrows is consistent in both studies. 
The crude protein (CP) concentration of fresh timothy 
and alfalfa averaged 13.9% and 25.5%, respectively 
(table 1). After rainfall and drying, CP averaged 14.5% for 
timothy and 24.3% for alfalfa (tables 5 and 6). The only 
significant differences between final CP concentration 
were due to maturity and species. There was no significant 
difference between rained-on or non-rained-on forage with 
respect to CP concentration. 
Final concentrations of Ca and Mg were, respectively, 
4% and 8% lower in rained-on mats than in non-rained-on 
mats. Similar declines in Ca and Mg were not observed in 
windrows after rain, suggesting that maceration facilitated 
leaching of some of the soluble minerals. 
Management of mat making technology will require 
consideration of the relative risk of losses due to rain. It is 
clear that chemical composition and dry matter losses are 
not much more affected by rain in timothy mats than in 
timothy windrows. However, alfalfa mats are more 
sensitive to rain losses than alfalfa windrows. The user of 
mat making technology to harvest alfalfa will have to 
consider more carefully weather predictions and the 
probability of rainfall in the next 24 to 48 h. If the weather 
is sunny, matted forage can usually be harvested as hay 
within 6 to 30 h after mowing (Shinners et al., 1987a; 
Savoie et al., 1993). If the forage is harvested for silage, 
wilting time is likely to be reduced to between 2 and 8 h. 
Realizing that matted alfalfa will be subject to substantial 
losses if rained on, a careful manager should nonetheless 
be able to take advantage of mat making. Mat-making 
considerably reduces the average wilting time in the field, 
and therefore the risk of having rain fall on the forage 
before it is picked up. 
CONCLUSIONS 
• Mats produced in the field and subjected or not 
subjected to rainfall always dried faster than 
conventional windrows in similar conditions. 
Drying coefficients of timothy mats were 135% 
higher than that of timothy windrows in non-rain 
conditions; timothy mats dried 87% faster than 
windrows after rain. Drying coefficients of alfalfa 
mats were 87% higher than that of alfalfa windrows 
in non-rain conditions; alfalfa mats dried 54% 
faster than windrows after rain. 
• Under a light rainfall up to 6 mm, mats absorbed a 
greater proportion (60%) of rain than windrows 
(54%). 
• Relatively high drying rates obtained with mats 
compared to windrows will occur more consistently 
in areas of frequent sunshine than in areas 
characterized by prolonged cloudiness and high 
humidity. 
• When no rain occurred, mats had less respiration 
loss (2.7%) than windrows (6.8%). However when 
rain occurred, total respiration and leaching losses 
were higher in mats (8.5% for timothy, 19.7% for 
alfalfa) than in windrows (7.4% for timothy, 13.0% 
for alfalfa).
