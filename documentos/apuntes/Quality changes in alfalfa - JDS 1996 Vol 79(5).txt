Quality Changes in Alfalfa Hay During Storage in Bales' 
ABSTRACT 
This study quantified changes in forage quality 
and chemical composition that occurred in baled al- 
falfa hay. Conventional and laboratory-scale alfalfa 
hay bales of three densities (1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 times 
the density of the conventional bale) were made at 
two percentages of moisture (20 and 30%) and were 
stored for 60 d in small haystacks insulated with 
straw. Bale treatments were sampled before storage 
and at 4, 11, 22, and 60 d postbaling. These sampling 
times corresponded to specifically chosen temperature 
maxima and minima found in typical curves of tem- 
perature and time for baled alfalfa hay. Most changes 
in forage chemical composition (quality) appeared to 
be closely related to accumulated heating degree days 
>3OoC. The most active period of quality degradation, 
as measured by increases in NDF and ADIN concen- 
tration, generally occurred between 4 and 11 d post- 
baling. High moisture treatment combinations that 
favored extended microbial activity continued to 
depress forage quality (particularly increased NDF 
and ADIN) between d 11 and 22. Little change in 
quality occurred after 22 d, indicating that most 
changes in forage quality were completed within a 
month after baling. Strong linear relationships were 
observed between both NDF and artifact N concentra- 
tions and heating degree days >30°C in high moisture 
treatments.

INTRODUCTION 
Negative quality changes that occur during the 
storage of baled alfalfa ( Medicago sativa L.) hay are 
associated with a forage moisture content at baling 
>20% ( 7 ) , Quality changes, primarily facilitated by 
microbial activity and the subsequent generation of 
heat, include oxidation of nonstructural carbohy- 
drates, DM loss, mold growth, and increased concen- 
trations of fiber components and artifact (unavaila- 
ble) N. Such changes can negatively affect the feed 
value of the forage and result in decreased animal 
performance. Therefore, understanding the mechan- 
isms that govern negative quality changes is impor- 
tant to maximize the quality of stored forage and 
animal productivity. 
Previous research (7, 12, 13, 14, 16) designed to 
investigate changes in forage quality during bale 
storage has been conducted largely on the basis of a 
single sampling time that normally is associated with 
the cessation of microbial activity. Such an endpoint 
often is verified when internal bale temperatures 
have returned to approximately ambient air tempera- 
ture for several weeks. Internal temperature patterns 
exhibited by bales in hay storage typically display two 
distinct temperature maxima that occur immediately 
after baling (5, 6, 10) and between 5 and 20 d 
postbaling (5, 6). Single sampling at the endpoint 
can provide useful information about total quality 
changes that occur in hay during bale storage, but 
does not address when these quality changes occur or 
how they are related to associated heating. Utiliza- 
tion of multiple sampling at specific times that cor- 
respond to expected temperature maxima and 
minima in the normal heating pattern in baled alfalfa 
hay should provide additional insight into the 
mechanisms that govern changes in forage quality 
during storage. This aspect of hay storage research 
has not been extensively studied, but a firm under- 
standing of these relationships should ultimately con- 
tribute to optimizing the quality of conserved hays. 
The objective of this study was to utilize multiple 
samplings to quantify the quality changes that oc- 
curred in alfalfa hay baled at two moisture contents 
with specific emphasis on when these changes oc- 
curred during the storage period and how these

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Generation of a Sample File 
A 3-yr-old stand of 'Kansas Common' alfalfa was 
harvested (fourth cutting) at 10% bloom with a 
mower conditioner on September 9, 1992 near Keats, 
Kansas. The forage was mowed in three blocks of two 
swaths each and was allowed to dry undisturbed until 
the desired moisture content was reached on Septem- 
ber 11, 1992. Swaths in each block were allocated 
randomly to one of two whole plots based solely on 
moisture percentage at the time of baling. Moisture 
contents averaged across bale types were 30% (high) 
and 20% (low) (Table 1) and were chosen to gener- 
ate intense (high moisture) and minimal (low 
moisture) heating and resultant quality change. 
Twelve 37-cm x 46-cm x 94-cm conventional bales 
(COW) were made from each swath. Densities of 
the CONV were 313 and 189 kg/m3 (as is basis) for 
high and low moisture forages, respectively (Table 
1). Then, 2 of the 12 COW from each swath were 
selected randomly to provide a forage source for the 
subsequent manufacture of laboratory-scale bales 
(LAB). 
The LAB system was used in this study for several 
specific reasons. Bale density in LAB was used to 
generate diverse levels of incurred heat within a 
given moisture percentage. Density previously has 
been established as a factor influencing spontaneous 
heating in both COW ( 3 1 and LAB stored in isolated 
environments (6 ) . In addition, sampling bales over 
the entire storage period rather than after storage re- 
quired some disruption of the haystack during sam- 
pling. In this study, whole LAB were quickly removed 
from the stack, thereby minimizing disruptive in- 
fluences of sampling, as opposed to traditional coring 
methods used for CONV. Other advantages of this 
system have been described previously ( 5 1. 
The LAB were made at 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 times the 
density of the CONV (LABl.0, LAB1.5, and LAB2.0, 
respectively) using the procedures described by 
Coblentz et al. (4, 5). Within each block, 6 
10.3-cm x 10.8-cm x 13.4-cm LAB at each density 
were made and then assigned randomly to one of four 
sampling times (4, 11, 22, and 60 d). Three of the 6 
LAB were assigned to the 60-d (final) sampling time 
to allow multiple observations of internal bale tem- 
perature over the entire storage period for each repli- 
cate and to provide additional material for further 
study. 
For each block, an additional LAB was made at 2.0 
times the density of the parent CONV to estimate 
both DM content and indices of forage quality of LAB 
before storage. Immediately after formation and be- 
fore the storage period, bale wires were removed from 
the LAB, and the contents were dried to a constant 
weight in a paper bag under forced air at 50°C. Prior 
to manufacture of LAB, indices of DM content and 
prestorage quality for COW were determined by 
removing approximately one-quarter portions of six 
bale flakes from each of the 2 CONV designated as 
forage sources for LAB. This forage subsequently was 
composited and dried in the same manner as the 
prestorage LAB. 
For each field replicate, within each moisture per- 
centage, wooden pallets were placed on the concrete

floor of an open air pole shed and covered with sheets 
of Styrofoam@ insulation (Celotex Corporation,

positioned in a row, side by side (wires up), on top of 
the Styrofoam@ sheets. All 6 LAB from a single den-

(wires up) with no air space between adjacent LAB. 
The LAB of the other two densities were positioned in 
a similar manner on top of 2 of the other 3 stacked 
COW. Loose, unused flakes of experimental hay that 
remained after manufacture of LAB subsequently 
were packed around the LAB to prevent direct air 
access and to provide a stable base for the 4 remain- 
ing unstacked CONV that then were placed on top of 
the LAB. One pair of top and bottom COW did not 
have LAB sandwiched between them, and loose, un- 
used bale flakes were substituted between them to 
maintain the integrity of the stack. The relative posi- 
tion within the stack for each set of 6 LAB constitut- 
ing one density level was randomized independently 
for each stack. Each complete stack, including 8 
COW and three sets of 6 LAB, then was covered on 
the top and sides with a single layer of straw bales to 
insulate the stack, thereby minimizing the effects 
from fluctuations in ambient air temperature. 
On d 4 of bale storage, 1 LAB from each density 
was taken from each stack, the wires were removed, 
and the contents were subsequently dried in the same 
manner as used for prestorage samples. The position 
of the remaining 5 LAB at each density were adjusted 
to remove air pockets created by the removal of the 
LAB. At the same time, three 30- to 40-cm cores were 
obtained from each end of 2 randomly selected CONV 
from each stack (1 each from the top and bottom 
layers). Top CONV were cored when they were dis- 
placed to remove LAB. Bottom CONV were cored in 
place. Core samples were obtained with a core sam- 
pler and dried in the same manner used for LAB. The 
CONV then were returned to their previous position 
in the stack to maintain stack integrity. This process 
was repeated on d 11 and 22 of storage. 
After 60 d of bale storage, CONV and LAB from 
each stack were sampled as described for other 
storage periods and then visually appraised for mold 
growth (five-point scale: 1 = no visible mold, 2 = 
presence of spores between flakes, 3 = presence of 
spores throughout bale, 4 = mycelial mat between 
flakes, and 5 = mycelial mat throughout the bale) by 
the method described by Roberts et al. (15). When 
appropriate, increments of 0.5 were used to evaluate 
each bale. Recoveries of DM for CONV and LAB were 
determined from calculated DM weights of each bale 
before and after storage. In LAB with low moisture, 
bale wires weighed up to 10% of the weight of the 
baled forage; therefore, weights of bale wire were 
obtained for all LAB and were excluded from all 
calculations of bale weight, bale density, and DM 
recovery. 
Chemical Analysis of Forage 
Dry forage samples were ground through a Wiley 
mill (1-mm screen; Arthur H. Thomas, Philadelphia. 
PA) and subsequently analyzed for N, NDF, ADF, 
ADIN, and in vitro DM indigestibility ( WDMI. 
IVDMI = 1000 g/kg of DM - in vitro DM digestibility. 
or the DM remaining after a 72-h in vitro digestion 
with buffered rumen fluid) ( 11 1. Concentrations of 
ADIN were calculated and reported on the basis of 
total DM ( ADIN-DM) and total N ( ADIN-NI Total 
plant N was determined using a micro-Kjeldahl proce- 
dure ( 1). Neutral detergent fiber (omitting sulfite) 
and ADF, both reported on the basis of total DM, 
were determined by procedures outlined by Van Soest 
et al. (21). The ADIN fractions were determined by 
the method of Goering and Van Soest (8). 
Temperature Analysis 
For each stack, bale temperatures were monitored 
by inserting single thermocouples into the center of 2 
CONV and 2 LAB of each density that were assigned 
to be sampled on d 60 of bale storage. For all bales, 
thermocouples were inserted, and initial temperature 
was recorded when stacks were formed (approxi- 
mately 3 h aRer the forage was removed from the 
field). Bale temperatures were recorded at 0800 and 
1500 h for the first 14 d of storage and once daily 
thereafter throughout the 60-d storage period. All 
temperature data were compiled with a thermocouple 
thermometer (Omega 450 AKT Type K; Omega En- 
gineering, Stamford, CT). For computation, the mean 
internal bale temperature for a given day was consi- 
dered to be the same as the observed temperature, 
except during the first 14 d, when the mean of the two 
observations was used. Heating degree days >3OoC 
( HDD) were computed as the sum of the daily incre- 
ment by which the mean internal bale temperature 
was ~30°C. Treatment means were compared for max- 
imum temperature, mean temperature, and HDD. 
Temperature responses monitored in this manner 
described the total heat increment present in the 
center portion of the bales of each treatment combina- 
tion that was incubated within a small stack. Based 
on past experience (5, 61, all heat measured in a 
given LAB should not be assumed to have been gener- 
ated by respiratory processes occurring within that 
specific bale, although, for practical purposes, that

factors make interpretation of results more complex, 
the procedures used provided a strong indication as to 
the nature and intensity of respiratory processes af-

methods for separating transferred and self-generated 
heat from specific LAB within small haystacks should 
aid in interpreting results. 
Statistical Analysis 
Temperature indices were analyzed as a split-split 
plot (repeated measures) with two moisture percen- 
tages as whole plots, four baling treatments as sub- 
plots, and four sampling times as sub-subplots. In- 
dices of forage quality were analyzed as a split-plot 
design with two moisture percentages as whole plots. 
Because LAB had to be destructively sampled for 
quality analysis, repeated measurements on the same 
bales were not possible. Therefore, 16 subplots were 
allocated in a 4 x 4 factorial arrangement of baling 
treatments (CONV, IAB1.0, LAB1.5, and LAB2.O) 
and times (4, 11, 22, and 60 d). Analysis on a single 
endpoint (d 60) was most appropriate for certain 
specific response variables (DM content after storage, 
DM recovery, visible mold appraisal, and maximum 
temperature). In such cases, results were analyzed as 
a split plot; two moisture percentages served as whole 
plots, and four baling treatments were the subplots. 
Prestorage forage quality traits were analyzed 
separately as a split plot; two moisture percentages 
were the whole plots, and bale types (CONV and 
LAB) were subplots. The ANOVA procedure of SAS 
( 18 was used in all cases. For mean comparisons, 
sampling times and baling treatments were consi- 
dered to be unstructured because sampling times 
were designed to correspond to specific points on tem- 
perature and time curves that did not represent 
graded intervals of time, and bale density was graded 
in LAB primarily to generate a wide diversity of 
incurred heat among baling treatments within a 
given moisture percentage rather than to study den- 
sity effects per se. For these reasons, actual treatment 
means were compared using Fisher’s protected least 
significance difference test. Significance was declared 
at P I 0.05 unless otherwise indicated. 
Regression 
Quality indices for each replicate were linearly 
regressed on associated HDD within moisture percen- 
tages using the REG procedure of SAS (18). These 
relationships included quality responses at all sam- 
pling times (prestorage and at 4, 11, 22, and 60 d in 
storage). The commonality of slopes and intercepts 
among baling treatments within moisture percen- 
tages was determined by the general linear models 
procedure of SAS 18) I Significance of regressions 
and commonality of slopes and intercepts was 
declared at P = 0.05. When regression lines were not 
common to all treatments, multiple pairwise compari- 
sons were used to declare significant differences 
among regression parameters. Bonferroni’s adjust- 
ment (19) to the t value was used to adjust the 
pairwise error rate, depending on the number of com- 
parisons necessary to maintain an error rate of 10% 
within a moisture percentage. 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Temperature Responses 
Sampling times were chosen before the experiment 
to coincide approximately with specific temperature 
maxima and minima found in typical curves of tem- 
perature and time for alfalfa hay bales during 
storage. Such curves typically are characterized by 
two prominent temperature maxima. The first is brief 
(1 to 4 d), occurs immediately aRer storage begins 
(5, 6, 10, 13), is frequently less intense than the 
second temperature maxima ( lo), and is thought to 
be associated with respiration of plant cells (10, 22). 
The second temperature maximum, created by respi- 
ration of bacteria, fungi, and yeasts associated with 
the hay (171, normally is found as part of a broad 
peak or group of peaks occurring aRer internal bale 
temperatures subside following the first maximum. 
This maximum normally occurs 5 to 20 d after baling 
(2, 13, 14). Bales were sampled on d 4 to separate 
effects of the first and second temperature maxima. 
Additional sampling at 11 and 22 d was targeted at 
the second temperature maximum, coinciding approx- 
imately with the peak internal bale temperature and 
the conclusion of intense heating, respectively. Day 60 
was considered to be a poststorage sampling time. 
Both high and low moisture treatments displayed 
the expected pattern of temperature responses 
described previously (Figures 1 and 2). The plot for 
high moisture (Figure 1) demonstrates both the posi- 
tive relationship between bale density and tempera- 
ture responses in LAB described previously (6 ) and 
the successful use of this relationship to generate 
diverse heating increments. During the period of ac- 
tive heating, the most dense (LAB2.O) bales main- 
tained the highest internal bale temperatures, fol- 
lowed, in descending sequence, by less dense 
treatments. Regardless of bale density, LAB generally

Forage Analysis 
Prestorage quality. No significant differences oc- 
curred in the quality of forage prior to the storage 
period between bale types within a moisture percen- 
tage (Table 4). This result indicated that the process 
of manufacturing LAB did not affect forage quality 
relative to the parent CONV. The excellent agree- 
ment between bale types in initial moisture content 
within each moisture percentage indicated that little 
water was dissipated from experimental forage dur- 
ing the period of forage handling associated with the 
manufacture of LAB. 
DM recovery. Analysis of variance (not shown) 
indicated that the interaction between moisture and 
bale treatment was significant (P = 0.04). Recovery 
of DM in high moisture CONV was less than that in 
LAB1.0 and LAB1.5 but was similar (P > 0.05) to 
that in LAB2.0. No differences were observed among 
low moisture treatments. 
Mold growth appraisals. The ANOVA (not 
shown) indicated that main effects for moisture (P = 
0.03) and bale treatment (P c 0.01), as well as their 
associated interaction (P c 0.011, were all significant.

Regression of Quality Indices on HDD 
Regression analysis clearly established significant 
(P c 0.05) relationships for all changes in forage 
quality on HDD during bale storage (probabilities not 
shown) for both high and low moisture forages. Bal- 
ing treatments lacked a common slope only for IVDMI 
(P = 0.02) at high moisture and ADIN-N (P = 0.02) 
at low moisture. All treatments with the same 
moisture percentage had common intercepts. 
The closest relationships between quality indices 
and HDD occurred for high moisture bales (Table 9) 
in which the most intense heating occurred during 
storage. These relationships were particularly strong 
(r2 > 0.80) for artifact N and NDF concentrations. At 
low moisture (Table 9), changes in quality were more 
limited, but the strongest relationships between qual- 
ity indices and HDD were observed with respect to 
the same quality indices (ADIN-N, ADIN-DM, and 
NDF). Predictably, the associated correlations for low 
moisture treatments were considerably poorer than 
those for high moisture treatments, reflecting the 
limited response to treatment, the narrower range of 
HDD accumulated in low moisture treatments, and 
the increased effect of random sampling error in ex- 
plaining experimental variation. 
The observed relationships between fiber compo- 
nents and HDD are difficult to explain. Although 
regressions of NDF on HDD for high moisture treat- 
ments were characterized by close (r2 = 0.8131, posi- 
tive relationships, the corresponding relationship 
with respect to ADF was substantially poorer (r2 = 
0.559). Predictably, at low moisture, more variation 
was unexplained by the model, but HDD were still 
better predictors for NDF (r2 = 0.453) than for ADF 
(r2 = 0.151). Numerous studies (3, 5, 6, 7, 16) have 
reported increases in the concentration of fiber com- 
ponents after bale storage. Those increases have been 
associated primarily with the apparent stability of

fiber components during bale storage concurrent with 
substantial respiratory losses of nonstructural carbo- 
hydrates (10, 16, 17) and, to a minor extent, with N 
(16). If fiber components were stable during storage 
and if changes in NDF and ADF concentrations were 
primarily the indirect results of DM loss, then similar 
fit of the data should be expected for regressions of 
both response variables on HDD, because the NDF 
and ADF concentrations for each replicate were as- 
sociated with both identical DM loss and accumulated 
HDD. The reasons for the large differences in fit for

CONCLUSIONS 
Most changes in forage quality appeared to be 
linearly related to HDD accumulated during bale 
storage, particularly for NDF and artifact N in high 
moisture treatments (r2 > 0.8). The most active 
period of quality degradation, particularly for NDF 
and artifact N, generally occurred between the cessa- 
tion of heating from plant respiration (d 4) and the 
approximate temperature maximum created by 
microbial activity (d 11). High moisture CONV, 
LAB1.5, and LAB2.0, treatment combinations that 
favored extended microbial activity, continued to 
depress forage quality between the temperature max- 
imum (d 11) induced by microbial respiration and 
the cessation of this heating (d 22). Quality changes 
during the plant respiratory phase of heating (0 to 4 
d) were particularly noticeable for NDF in all high 
moisture treatments. Most changes in forage quality 
appeared to be complete within a month under the 
storage conditions of this study.
