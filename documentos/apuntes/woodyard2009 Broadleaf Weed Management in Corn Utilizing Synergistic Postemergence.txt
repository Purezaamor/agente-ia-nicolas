POST combinations of photosystem II (PSII) and the 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD) inhibitors are
effective for control of broadleaf weeds in corn. Field studies were conducted during 2007 and 2008 in Urbana and
Dekalb, IL, to determine the nature of interactions between two PSII inhibitors, atrazine and bromoxynil, and the HPPD
inhibitor mesotrione, based on control of common waterhemp, common lambsquarters, and giant ragweed. Two rates of
each herbicide were evaluated, with the highest representing a typical recommended rate, and the lowest a fraction of that
rate. Synergistic interactions occurred for common waterhemp control from 10 to 30 d after treatment (DAT) regardless of
herbicide rates, rainfall accumulation, or plant height. Synergism between mesotrione and bromoxynil was not observed for
common lambsquarters control at Urbana in 2008 at the lower herbicide rates, possibly due to taller weed heights at the
time of herbicide application relative to 2007. Giant ragweed control indicated that a synergistic interaction occurred for
all herbicides and rates in 2008. However, synergism between bromoxynil and mesotrione did not occur in 2007, likely
due to limited rainfall before herbicide application. Reduced herbicide rates and adverse environmental conditions have the
potential to regulate the expression of synergism between mesotrione and PSII inhibitors, and therefore may limit their
effectiveness for weed management in corn.

Weed management in many modern agricultural systems is
dominated by the use of herbicide-resistant crops, yet weed
control remains as challenging and essential for maintaining
crop yield as it has ever been. Due to the widespread
utilization of herbicides with the same site of action and the
lack of new herbicide discovery in recent years (Cole et al.
2000), there are 319 documented cases of herbicide resistance
in 185 weed species, with expectations of continuing selection
for additional resistant biotypes (Heap 2008). Therefore, it
has become essential to manage, extend, and utilize existing
herbicide options in ways that will preserve the sustainable use
of these products for weed management.
Atrazine, a PSII-inhibiting herbicide, is among the
herbicides with extensive resistance issues. The first reported
case of weed resistance to atrazine was identified in common
groundsel (Senecio vulgaris L.) (Ryan 1970). Today, 67 weed
species have been reported to be resistant to triazine
herbicides, many of which are difficult to control in corn
(Heap 2008). The most common mechanism for atrazine
resistance results from a mutation in the psbA gene that
encodes a serine 264 to glycine substitution in the D1 protein,
the target site for atrazine (Devine and Preston 2000;
Hirschberg and McIntosh 1983). Another mechanism for
atrazine resistance occurs in velvetleaf biotypes (Abutilon
theophrasti Medik), where atrazine is rapidly detoxified by
glutathione S-transferase enzymes (Anderson and Gronwald
1991; Gray et al. 1996). However, even though triazine
resistance issues are abundant, atrazine is still one of the most
popular herbicides in corn. Atrazine is used on over 68% of
corn acres in the United States (Anonymous 2003) due to its
low cost, compatibility with many other herbicides for both
PRE and POST applications, ability to enhance the weed
control spectrum of other herbicides, and residual control of
broadleaf weeds. Bromoxynil is another PSII inhibitor that
can be used POST to control broadleaf weeds in corn, but it
differs from atrazine in that bromoxynil has limited soil
residual activity and provides poor control of several
important broadleaf weed species (Corbett et al. 2004), most
notably common waterhemp, Palmer amaranth (Amaranthus
palmeri S. Wats.), redroot pigweed (Amaranthus retroflexus
L.), and smooth pigweed (Amaranthus hybridus L.).
One of the newest commercially available herbicide classes
for use in corn is the HPPD inhibitors. The recent
introduction of topramezone and tembotrione, along with
the currently available HPPD inhibitors isoxaflutole and
mesotrione, raises concerns about increased selection pressure
for weed resistance to this class of herbicides. Applying
herbicides with different sites of action in combination has
been proposed to reduce the selection for herbicide resistance
in weeds (Diggle et al. 2003). Previous research demonstrated
a synergistic interaction between PSII and HPPD inhibitors
applied POST, in which the herbicidal activity of the two
herbicides combined is greater than the additive effect of each
herbicide alone (Abendroth et al. 2006; Hugie et al. 2008;
Sutton et al. 2002; Woodyard et al. 2009). This interaction
can potentially be used to reduce selection for weed resistance
to these herbicides. Additionally, this synergistic response
causes atrazine-like symptoms on triazine-resistant (TR) weeds
with an altered site of action as the mechanism of resistance,
thus overcoming the mechanism for atrazine resistance (Hugie
et al. 2008; Sutton et al. 2002). The synergistic interaction

Assessing the joint activity of herbicides with different
modes of action is most often accomplished using variations
of the Multiplicative Survival Model (MSM) (Flint et al.
1988; Green et al. 1997; Kelly and Chapman 1995; Streibig
et al. 1998). The MSM often utilizes Colby’s equation
(Equation 1) for determining herbicide joint action through
the analysis of various quantitative observations, including
plant biomass (fresh or dry weight), percent growth
suppression, or percent survival (Colby 1967; Gowing
1960). Colby’s equation is expressed as
E~ (XY ) 100 ½1/C138
where values are expressed as growth (or dry weights) as a percent
of control. In our study, E is the expected plant response (i.e.,
percent weed control) when both herbicides A and B are applied
together, and X and Y each represent plant responses when
herbicides A and B are applied independently at specific rates.
Although it is common to use Colby’s equation to calculate
herbicide joint activity within the MSM, it is still necessary to
utilize a statistical test to analyze herbicide joint activity to
determine if a statistically significant interaction occurs.
Several methods can be used to analyze herbicide joint action,
including one proposed by Flint et al. (1988). Their method
applies a statistical test to Colby’s equation by utilizing slope
comparisons. Based on an ANOVA model for a factorial
experiment, this method can be applied to a wide range of
parameters (Flint et al. 1988). Their method takes into
account the effect of each herbicide applied independently,
then applies those slope deviations to create an expected plant
response of the two herbicides in combination. If the slope
deviates significantly from the expected result, the herbicides
applied in combination may be considered either synergistic if
a greater than expected plant response occurs, or antagonistic
if a less than expected plant response occurs (Flint et al. 1988).
This statistical method has been previously used to analyze
synergism between mesotrione and atrazine in greenhouse
studies with redroot pigweed (Hugie et al. 2008) and
velvetleaf (Woodyard et al. 2009). The treatments in the
current study were also designed to fit Flint et al.’s (1988)
method for analysis of herbicide joint activity.
Results from previous studies demonstrated the synergism
between mesotrione and PSII inhibitors applied POST
(Abendroth et al. 2006; Hugie et al. 2008; Sutton et al.
2002), PRE (Bollman et al. 2006), or in PRE–POST
sequential applications (Woodyard et al. 2009) under
greenhouse and field conditions. An objective of the current
study was to determine the nature of any interactions between
HPPD- and PSII-inhibiting herbicides across a range of rates
at different time periods following application, with the
ultimate goal of utilizing synergistic herbicide combinations
for broadleaf weed management in corn. Furthermore,
differences in weed control resulting from the residual activity
of POST applications were assessed when comparing atrazine
or bromoxynil in combination with mesotrione.
MaterialsandMethods
General Procedures. Field experiments were conducted during
2007 and 2008 at the University of Illinois Crop Sciences
Research and Education Centers in Urbana and Dekalb, IL.
The fields were in a soybean–corn rotation and were chisel
plowed the previous fall, followed by a single pass with a field
cultivator in the spring. Dekalb ‘DK592SR’ was planted at a
population of 83,000 seeds/ha in Urbana and 94,000 seeds/ha
in Dekalb. This hybrid was chosen so that sethoxydim could be
applied POST to control grasses that could potentially interfere
with broadleaf weed growth. Soil types were a Drummer silty
clay loam (Fine-silty, mixed, superactive, mesic Typic
Endoaquolls) in Urbana and a Flanagan silt loam (Fine,
smectitic, mesic Aquic Argiudolls) at the Dekalb research farm.
The studies in Urbana were planted on April 30, 2007, April
23, 2008, and May 23, 2008. A second planting date was added
in 2008 because the first study contained a substantial
population of morningglory (Ipomoea spp.) that required a
very early POST application of paraquat to suppress the
morningglory seedlings and allow for emergence of common
lambsquarters and common waterhemp. The Dekalb studies
were planted on May 3, 2007 and May 19, 2008.
Plots were four rows and 3 m wide by 9 m in length.
Herbicide treatments were applied when corn plants were
between 25 to 32 cm tall in the V4 to V5 growth stage (or 26 to
30 d after planting). V4 and V5 are growth stages of corn as
described by Ritchie et al. (1997), where the V4 and V5 stages
represent four or five fully-expanded leaves and when the fourth
or fifth leaf has a collar visisble from the whorl. The exception
to this occurred in Urbana in 2008, where POST treatments
were applied 49 d after planting due to slow crop and weed
growth. Most common waterhemp and common lambsquar-
ters plants were 10 to 15 cm in height when the herbicides were
applied in Urbana, with some plants up to 20 cm tall. Giant
ragweed plants were 10 to 20 cm tall in Dekalb, with some
plants up to 25 cm tall in 2008. Common lambsquarters
seedlings at Dekalb were typically 5 to 10 cm tall at the time of
the POST treatment. Herbicides were applied with a CO2-
pressurized backpack sprayer equipped with a 2.3-m-wide
handheld boom and five 110025 air induction extended range
TeeJet1 flat-spray nozzles spaced 46 cm apart. The sprayer was
calibrated to deliver 187 L/ha at a pressure of 240 kPa. Three
rows of each plot were treated, allowing two rows to serve as a
nontreated control between the plots.
POST treatments included the PSII inhibitors atrazine2
and bromoxynil3 and the HPPD inhibitor mesotrione,4
applied alone or in combination, to allow for a calculation
of joint activity between the herbicides. Mesotrione was
applied at 35 and 105 g ai/ha alone and in combination with
atrazine at 280 and 560 g ai/ha, or bromoxynil at 140 and 280
g ai/ha. Rates were chosen based upon a dose-response
experiment conducted previously with redroot pigweed in the
greenhouse, in which synergistic herbicide activity had
occurred (Hugie et al. 2008). Herbicides were applied with
1% (v/v) crop oil concentrate (COC)5 and 2.5% (v/v) urea
ammonium nitrate (UAN)6 as spray adjuvants.
Data Analysis. The experiment was a randomized complete
block with three replications. Weed control was visually

estimated at 10 and 30 DAT using a scale ranging from 0 (no
control) to 100 (plant death). Data were analyzed using the
mixed procedure of SAS.7 Data were not combined over years
and locations as there were insufficient environments for each
of the weed species to be considered a random sample
representative of the larger population (Carmer et al. 1989).
Year and location were considered fixed variables in the
statistical analysis, and each combination of location and year
was analyzed separately. Data from visual assessment of weed
control were transformed before analysis, using a log
transformation to stabilize variances. Nontransformed data
are presented with a statistical interpretation based upon the
transformed data. Means were separated using Fisher’s
Protected LSD test at the 0.05 level of significance. Synergistic
responses in weed control between the PSII inhibitors and
mesotrione were calculated with a mixed linear model in SAS,
using a repeated measures analysis based on Colby’s (1967)
method and a statistical interpretation based on Flint et al.’s
(1988) method. This analysis was used previously to
determine herbicide joint activity between mesotrione and
PSII inhibitors in redroot pigweed (Hugie et al. 2008).
The expected results of the combination of a PSII inhibitor
and mesotrione were calculated based upon control ratings
determined from each of the herbicides applied alone in
comparison with the nontreated control. Synergistic interactions
were then determined by comparing the expected response of
the two herbicides in combination to the observed response
using Fisher’s Protected LSD test at a 0.05 level of significance.
Contrasts were used to estimate the deviation of the response
from additivity, and synergism was determined to occur when
the observed response of the two herbicides in combination was
greater than the expected outcome at a P value , 0.05.
ResultsandDiscussion
Weed control at 10 and 30 DAT indicated that synergism
can occur between mesotrione and either PSII inhibitor, but
in certain weed species and years, synergism only occurred at
specific herbicide rates. The two rating intervals were chosen
because at 10 DAT, synergism was first consistently detected
for foliar herbicide activity on existing weed populations, and
by 30 DAT, sufficient time had elapsed for new weed
emergence as well as crop canopy closure. As a result, the 30
DAT ratings reflect both initial control of plants that existed
at the time of POST application along with residual control.
Common Waterhemp. In both years, common waterhemp
control with atrazine was low and variable; 17 to 67% at 10
DAT and 12 to 43% at 30 DAT (Table 1). Bromoxynil is
inherently weak in controlling common waterhemp (Corbett et
al. 2004; Hugie et al. 2008). Common waterhemp control with
bromoxynil in our study reflected this poor activity, ranging
from 15 to 27% at 10 DAT and 11 to 23% at 30 DAT
(Table 1). Poor control with the PSII inhibitors alone
(Table 1) may be attributed to several factors, including the
use of lower than recommended rates of PSII inhibitors in the
field. These reduced rates, however, are typically used for
determining interactions between herbicides (Green et al. 1997;
Hugie et al. 2008; Streibig et al. 1998). In addition, during the
month before herbicide application in 2007, there was very
little precipitation (Table 2), which likely resulted in drought-
stressed plants at the time of POST herbicide treatment.
In 2007 and 2008, common waterhemp control at 10 DAT
indicated synergism between mesotrione and either PSII
inhibitor regardless of herbicide rates (Table 1). In 2008 at
Urbana, there was greater precipitation the month before
treatment relative to the 2007 season (Table 2), indicating
that the expression of synergism was consistent for common
waterhemp control under environments that differed in
rainfall amounts. Common waterhemp control at 30 DAT
indicated synergism at all rates of mesotrione plus either PSII
inhibitor in both years, but in 2008 the greatest level of
control resulted from combinations that included the highest
rate of mesotrione (Table 1). This may be related to the
greater amount of residual activity resulting from the higher
rate of mesotrione.
Common Lambsquarters. Trends in common lambsquarters
control at Urbana were similar to those observed for common
waterhemp. However, the nature of herbicide interactions at
10 DAT varied, depending on the rates of mesotrione or
bromoxynil used in combination (Table 3). At the lowest
rates of bromoxynil and mesotrione applied in combination,
there was an additive effect, whereas synergism occurred for
the other three combinations of mesotrione plus bromoxynil
(Table 3). The interactions between mesotrione plus bromox-
ynil at 10 DAT for common lambsquarters control were
consistent between 2007 and 2008 (Table 3).

Common lambsquarters control at 30 DAT varied
significantly with the mesotrione rate when applied in
combinations in 2008. As the mesotrione rate increased,
control at 30 DAT was higher when either of the bromoxynil
or atrazine rates were held constant (Table 3). For example, at
a constant atrazine rate of 560 g/ha, common lambsquarters
control increased from 74 to 93% as the mesotrione rate
increased from 35 to 105 g/ha (Table 3). As discussed
previously with common waterhemp control at 30 DAT, this
increase in common lambsquarters control 30 DAT might be attributable to the greater amount of residual activity that
results from the higher rate of mesotrione.
The overall decrease in common lambsquarters control
observed in 2008 relative to 2007, in particular at the lowest
herbicide rates examined in our study, may result from several
factors. In 2008, common lambsquarters plants were larger at
the time of POST application compared with 2007. The
majority of common lambsquarters were 10 to 15 cm tall in
2007, but plants were mainly 15 to 20 cm tall in 2008
(Table 3). Additionally, in 2008, a greater amount of rainfall
occurred 1 mo before applications of POST treatments as
compared with 2007 (Table 2). It may therefore be possible
that less parent herbicide was available in the soil later in the
season due to increased herbicide degradation and dissipation,
which may explain why the lower rates did not work as
effectively as the higher rates. Accordingly, the higher rate of
mesotrione in combination with either PSII inhibitor led to a
correspondingly higher level of common lambsquarters
control in 2008 at 30 DAT (Table 3).
Common lambsquarters control at Dekalb was similar
between years (Table 4). Giant ragweed was the primary weed
species evaluated in Dekalb, and the POST application timing
was targeted for 10- to 20-cm giant ragweed, whereas
common lambsquarters plants were only 5 to 10 cm tall
due to differences in relative growth rate between the two
species (Table 5). As a result, common lambsquarters
seedlings were treated at a smaller growth stage, 5 to 10 cm
tall, compared with the Urbana site where they were greater
than 10 cm tall (Table 3). Synergism was determined for
mesotrione plus all rates of either PSII inhibitor (Table 4),

Giant Ragweed. Giant ragweed is a large-seeded broadleaf
that is difficult to control in corn late in the season, and is
similar to common waterhemp in that it has an extended
emergence period (Sprague et al. 2004). Results for control of
giant ragweed varied substantially between 2007 and 2008,
and also varied between PSII inhibitors in 2007. Synergism
did not occur between mesotrione and bromoxynil in 2007;
instead, there was an additive response for all combinations of
these herbicides (Table 5). When either rate of mesotrione
was combined with the lowest rate of atrazine (280 g/ha),
synergistic interactions did not occur 10 DAT (Table 5). At
30 DAT, all combinations of mesotrione plus atrazine were
synergistic, but no combination provided greater than 88%
control of giant ragweed (Table 5). Giant ragweed control
ranged from 69% with atrazine at 280 g/ha and mesotrione at
35 g/ha, to 88% when 560 g/ha of atrazine was combined
with 105 g/ha of mesotrione (Table 5). At 30 DAT in 2007,
all combinations of mesotrione plus atrazine were synergistic
while mesotrione–bromoxynil combinations were not, poten-
tially due to the inherent differences in residual activity
between atrazine and bromoxynil.
Weather conditions at Dekalb were similar to those at
Urbana in 2007 (Table 2). There was a relatively low amount
of rainfall during the month before POST herbicide treatment
in Dekalb (i.e., less than half the average amount of rainfall
for this time period), leading to delayed crop development
and drought-stressed plants. The drought stress may have
resulted in reduced herbicide activity on giant ragweed plants
10 DAT, thus limiting the expression of synergism (Table 2).
However, in 2008, the high amount of rainfall 1 mo before
application of POST treatments in Dekalb (Table 2) may
have contributed to significantly different results for giant
ragweed control 10 DAT as compared with 2007. For
example, when either PSII inhibitor was combined with
mesotrione in 2008, giant ragweed control was greater than
90%, and synergism was uniformly observed at 10 and 30
DAT (Table 5). Likely due to the higher amount of rainfall in
2008 relative to 2007 (Table 2), the corn was noticeably more
competitive with giant ragweed plants. Additionally, corn
plants reached canopy closure earlier in 2008 than in 2007
(data not shown), which may have contributed to the lack of
late-emerging giant ragweed. Giant ragweed plants were
similar in height at the time of POST application in 2007 and
2008, but the additional rainfall in 2008 may have
contributed to greater herbicidal activity on actively-growing
weeds, resulting in more effective weed control and expression
of synergism between the PSII inhibitors and mesotrione
(Table 5).
In general, the most effective weed control in our studies
occurred with the highest rate of either PSII inhibitor
combined with the highest rate of mesotrione. In 2007,
control of giant ragweed 30 DAT was maximized when using
the highest rate of mesotrione combined with atrazine.
Similarly, common waterhemp and common lambsquarters
control 30 DAT at Urbana in 2008 was mainly affected by the
mesotrione rate. Regarding herbicide interactions, synergism
between either PSII inhibitor and mesotrione occurred in all
weed species examined. Synergism was typically more
dependent on the mesotrione rate than on the rate of the
PSII inhibitor; i.e., an increase in the mesotrione rate caused a
significant increase in overall weed control and expression of
synergism compared with increasing the rate of the PSII
inhibitor. For example, control of common lambsquarters in
2008 at Urbana with the lowest rate of bromoxynil combined
with the lowest rate of mesotrione showed that synergism did
not occur, indicating that synergism is dependent upon rate
when mesotrione is paired with bromoxynil. This observation
is similar to the rate requirement for expression of
mesotrione–atrazine synergism in reducing biomass of redroot
pigweed (Hugie et al. 2008). However, giant ragweed control
at 10 DAT in 2007 indicated that synergism between
mesotrione and atrazine was dependent upon the rate of
atrazine. In 2008, when rainfall was higher before POST

From this study it was determined that the novel synergistic
interaction between PSII and HPPD inhibitors can be utilized
to control broadleaf weeds that are considered problematic,
difficult to control, or tolerant to PSII inhibitors in corn
production in the Midwest. However, differences in weed size
or growth stage at the time of herbicide application, or
environmental factors such as rainfall before or after herbicide
application, may affect the potential for expression of
synergism. Because of continued concerns regarding herbicide
resistance (Heap 2008) and the lack of introduction of new

It is important to note from the results of this study that
synergism between HPPD inhibitors and PSII inhibitors is
not always consistent among weed species or when varying
herbicide rates. However, two noteworthy observations,
regardless of weed species or environmental conditions before
of after herbicide application, are that: (1) the higher rate of
either PSII inhibitor mixed with the higher rate of mesotrione
provided the most effective POST treatments for broadleaf
weed control, and (2) POST-applied atrazine and mesotrione
in combination provided higher levels of weed control than
bromoxynil paired with mesotrione (particularly for giant
ragweed in 2007). In the case of mesotrione mixed with
atrazine, this most likely results from a combination of higher
soil residual activity as well as from reaching the threshold
rates of both herbicides to achieve an optimal synergistic
interaction for foliar activity from the tank-mix application
(Hugie et al. 2008). Therefore, while reducing POST
herbicide rates is a common practice currently used by
growers for weed management for economic reasons, this
practice may also limit the effectiveness of this beneficial
POST herbicide interaction for attaining optimal control of
problematic, herbicide-tolerant, or herbicide-resistant broad-
leaf weeds in corn.
