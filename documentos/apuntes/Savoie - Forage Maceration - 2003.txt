 FORAGE MACERATION:  PAST, PRESENT AND FUTURE 
P. Savoie1 
ABSTRACT 
Forage maceration is an intensive mechanical conditioning treatment usually applied at the time of 
mowing.  Mechanisms such as corrugated rolls or impact rolls turning at high speed can cause a 
shearing or bruising effect on the fresh forage, considerable cell rupture and a release of 
intracellular water.  Over the last 25 years, several experiments have been carried out to evaluate 
maceration and its effect on fiel d drying, power requirement, loss and feed value of forage.  In 
some instances, maceration increased the field drying rate and improved the dry matter (DM) 
intake or digestibility of forages fed to ruminant s.  In other instances, maceration caused high loss 
and produced a low quality feed, especially under rainy or humid wilting conditions.  In eleven 
experiments, maceration increased the drying co efficient from 26 to 160%, thereby reducing the 
required field wilting time from 21 to 62%.  Speci fic energy required for maceration ranged from 
2 to 9 kW•h/t DM and averaged 4 kW•h/t DM.  Th e quality of forage measured by fiber content 
either remained constant in three studies or d ecreased in four other studies, especially when 
macerated forage was exposed to rain during wilti ng.  The feed value of forage measured by milk 
production, DM intake or gain to feed ratio in creased in six experiments.  In five other 
experiments, macerated forage did not show a significant improvement in feed value.  Improved 
feed value following maceration is usually expl ained by mechanical shredding of fiber and by 
reduced respiration and proteolysis, especially when the forage dries rapidly.  Reduced quality and 
feed value after maceration are often due to incr eased respiration and fragmentation loss when the 
forage dries slowly and is subjected to rain.  A few commercial machines have been proposed on 
the market but have not been widely adopted. Th e future of forage maceration depends partly on 
the development of innovative mechanisms that are simple enough to maintain a low cost of 
fabrication and operation while still providing the benefits of fast drying and improved feed value. 
KEYWORDS.  Forage, hay, silage, conditioning, maceration, processing, drying, feed value. 
INTRODUCTION 
Forage maceration has been the object of research at several sites around the world over the last 25 
years.  Krutz et al. (1979) proposed an intensiv e forage conditioner made of two corrugated steel 
rolls turning at high and differential speeds with a narrow clearance between the rolls.  The 
peripheral speed difference caused a shearing effect on the crop, considerable cell rupture, a 
release of intracellular water and extremely rapi d water evaporation.  To minimize the loss of 
detached leaves and nutrient-rich juice, the macerated forage was compressed into a thin mat (5 to 
10 mm thick) which was deposited delicately onto the stubble.  Forage juice extracted during 
conditioning was either recuperated for processing into high value components or channelled back 
on top of the mat.  Related patents were obt ained by Krutz (1981), Cicci (1982) and Holdren 
(1982).  These patents covered the concept of forage maceration but did not provide enough detail 
to develop effective mechanisms that could be applied in field working machines.

ENGINEERING DEVELOPMENTS 
Macerator Design 
Three types of designs have received m ost attention:  the peripheral roll m acerator, the staggered 
roll macerator and the crushing-impact macerator (Fig. 1).   
Peripheral roll macerator.  This design was developed by a re search group in Wisconsin (Koegel 
et al. 1988).  A field working prototype consiste d of a cutterbar , a m aceration unit com posed of 
seven small (0.1 m diameter) peripheral rolls turning around a larger (0.4 m diameter) central roll, 
and a belt-and-drum press to form the mat.   The time to dry alfalfa below 20% moisture was less 
than 6 h with the macerator (Shinners et al.1987).  This was a considerably less than the typical 3-
d time period required in Wisconsin with conventional haymaking equipment.  The peripheral roll 
macerator design has also been evaluated by other researchers, notably in Germ any (Schurig and 
Rödel 1989) and Turkey (Öztekin and Özkan 1997).

The formation of a cohesive m at was required to  minimize handling losses of the highly bruised 
material.  Shinners et al. (1988a) com pared various numbers of rolls and speed ratios to optim ize 
the process. They suggested five planetary rolls  and a speed ratio of 1.5:1.  A commercial attempt 
was made to introduce the peripheral roll m acerator in the early 1990' s (Schmittbetz and Liebers 
1991; Deutz Fahr 1993) but was not conclusive. 
Staggered roll macerator.   This design was also developed in Wisconsin but first reported and 
evaluated in Québec as a result of collaborativ e work with the Wisconsin group (Savoie and 
Beauregard 1991).  A field prototype included a cu tterbar, eight staggered rolls and a belt press 
(Savoie et al. 1993).  The staggered roll design was sim pler in term s of roll fabrication than the 
peripheral roll design because all rolls could be of the sam e diameter.  The Prairie Agricultural 
Machinery Institute in Manitoba developed a two -roll staggered macerator adapted to a cutterbar 
mower (May 1994; P AMI 1994).  It was very ef ficient in term s of reducing the wilting tim e for 
alfalfa under the relatively dry conditions of Manitoba.   
Under m ore hum id conditions in Québec, Savoie et al. (1997) developed a four -roll staggered 
macerator for a disk mower.  They obtained high throughputs and good drying rate increases in the 
field.  Subsequently, the same research group de veloped a three-roll staggered m acerator adapted 
to a 4.2 m  wide cutterbar for a self-propelled mower-macerator (Savoie et al. 1999).  These 
prototypes had throughputs above 50 t of fresh cr op per hour.  These conditioners had a reduced 
maceration effect due to the sm aller number of rolls; they allowed form ing windrows without the

need to com press into a thin m at.  A patent was obtained for a three-roll m acerator (Savoie and 
Lajoie 2000) but this configuration has not yet been developed commercially. 
Crushing-impact macerator.  This type of m acerator was proposed as a sim pler alternative to the 
peripheral roll m acerator (Kraus et al. 1992; Koegel  et al. 1992a).  It consisted of two crushing 
rolls and an impact rotor.  The forage was broken into ribbon-like fibre as it struck the rotor .  The 
researchers observed that this design required c onsiderably less ener gy than the peripheral roll 
macerator for a sim ilar level of conditioning.  Se veral patents have been obtained for crushing-
impact maceration (Koegel et al. 1992b; Haldeman et al. 1999; Kraus et al. 2000a, b).   
Impact m aceration, without crushing, was attem pted com mercially (Krone 1993) as a windrow 
handling treatm ent to be applied after m owing.  Crushing m aceration, without im pact, was 
proposed by Nishizaki et al. (1997).  Four pairs of crushing rollers exerted a high pressure on the 
fresh crop to break the stem s longitudinally .  However, none of these crushing or im pact 
macerators has yet been developed commercially. 
Mat forming system 
Because maceration destroys the physical integrity of the stems, various compression devices were 
developed to agglom erate the bruised m ass into a cohesive m at.  The goal of the m at form ing 
system was to place the macerated forage on the stubble for rapid wilting and subsequent handling 
without excessive loss.  Work carried out in th e early 1980' s in Wisconsin used either a platen 
press or a roll press to study the wet strength of m acerated forage m ats (Risser et al. 1985).  A 
number of other press designs were developed subsequently.  
Belt press.  The m acerated forage is deposited onto a wide belt (typically 1.2 m  or wider).  A 
second belt of the sam e width m oving in the sam e direction conver ges against the first belt to 
compress the f orage mass into a thin layer (typi cally 5 to 10 m m thickness).  The two belts are 
compressed by m oving together between a series of  compression rolls.  Eventually the two belts 
separate and the com pressed forage m at is released onto the stubble.  Usually , an escape route is 
required for juice produced during compression.  One approach is to use of a perforated belt and to 
deflect the juice back on top of the mat when it is deposited onto the stubble.  
Wheel press.  A series of parallel wheels can be used to com press macerated forage.  The forage 
moves on a conveyor which passes over a lar ge roll of constant central axis.   A series of parallel 
wheels is pushed down over the macerated forage and exerts a pressure to form a mat. 
Slatted conveyor press.  A slatted conveyor press is made of two conveyor belts composed of rigid 
slats.  The lower and upper conveyors each m ove around rolls of constant central axes.  The 
macerated forage is fed between the two conveyors while press plates, positioned within the upper 
conveyor, compress the forage.  This system requires a low friction coef ficient between the press 
plates and the m oving slats, usually m ade of sm ooth plastic.  Savoie et al. (1997) observed that 
such a press worked well with hydraulic power and an oil droplet spreading system  to m aintain 
excellent lubrication and minimize friction between the press plates and the slats. 
No pr ess.  A high capacity press system  requires re latively high power .  Savoie et al. (1997) 
estimated a hydraulic power requirem ent of 13.1 kW for compression when the dry m atter (DM) 
flow rate was 6.7 t DM/h, i.e. a specific ener gy requirem ent of about 2 kW •h/t DM.  Another 
constraint, especially with the belt-type press,  is the need for belt guidance because of strong 
lateral forces that occur under non-uniform  forage flow .  To overcom e these dif ficulties, May 
(1994) and Savoie et al. (1997) s uggested a reduction of the m aceration intensity to elim inate the 
need for a com pression system .  However , less intensive m aceration was observed to have a 
smaller benefit on the field drying rate (see below).

The drying coefficient was always increased after maceration, by 26 to 160%.  Increases tended to 
be higher for short wilting periods (6 to 9 h) and to be lower for longer wilting periods (30 to 34 
h).  The drying coefficient increased more when maceration was applied under sunny rather than 
overcast conditions.  The macerated windrows also lost water more rapidly in the early stages than 
in the later stages of drying.  Maceration had a pr olonged beneficial ef fect in dry areas such as 
Manitoba (May, 1994) and Queensland (Gupta el al ., 2002).  Night-tim e intervals, dew and rain 
attenuated the advantage of m aceration (Sundber g and Thylen 1994).  More m aceration rolls 
tended to result in a higher drying coefficient in similar environments environments. 
The crop density in the swath af fects the drying rate .  Spreading forage as wide as possible in the 
field increases the absorption area of solar radia tion for both conventional and m acerated swaths.  
Shinners et al. (1985) suggested that compressed mats should have a maximum thickness of 6 mm 
to achieve very rapid drying.  Savoie et al. (1992) estimated that the density should not be greater 
than 0.45 kg DM/m 2 for macerated alfalfa to dry down to le ss than 20% moisture within a day of 
sunshine.  With ryegrass, rapid drying was also  observed when the swath density was relatively 
low at 0.4 kg DM/m 2; dry hay could be obtained after en ergy absorption equivalent to a pan 
evaporation of 5 mm.   
Power requirement.  A comparison of six studies by Savoi e (2001) indicated a range of ener gy 
requirement between 2 and 9.2 kW •h/t DM.  The prototype with the most rolls, a 7-roll peripheral 
macerator, had the highest ener gy requirem ent.  Crushing im pact rolls and staggered rolls had 
similar ener gy requirem ents between 2 and 4 kW•h/t DM.  Besides ener gy for m aceration, 
additional energy is required to form  a m at.  This was estim ated as about 2 kW •h/t DM for the 
slatted conveyor press (see above).  The total energy to m acerate and com press can becom e a 
limitation, especially at high mowing rates.  
Other technical issues.   Research related to roll surface has received little attention.  The typical 
surface of peripheral or staggered rolls is formed by a series of longitudinal grooves with pointy or 
slightly flattened apexes.  Further evaluation w ould be needed to optim ize the roll surface versus 
the cost of fabrication of the rolls.  Beside s the geom etry of the roll surface, hardness and 
durability are other important properties.  Since most prototypes have been used only for a limited 
time, there are not enough data at this stage to compare various roll surfaces and their durability .  
Empirical evidence has shown that apexes of so ft iron rolls becom e dull and rounded within 100 
to 200 hours of use.  Therefore, steel hardening should be applied to m acerating rolls to prolong 
their useful life to that of typical mower-conditioners, i.e. 1000 to 2000 hours. 
The developm ent of various prototypes in dif ferent countries has m ade it dif ficult to com pare 
results because of differences in the level of mechanical conditioning.  A number of methods have 
been proposed to quantify m echanical conditioning:  dif fusion of K + ions in a water solution 
(Locus et al. 1994), com pressibility of forage unde r a fixed pressure (Sa voie et al. 1996) and the 
electrical conductivity of leachate obtained from  the conditioned forage (Kraus et al. 1999).  As 
 3
the forage is increasingly m acerated, ions and soluble constituents are m ore easily released.  
Similarly, the forage is m ore easily compressed.  There is not a single, standard m ethod currently 
used to assess the level of m echanical conditioning.  However , such a standard would facilitate 
comparison of maceration treatments at various sites. 
FEED VALUE OF MACERATED FORAGES 
Forage quality 
Maceration can af fect forage quality in several ways:  increased m echanical loss by shattering, 
reduced respiration loss as a result of rapid dryi ng, increased leaching loss if exposed to rain, and 
improved lactic ferm entation of silage.  Savoie (2001) reviewed several studies reporting forage 
quality after m aceration.  The fiber content rem ained constant in three studies and decreased in 
four others.  In general, maceration did not improve the chemical composition of forages; in some 
cases, it decreased the feed quality .   However , with sim ilar chem ical com position, m acerated 
forages tended to provide a better feed than unmacerated forage.  This is further discussed below.

The improved nutrient utilization after maceration can be explained partly by the increased broken 
crop area which f acilitates microbial colonization in the rumen.  In som e instances, an apparently 
lower f eed quality based on chem ical com position still resulted in equal or better anim al 
performance compared to control forage.   
Maceration is also thought to increase the retenti on time and the digestion of forage in the rum en 
because it is m ore com pressible.  Kraus et al. (1997) showed that rum inal digestion was m ore 
complete during the first 24 h for m acerated fora ge than for unm acerated forage.  Hintz et al. 
(1999) suggested that high perform ance animals with rapid feed turnover rates would be expected 
to benefit m ost from maceration while anim als at maintenance level will experience few if any 
nutritional benefit from  maceration.  With high yi elding cows (38 to 47 kg m ilk/d), Broderick et 
al. (1999) observed that m acerated alfalfa silage increased m ilk production in two trials out of 
four, and provided on average 4% m ore effective metabolizable energy (5.94 vs 5.69 MJ/kg) than 
control alfalfa silage.  With lower yielding cows (24 kg/d), Koegel et al. (1992b) observed no milk 
production increase, but macerated alfalfa silage still provided 11% more metabolizable energy as 
a result of a greater weight gain.   
FUTURE PERSPECTIVES 
Livestock producers.  Maceration has two m ajor advantages for livestock producers:  reduction of 
the field wilting time, and improvement of the forage feed value.  However, maceration may result 
 5
in a lower quality feed if the forage is exposed to rainfall or conserved as very wet silage.  Another 
disadvantage is the higher cost of a m ower-macerator com pared to a conventional m ower-
conditioner, because of the more complex mechanisms. 
Rotz et al. (1990) carried out an econom ic an alysis of m aceration on dairy farm s under the 
climatic conditions of Michigan.  Considering th e potential increase in feed value as a result of 
reduced field respiration and increased dry matter intake, they estimated that a dairy producer with 
100 cows could have a net incom e increase up to $15,000 because of substantially lower 
purchased feeds.  Another economic study carried  out in the United Kingdom  (McGechan et al. 
1993) estim ated that m aceration technology resulted in a higher benefit for farm ers with a hay 
conservation system than for farm ers with a sila ge conservation system .  As livestock producers 
move towards more silage and less hay, maceration technology faces a smaller market potential. 
Alfalfa pr ocessors and commer cial hay gr owers.  The production of alfalfa cubes and pellets 
requires artificial drying which has been estim ated to cost between $5 and $30 (U.S.) per ton 
depending on the price of natural gas and the m oisture content of  alf alfa delivered at the 
processing plant.  Savoie et al. (1995) carried  out a sim ulation study showing that m aceration 
could reduce the cost of artificial drying and pr ovide a net gain of $3 to $4/t.  Sim ilarly, 
commercial hay growers could benefit from  increased field drying rate provided by m aceration.  
However, the high risk of shatter and leaching lo ss has been a m ajor cause of lim ited interest for 
maceration by processors and commercial hay growers. 
Applied r esearch and development .  A few com mercial attem pts to develop m ower-macerators 
received som e attention in Europe in the early 1990' s (Deutz-Fahr 1993; Krone 1993).  Som e 
innovative conditioning devices, based on crushing, i.e.   less intensive than m aceration but m ore 
intensive than conventional roller conditioning, have been reported recently (Hill 1997; McMorran 
1999).  Recent research has proposed to separate  maceration from mowing and to apply it as a 
windrow handling treatm ent after a period of  wilting (Descôteaux and Savoie, 1999).  One 
company has commercialized such a pull-type macerator applied after mowing (Tietz, 2002). 
Maceration has been shown to im prove the fiel d drying rate and the forage feed value for 
ruminants in several experim ents.  However, maceration has also been shown to cause m ore field 
losses and reduce forage quality when windrows are subjected to rain, hum id conditions or 
excessive windrow handling.  Forage m aceration is therefore a technology m ost suitable to 
geographical areas with predictable weather patterns over several days and to livestock operations 
with high productivity.   
CONCLUSIONS 
1.  Various designs (peripheral rolls, staggered rolls, impact rolls) have been proposed to macerate 
forage.  Maceration can be im plemented on vari ous m owers (cutterbar , disk, pull-type, self-
propelled) or as a windrow handling m achine.  To m aintain high m ass-flow-rate, m aceration 
requires additional energy in the order of 2 to 4 kW•h/t DM processed. 
2.  Maceration has been shown to considerably im prove the field drying rate and improve the feed 
value of forage, especially under excellent drying conditions where the m oisture content declines 
rapidly.  Maceration can however reduce the feed quality of forages if it is followed by rain or 
humid conditions which extend the period for respiration and other field loss. 
3  The future of m aceration depends partly on further developm ent of a m echanism that is sim ple 
enough to m aintain low cost of fabrication and operation while still providing the ef fects of fast 
drying and im proved feed value.  Thermal or el ectromagnetic technologies m ight in the future 
represent a useful com plement to mechanical conditioning by providing a ra pid control of forage 
moisture while minimizing field loss.
