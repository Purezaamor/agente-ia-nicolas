Abstract
 
Light interception and use were studied in ferti-
lised stands of 5 
 
Brachiaria
 
 species: 
 
B. brizantha,
B. decumbens
 
, 
 
B. dictyoneura
 
,
 
 B. humidicola
 
 and
 
B. mutica
 
 over a regrowth period of 45 days
during the wet season. Aerial dry matter (DM)
accumulation, nutrient concentrations in the
forage, photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)
interception and light use efﬁciency (
 
ε
 
) for
different plant components were measured on
6 occasions. Growth and forage yield were
analysed in terms of biomass partitioning and its
inﬂuence on absorbed PAR and 
 
ε
 
. Final DM yields
were similar among species (280–350 g/m
 
2
 
),
despite wide variation (1.7–4.1) in the leaf area
index (LAI). In general, N accumulation into
aerial biomass was lower than theoretical values
expected for a C
 
4
 
 grass. Although 
 
ε
 
 for leaf pro-
duction and consequently LAI in 
 
B. humidicola
 
and 
 
B. mutica
 
 were low, 
 
ε
 
 for tiller production was
high, thus compensating for the lower partitioning
to assimilatory biomass. The pattern of biomass
partitioning in 
 
B. brizantha
 
, 
 
B. decumbens
 
 and
 
B. dictyoneura
 
 favoured leaf DM resulting in a
higher leaf:tiller ratio, LAI and 
 
ε
 
 for leaf biomass.
Different partitioning coefﬁcients resulted in
similar PAR interception among species. It was
concluded that similar ﬁnal yields were achieved
by different strategies of biomass partitioning and
therefore conversion efﬁciencies. The calculated
value of 
 
ε
 
 for total forage biomass fell within the
range 1.3–1.7 g/MJ.
 
Introduction 
 
For any crop and particularly for forage grasses,
both the rate at which a stand produces biomass
and the total amount accumulated by the time it
is harvested or grazed, depend on many environ-
mental and genetic factors. Success in pasture
management will depend, therefore, on how well
these interactions are understood.
Several authors have proposed a physiological
framework for the analysis of crop growth and
biomass accumulation (Monteith 1972, 1977;
Charles-Edwards 1982; Charles-Edwards 
 
et al
 
.
1986), which has been very helpful in deter-
mining causes of yield variation among C
 
4
 
grasses (Ludlow and Charles-Edwards 1980;
Sinoquet and Cruz 1993; Muchow 
 
et al
 
. 1994;
Maddonni and Otegui 1996; Robertson 
 
et al
 
.
1996).
Charles-Edwards (1982) and Charles-Edwards
 
et al
 
. (1986) consider that growth rate and eco-
nomic yield can be analysed biologically in terms
of 5 determinant factors which are common to all
crops: 1) the daily amount of photosynthetically
active radiation (PAR) intercepted by the leaf
canopy (J); 2) the efﬁciency (
 
ε
 
) with which light
energy is converted into biomass through photo-
synthesis (PAR use efﬁciency or RUE); 3) the dry
matter partitioning coefﬁcient into different plant
components (
 
η
 
); 4) the length of the growth
period; and ﬁnally 5) the rate of loss of dry
matter (V). If we consider only the above-ground
biomass, the net growth rate of a forage crop per
unit area over a time period (

Thus, under optimal or non-limiting condi-

the amount of PAR intercepted by the canopy,
the conversion efﬁciency for radiation intercep-
tion, and the length of the growing period are
the main factors controlling crop growth rate

and grasses, ranges for maximum growth
rates and annual production are typically
37–57 g/m

(Ludlow 1985; Squire 1990).
Under such environmental conditions and at
least during vegetative growth, forage biomass
accumulation is linearly related to the daily
amount of PAR absorbed by the stand foliage
(Monteith 1972), and depends primarily on the
degree of leaf area index (LAI) development
(Squire 1990). This relationship is maintained
until nearly 80–85% of radiation is intercepted,
which usually starts to occur at LAI values
around 4–5 for most crops and forage grasses
(Charles-Edwards 
 
et al
 
. 1986; Humphreys 1991;
Sinoquet and Cruz 1993; Maddonni and Otegui
1996). Differences in J among forage stands are
attributed to differences in the efﬁciency of light
interception, which in turn depends on canopy
architecture, and hence LAI vertical distribution
through the canopy (Ludlow and Charles-
Edwards 1980; Sinoquet and Cruz 1993).
Under optimal environments, the efﬁciency of
light conversion appears also to be relatively
more stable among tropical legumes and grasses
than J (Ludlow 1985; Charles-Edwards 
 
et al
 
.
1986; Squire 1990), but it can be substantially
modiﬁed by environmental factors (Cruz 1995,
1996; Jeuffroy and Ney 1997; Sinclair and
Muchow 1999; Black and Ong 2000).
The advantage of this physiological approach
for the analysis of crop growth is that it allows a
comparison of the performance of grass stands
under different agronomic situations, as related to
2 parameters (J and 
 
ε
 
), which are fairly easy to
quantify under ﬁeld conditions. 
In the present study, we applied this growth-
analysis technique to compare growth, dry matter
accumulation patterns, radiation interception and
light-use efﬁciency in 5 
 
Brachiaria
 
 species under
fertilised conditions in a tropical environment. 
 
Materials and methods
 
Location, species, plot management and 
harvesting 
 
The ﬁeld experiment was conducted at the experi-
mental station of the Universidad Central de
Venezuela (UCV), Maracay (10°15
 
′
 
N, 67°39´W;
elevation 450 masl), on a sandy loam soil, during
the peak of the wet period (July–September) of
1999. The climate is classiﬁed as dry to subhumid,
with a monthly mean temperature of 24.6°C and
a mean annual rainfall (1973–2001) of 980 mm
and potential evapotranspiration (1980–1998) of
1359 mm. Nearly 90% of the annual rainfall falls
in April–November (meteorological service of the
Agronomy Faculty, UCV). Weather data were
collected from a meteorological station located
near the site. An initial soil analysis gave the
following characteristics (0–20 cm): pH-6.8;
organic matter-1.34%; electrical conductivity-
0.13 dS/m; P, K, Ca and Na concentrations of 42,
100, 954 and 30 ppm, respectively. 
For this study, 5 species of 
 
Brachiaria
 
 were
used: 
 
B. brizantha
 
,
 
 B. decumbens, B. dicty-
oneura
 
,
 
 B. humidicola
 
 and
 
 B. mutica
 
. The
grasses had been established 3 years before in
9 m 
 
×
 
 5 m plots, and were frequently mowed
and fertilised to allow continuous stand regrowth
and full plant cover. For the purpose of this
experiment, all plots were mowed on July 10,
1999 and 2 days after they were fertilised with a
complete formula of N:P:K (12:24:12) at a rate
of 150 kg/ha.
Aerial biomass production was measured at
weekly intervals (8, 17, 22, 29, 39 and 45 days
after mowing, DAM) using a single 1 m 
 
×
 
 0.5 m
quadrat placed at random within each plot. A new
sampling site was used on each occasion. Weed
control was performed manually. No insect attack
was observed during the experiment, which
extended for 45 days: July 10–August 24.
 
Plant and light measurements

each quadrat was collected, and a subsample was
separated into green leaves, tillers (stem + leaf
sheath) and dead matter. A subsample of green
leaves (200–350 cm
 
2
 
) was also taken for determi-
nation of speciﬁc leaf area (SLA). The area of
leaf lamina was measured by using a leaf area
meter (CI-202; CID Inc., USA) before drying all

Mineral nutrition of the pastures was moni-

comparing the theoretical relationship between N

the actual curve obtained from the experimental
data (Cruz 1997). 
A randomised block design with 3 replicates
was used, and the proper ANOVA test and mean
comparisons (Duncan, P<0.05) were performed

lyse the relationship between forage yield and
accumulated PAR interception, and the slopes
were compared by Duncan’s test using the SAS-
GLM-MCSLO procedure. The value of k was
compared among species by analysing the linear
regressions of ln (T) on LAI. The SAS-NLIN
procedure was used to ﬁt experimental data to
exponential and polynomial equations.

12 MJ/m
 
2

Discussion 
All grasses showed a similar dry matter
accumulation pattern, with ﬁnal yields around
2800–3500 kg/ha for a 6-week regrowth period.
Both rainfall and temperature were considered
very favourable for growth so there was contin-
uous biomass accumulation and minimum plant
senescence. These yields were similar to those
reported in different agronomic evaluations
carried out in neotropical savannas under
medium-high fertility conditions (Mata 1989;
Navarro et al. 1992; Pietrosemoli et al. 1996). 
Estimation of absolute rates of regrowth gave
values around 71–97 kg/ha/d. This range is
similar to that obtained by Navarro et al. (1992)
in B. humidicola  (82–92 kg/ha/d) for a period of
42–70 days of regrowth, and the maximum value
(101 kg/ha/d) reported by Tejo et al . (1985) in
Dichanthium annulatum  after 2 months of
regrowth. However, these values are well below

grasses (Monteith 1978; Piedade et al . 1991;
Kubota et al. 1994). 
Concentrations of P and K in the shoot bio-
mass showed no apparent limitation for growth,
since they were always above the critical levels
reported by Rao et al . (1998) for these species.
In the case of N, the overall mean value of
1.4 ± 0.6% for the regrowth period is also similar
to that reported for these and other forage
grasses grown on infertile soils but with
medium-high N fertilisation levels (Navarro
et al . 1992; Pietrosemoli et al . 1996; Rao et al .
1998; Pérez-Silva et al . 1999). However, the
observed common pattern of N uptake by the
grasses indicated an obvious low N availability
for a given level of accumulated above-ground
biomass (Cruz 1997). Theoretically, critical N
concentrations for total forage yield in C 4
grasses are above 2% (Lemaire et al . 1989;
Greenwood et al . 1990). This may explain the
lower absolute growth rates reported here when
compared with the maximum values mentioned
above. In savanna soils with low natural fertility,
this critical N percentage is usually reached with
only very high levels ( i.e. ≥ 200 kg/ha/yr) of N
fertilisation (Pietrosemoli et al. 1996). Addition-
ally, these Brachiaria species are reputed to be
well adapted to soils with restricted N conditions
(Rao et al . 1998), so we can only speculate
whether growth and hence biomass accumulation
in these species were strongly limited by
N availability. 
Differences in the pattern of leaf and tiller dry
matter accumulation with time were related to
growth habit. Thus, in the case of B. brizantha,
more assimilates were devoted to leaves, so the
pattern of above-ground biomass accumulation
was very similar to that observed for leaves. This
was supported by the higher leaf:tiller ratio
observed in this species. In contrast, the high
proportion of tillers and stolons in B. mutica
meant that patterns of accumulation of above-
ground biomass and tillers were similar. In
B. humidicola  and B. dictyoneura , leaf produc-
tion and hence LAI growth were reduced by initi-
ation of ﬂowering. For these species, it is evident
that ﬂowering induction may in some cases even
reduce total biomass accumulation by the end of
the growing period and consequently reduce
potential forage production during this time
of the year. Flowering induction may then be the
reason for B. humidicola and B. dictyoneura
producing relatively lower forage yields within
the range observed in this study. Some Brachi-
aria species are recognised as long-day plants so
they usually ﬂower during the wet period in the
neotropics (Hopkinson et al. 1998).

different growth habits may be explained by the

interaction between biomass partitioning and
light interception and use. Slow-growing species
like B. humidicola  and B. dictyoneura  accumu-
lated lower LAI (1.7–2.5), which was also associ-
ated with more competition for assimilates by
reproductive structures. However, these 2 species
produced the same DM yield as B. decumbens
and B. brizantha , which developed maximum
LAI of 3.5–4.1. In this case, an increase in LAI
was not directly related to higher radiation inter-
ception due to the different light extinction
coefﬁcients among the species. The lower LAI in
B. humidicola and B. dictyoneura was apparently
compensated for by a better vertical distribution
of light within the canopy (0.3<k<0.5). This
implies a pattern of mostly erect leaves and
this feature is considered as the main reason for
the high productivity in Pennisetum purpureum
(Kubota et al . 1994). Light attenuation rate was
very high (k = 0.8) in stands of B. mutica, which
may be related to a high proportion of horizontal
leaves. However, according to ﬁeld observations,
this characteristic seemed not to be present, so
the relatively high k value is perhaps explained
by the presence of a large amount of stemmy
material like stolons contributing to high light
interception in this species. A huge proportion of
non-photosynthetic structures can strongly
increase k (Asner et al. 1998), so estimating light
interception in these forage grasses may need
more detailed measurements of radiation inter-
ception by leaves. In the stoloniferous grass
Digitaria decumbens, a k value of 0.87 was con-
sidered high by Sinoquet and Cruz (1993) when
compared with other grasses (0.5–0.6). Ludlow
(1985) summarised information from other
studies, where k values for C 4 grasses like
Panicum maximum  var. trichoglume, Setaria
sphacelata and D. decumbens  were 0.4–0.7, 0.6
and 0.9, respectively. 
The other physiological determinant of grass
yield according to Equation 2 is ε. The range
obtained in this study (1.3–1.7 g shoot dry
matter/MJ PAR) was higher than the one reported
for D. decumbens  at a low N supply (Sinoquet
and Cruz 1993), but closer to 2.1 g/MJ when this
species was cultivated in mixtures with forage
legumes (Cruz and Sinoquet 1994). For forage
grasses like P. maximum , B. decumbens  and
D. aristatum , light use efﬁciency under fertilised
and well watered conditions was 1.6–2.3 g/MJ
(Wilson and Ludlow 1991; Cruz 1995).
The range is still lower than the potential
value (2.5–4.0 g/MJ) proposed for C 4 species
under optimal growth conditions (Goose et al .
1986; Sinclair and Muchow 1999). This again
may be attributed to the low level of N nutrition
experienced by these forage grasses. However, it
is similar to more realistic values reported for
other C 4 grasses (Sinclair and Muchow 1999),
such as maize (1.6–1.7 g/MJ), sorghum
(1.2–1.4 g/MJ) and sugar cane (1.8–2.0 g/MJ). 
Though statistically similar among species,
the relatively higher values for εT (1.5–1.7 g/MJ)
found in B. humidicola  and B. mutica may have
compensated for the lower LAI. In these species,
equivalent ﬁnal yields in comparison with the
other Brachiaria species were partially achieved
by a higher efﬁciency of radiation conversion
into tiller biomass. In B. brizantha, B. decumbens
and to a lesser extent, B. dictyoneura , relatively
lower εT values (1.3–1.4 g/MJ) were apparently
compensated for by a greater εL which in turn
resulted in a higher LAI growth and perhaps radi-
ation interception for a given k value. A high LAI
development was a consequence of a higher pro-
portion of assimilates diverted into assimilatory
tissue. In agronomic terms, a pasture containing a
tufted grass such as B. brizantha , with a high
leaf:stem ratio, is more suitable for grazing, since
leaﬁness increases pasture intake and the amount
of good quality forage to be ingested by the
animal (Humphreys 1991). However, stolonif-
erous grasses such as B. humidicola  and
B. mutica , though having a lower L:T ratio
and hence less feeding value for livestock, may
be able to withstand intensive grazing by having
a great number of meristems well below bite
height. 
We concluded that, under N-limiting condi-
tions, an apparent compensatory effect between
radiation interception efﬁciency and ε resulted in
similar ﬁnal yields among these forage grasses.
