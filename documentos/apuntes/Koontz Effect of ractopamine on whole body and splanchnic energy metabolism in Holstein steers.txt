study was designed to examine the influence of ractopamine (RAC) on whole-body and splanchnic energy balance. Six
growing Holstein steers [body weight (BW) /C30402939.5 kg] surgically fitted with an arterial and portal, hepatic, and
mesenteric venous indwellingcatheters were used in a repeated measures study. Treatments were a basal diet of alfalfa

days 22/C142). On day 14 of each period, splanchnic and portal-drained viscera (PDV) energy balances were determined as
the product of arterio-venous O2 difference and blood flow. Blood flow was determined usingdown-stream dilution of
p-aminohippuric acid. Whole-body energy balance was determined on days 15/C121 of each period, which included 7 d total
excreta collection and 3 d of respiratory gas exchange measurements. Body weight and DM intake were greater (PB0.05)
for steers receivingRAC compared with those receivingthe control diet; however, no difference was observed in either BW
or DMI when expressed on a BW0.75 basis. Similarly, as a function of BW0.75, whole-body heat production (691 kJ kg
BW0.75 d/C281; P/C300.96) and retained N (0.85 gkgBW 0.75 d/C281; P/C300.34) and energy (298 kJ kg BW0.75 d/C11; P/C300.71) were
unaffected by RAC. In contrast, RAC tended to decrease (P /C300.09) energy use by splanchnic tissues (191 vs. 156 kJ kg
BW0.75 d/C281), largely due to a reduction (P/C300.12) in energy use by the PDV (100 vs. 86 kJ kg BW0.75 d/C281). These data
indicate that although whole-body energy use is not affected by RAC, energy use by splanchnic tissues is decreased,
thereby increasingenergy use by peripheral tissues.

Adrenergic receptor agonists are classified by the type of
receptor to which they bind. The two main classes of
adrenergic receptors are a- and b-; furthermore, there
are three beta- subtypes; b1AR, b2AR, and b3AR. Each
receptor subtype is located in a different organ and
performs different functions within the body (Deblasi
1990). Ractopamine is a b2AR, approved for use in
finishingbeef cattle. Early research with beta-adrenergic
receptor (bAR) agonists in livestock showed that oral
administration could increase muscle mass and reduce
fat deposition in sheep (Baker et al. 1984), swine (Jones
et al. 1985), poultry (Dalrymple et al. 1984), and cattle
(Ricks et al. 1984). This action leads bAR agonist
compounds to be classified as repartitioningagents.
However, the mechanisms by which these effects occur
have not been fully defined.
Most research has focused on the increase in lean
tissue mass that results from bAR agonist administra-
tion. Studies have shown hypertrophy of muscle fibers
occurs when feedingractopamine without a correspond-
ingincrease in number of muscle fibers (Aalhus et al.
1992). Additionally, research has indicated that RNA
concentrations are increased in the muscle tissue of
animals treated with bAR agonists (Grant et al. 1993;
Koohmaraie et al. 1991; Smith et al. 1989). Some studies
also show a reduction in muscle degradation as one of
the mechanisms of bAR agonist action (Eadara et al.
1989b; Wheeler and Koohmaraie 1992).
Other research has focused on the mechanisms by
which adipose mass is reduced. These studies have
shown that the reduced fat deposition may be explained
by increased lipolysis (Chwaliboget al. 1996; Mitchell
et al. 1991; Thornton et al. 1985), in combination with
decreased lipogenesis (Blum et al. 1982; Eisemann et al.
1988).
Further research has focused on the indirect effects
of bAR agonist administration. Williams et al. (1987)
found that ractopamine reduced organ mass in grow-
ingsteers. Such reductions may account for N and
energy savings, which allow for increased nutrient
availability to muscle tissues. Additionally, there is
evidence that bAR agonists increase blood flow to
skeletal muscles (Beermann et al. 1987; Eisemann
et al. 1988). This allows increased substrate availabil-
ity to skeletal muscle (Eisemann et al. 1988), as well
as promotingremoval of non-esterified fatty acids
from adipose tissues and thereby enhancinglipid
degradation (Mersmann 1995).
The majority of research related to ractopamine has
focused on tissue and cellular level effects or carcass
composition changes. Little attention has been given to
changes in energy metabolism on the organ and whole-
body levels. In order to optimize the effects of bAR
agonist administration, it is necessary to fully under-
stand its alterations to metabolism. The goal of this
experiment was to determine if there is an alteration in
whole body or splanchnic energy utilization in growing
steers as a result of oral ractopamine (RAC) adminis-
tration.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
All procedures involvinganimals were approved by
the University of Kentucky Animal Care and Use
Committee.
Animals
Six Holstein steers (average weight 402939.6 kg) were
utilized in a repeated measures experiment. The experi-
mental phase was 48 d in length and consisted of a
control period (days 1/C128; CON), followed by a
ractopamine test period (days 29/C148; RAC). Through-
out the experiment, the diet (Table 1) consisted of alfalfa
cubes top-dressed with a mineral pre-mix and polox-
alene (Bloat-Guard; Phibro Animal Health; Ridgefield
Park,NJ). The diet wasfedtosupply831kJkgBW /C280.75
d/C281 to meet approximately 1.5 /C29 the maintenance
energy requirements of the steers [NEm /C300.33 MJ
BW/C280.75 d/C281 ; National Research Council (NRC)
2001]. Duringthe second period, ractopamine (RAC;
Optaflexx; Elanco Animal Health; Greenfield, IN) was
added at the maximum dose recommended by the
manufacturer, 430 mghead /C281 d/C281. To provide steady-
state conditions duringoxygen flux measures, steers
were fed equal portions every 2 h duringthe experi-
ments. Duringthe nutrient balance trial, steers were fed
once daily at 0800 to facilitate uninterrupted 24 h
measures of respiratory gas exchange. Feed samples
were collected daily by random grab sampling and
composited over each period. Steers had ad libitum
access to water throughout the experiment. Steers were
housed in environmentally controlled rooms with an
average temperature of 218C and 35% humidity. The
light cycle was maintained at 16 h light:8 h dark
throughout the experiment.

Blood Flow and Splanchnic O2 Consumption
Steers were surgically fitted with chronic indwelling
catheters in the mesenteric artery, mesenteric vein,
hepatic portal vein, and hepatic vein (Harmon et al.
1991). Arterial, portal, and hepatic blood samples were
collected every 45 min from 1200 to 1730 on days 20 and
41 in syringes void of air and capped with rubber.
Samples were immediately analyzed for O2 saturation
and hemoglobin concentration (GEM Premier 3000;
Instrumentation Laboratories; Orangeburg, NY). Blood
flow was determined by a continuous infusion of
p-aminohippurate (PAH; 250 mmol; 1 mL min/C281) into
the mesenteric venous catheter (Harmon et al. 1991). O2
saturation and hemoglobin concentration of blood
samples were used to calculate oxygen use and heat
production (HP) by the splanchnic bed and portal
drained viscera as described below.
Nitrogen and Energy Balance
The excreta collection phase consisted of a 7d nutrient
balance phase where total fecal and urine collection was
performed. On the middle three days (days 3, 4, and 5)
the steers were placed in head-boxes for indirect
calorimetry data collection, as described below. Feces
and urine were collected at 0900 daily.
Continuous suction usinga rubber funnel system
attached to the ventral portion of the abdomen allowed
collection of urine into a plastic collection vessel. Urine
acidity was reduced to pH B3 by adding1 /C281.5 L of
a 23.5% solution of H3PO4 to the collection vessel in
order to prevent ammonia N loss. Urine output weight
was recorded daily. A constant percentage was sub-
sampled daily to contribute approximately 200 gto a
composite for each period and steer. This composite was
stored at 08C until analysis.
The wet weight of fecal output was recorded daily for
each steer. A subsample was taken at a constant
percentage to give approximately 2 kg wet matter per
day. Daily fecal samples were frozen at 08C until the end
of the experiment, when they was composited by period
and steer usinga Hobart mixer (Model H-600, Hobart
ManufacturingCo, Troy, OH). The composite samples
were stored at 08C for future analysis. Fecal samples
were dried daily in a 1008C forced-air oven to determine
daily fecal dry-matter output [Association of Officail
Agricultural Chemists (AOAC) 1975].
Duringthe indirect calorimetry phase, respiratory gas
exchange was measured for three consecutive 24-h
periods in head-box style respiration chambers. Each
head-boxwasconstructedofastainlesssteelframe;three
wallswerecomposedofplexiglasswindows,oneofwhich
washingedtoallowaccesstotheanimal’sheadforfeeding
andmonitoringofconditions.Thefourthwasfitted with
a large opening through which the animal’s head was
placed into the chamber. The openingwas of sufficient
size to allow the animal to stand up or lie down easily.
A canvas sheath extendingfrom the openingwas placed
aroundtheanimal’sneckandwastiedtightlyaroundthe
baseoftheneck,justinfrontoftheshoulder.Thecanvas
sheath and negative air flow within the head-box pre-
vented respiratory gas escape from the chamber. Each
head-box was fitted with a waterer, feeder, and air-
conditioningunit to maintain consistent temperature
(218C) and relative humidity (35%). Air flow from the
head-boxes was determined by individual mass flow
meters (Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH) and
was maintained at 600 L min/C281 duringmeasures of
respiratory gas exchange. Measures of inspired and
expired O2,CO2, and CH4 were collected at 9-min
intervals usingOxymax software (Columbus Instru-
ments, Columbus, OH). Prior to use, the O2 and CO2
recoveries for the head-boxes were determined to be
96.5%forO2 and98.3%forCO2.Feedintakeduringthe
calorimetryphasewasmaintainedatthesameamountas
in the excreta collection phase.
Chemical Analyses
Composite feed and fecal samples were dried at 608Cin
a forced-air oven, ground through a 1-mm screen in a
Wiley mill (Model 4; Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro,
NJ). The dried ground samples were analyzed for dry
matter and ash (AOAC 1975). Fiber analysis to
determine NDF and ADF was performed usingthe
detergent analysis system (Goering and Van Soest 1970)
modified for the ANKOM 200 fiber unit (ANKOM
Technology Corperation, Fairport, NY). Heat of com-
bustion was determined for feed, feces, and urine (Parr
1281 Bomb Calorimeter, Moline, IL). To measure urine
GE, samples were oven-dried for 2 d at 558Cin
polyethylene bags (Jeb Plastics Inc., Wilmington, DE)
before combustion (Wilkerson et al. 1997). To obtain
the urinary energy contents, the known heat of combus-
tion per gram of bag material was subtracted from the
total heat measured. Carbon and nitrogen content of
dried feed and feces and wet urine were determined
usingthe C:N method on an Elementar varioMAX
(Elementar Analysensysteme, Hanau, Germany).

the difference between total retained energy (RE) and

Statistical Analysis
The data were analyzed as repeated measures with the
Mixed procedure of SAS (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC,
2007) software, with period in the repeated statement
and animal as random effect. The statistical model used
individual steer as the experimental unit. Data are
presented as least squares means9SE. Effects were
considered significant at P 5 0.05 and a tendency for
significance at P 5 0.1. The experimental treatments
were confounded by time as all steers were used first in
the control period and second in the ractopamine
period. The experiment was designed in this fashion in
order to remove the possibility of long-term carryover
effects of ractopamine treatment. As a result of this
confoundingwith time, there was an increase in body
weight (BW) and dry matter intake for all steers during
the ractopamine period (Table 2). Dietary intake was a
function of metabolic body weight (MBW; BW0.75), by
experimental design, thus results were analyzed on
MBW basis to normalize for intake. Data related to
splanchnic energy use were analyzed on an absolute
basis and as a function of MBW, as one of the effects of
b-agonist administration is alteration of accretion pat-
terns with little to no change in organ or bone mass
(Beermann 2002). This action may cause a change in the
ratio of viscera to body mass, skewingthe splanchnic
energy results when presented as a function of MBW.
Duringthe experiment, portal and hepatic vein catheters
in two animals lost patency, one of these animals and a
third were excluded from the calorimetric analyses due
to a malfunction of the oxygen analyzer. These animals
were excluded from the data set where appropriate.

Energy Balance
As with nitrogen, there were no effects of ractopamine
on energy balance or energy excretion route (Table 5).
Ractopamine treatment did not affect digestible (P /C30
0.74) or metabolizable energy (P /C300.57) intakes. Addi-
tionally, there was no change (P /C300.96) in HP between
periods; 691 vs. 693 kJ d/C281 kg/C280.75, respectively (CON
and RAC). Ultimately, there was no difference (P ]
0.71) in whole-body energy retention due to treatment
with ractopamine.
Composition of Energy Gain
The depots for energy retention were defined as total
protein and fat (Table 6), no division was made between
protein accreted as organ tissue or muscle, nor was there
determination of location of adipose gain or loss. The
addition of ractopamine to the diet resulted in a 7%
increase in energy retained as protein, but this was not
statistically significant (P /C300.34). Energy retained as fat
was not different (P /C300.67) between treatments. The
percentages of energy retained as protein (41 vs. 47%)
and fat (58 vs. 52%) were also not affected by
ractopamine treatment (P /C300.64).
Splanchnic Energy Use
Addition of ractopamine to the diet resulted in a
tendency for a decrease (35kJ d/C281 kg/C280.75, P /C300.09)
in energy use by the splanchnic tissues (Table 7). PDV
energy use was numerically lower (P /C300.12) with
ractopamine treatment as compared with the control,
while energy used by the hepatic tissues was unchanged
(P /C300.64).
DISCUSSION
In the current study, there was no effect of ractopamine
on the digestibility of nitrogen or energy. Rikhardsson
et al. (1991) also found no change in digestibility of
protein or energy when feeding cimaterol to lambs.
Cimaterol is another b2-agonist; it binds to the same
receptor type and sub-type as ractopamine, in addition
to havingsimilar effects on muscle and fat deposition in
cattle (Chikhou et al. 1993), though small differences
can be seen in the timing, intensity, and length of action
(Chikhou et al. 1993; Byrem et al. 1998). In contrast,
Walker et al. (2007) found that feedingractopamine to
6-mo-old Holstein steers resulted in increased dry-
matter digestibility of a limit-fed corn based diet (76.9
vs. 79.0%). They postulated that b-agonists may act to
reduce gastrointestinal tract contractions, thus allowing
for alongerfermentationand digestion time, resulting in

Higher dietary levels of ractopamine have also shown

of Walker et al. (2007) was confounded by time, and,

while non-carcass retained nitrogen decreased. This
resulted in no net change in N-retention. It is possible
that a similar action occurred in the present experiment;
however, as no slaughter data was taken, this possibility
cannot be verified.
The steers in this study were limit fed, but comparison
with previous research shows that the level of nitrogen
intake was comparable with that of similar studies
(Abney et al. 2007; Gruber et al. 2007; Walker et al.
2007). Likewise, energy intake on a MBW basis was
similar to previous research (Eisemann and Bristol
1998). Evaluation of the current diet with Cornell Net
Carbohydrate and Protein System 6.1 (Cornell Univer-
sity, Ithaca, NY) showed that energy was the first
limitingnutrient and would therefore control the rate
of tissue deposition.
Accordingto the NRC (1994) for beef cattle, Holstein
steers grow at a reduced rate as compared with typical
beef breeds. Holstein steers can have an average daily
gain (ADG) of up to 1.5 kg d/C281 (DiCostanzo 2005), but
Miller et al. (1986) found that increasingthe alfalfa
content of the diet to 75% could significantly reduce
ADG in Holstein steers, to as low as 0.91 kgd /C281. The
current study utilized alfalfa cubes as the sole source of
dietary nitrogen and energy, and the steers gained an
average of 1.0 kg d/C281 duringthe control period. Thus, it
is possible that duringthe control period, the animals
were already accretinglean tissue at a maximal effi-
ciency for the given diet and the addition of ractopamine
to the diet could not increase nitrogen retention or
muscle gain as it customarily does, without requiring an
increased energy intake. Reeds and Mersmann (1991),
argue that if animals are already gaining at a maximal
level, b-agonist administration may not be able to
increase muscle growth beyond the biological max-
imums. It is possible that studies done with animals at
different stages of growth may show different levels of
beta-agonist activity due to the increased degree of
alteration possible. For example, mature animals accret-
ingalmost wholly adipose would have greater potential
for ractopamine to alter energy and nutrient use from
adipose to lean accretion as compared to growing
animals already accretingmuscle at a high rate.
Ractopamine is believed to be most effective when
given in conjunction with diets that have an excess of
dietary protein (Reeds and Mersmann 1991). Due to the
muscle-specific increases seen with bAR agonist treat-
ment, it is possible that the ratio of amino acids utilized
for muscle and adipose accretion by the body is altered
with their addition to a diet. Methionine and phenyla-
lanine are the limitingamino acids in muscle deposition
(Bohe et al. 2003), thus an increase in the dietary levels
of these amino acids may be necessary in order to see the
full effects of bAR agonist administration.
The data from this study do not allow for differentia-
tion of ractopamine effects as changes in synthesis,
degradation, or repartitioning. Ractopamine may have
functioned as a partitioningagent, alteringthe ratio of
nutrient use by specific tissue beds, without increasing
whole-body retention. The 14% increase in energy
retained as protein as a percentage of total RE, while
there was no change (P /C300.71) in whole body energy
retention, may provide evidence for partitioningaction.
Previous studies have shown similar results without
increased total energy gain. Hereford steers treated with
10mghead /C281 d/C281 ofclenbuterol,another b2ARagonist
with similar function to ractopamine (Strydom et al.
2009), showed no difference in slaughter weight (447 vs.
436 kg), but had increased protein percentage of the
carcass and reduced carcass fat percentage (Ricks et al.
1984). It is unclear from this study if this effect was due
to repartitioningof energy already present in fat to
muscle tissue, or if it is preferential partitioningof
dietary energy to muscle over fat.
Evidence of energy partitioning in the current experi-
ment can be found in the alteration of energy use by the
splanchnic tissues. The 13% reduction in splanchnic
energy use observed in steers consuming RAC was
primarily a function of a decrease in PDV energy use
rather than hepatic energy use, or an even reduction in
both tissue beds. The energy savings by the splanchnic
tissues is equivalent to a 5% increase in energy use
by peripheral tissues. Eisemann and Bristol (1998) used
a hyperinsulinemic, euglycemic clamp technique on
Hereford-Angus cross steers to examine oxygen use by
various tissue beds. They found that ractopamine
addition at 80 mgkg /C281 feed increased blood flow to

However, they found that oxygen uptake by the liver
was decreased, but uptake by the PDV was unchanged,

The reduction seen in splanchnic energy use in this
experiment is most likely related to a decrease in the
mass of these tissues (McLeod and Baldwin 2000). Such
a change in mass would contribute to the partitioning
effects of ractopamine and other bAR agonists. As
much as 25% of whole-body energy use (McBride and
Kelly 1990), up to 65% or whole-body protein synthesis
(MacRae et al. 1997), and approximately 65% of total
post-absorptive amino acid catabolism (Hanigan 2005)
can be attributed to GIT tissues. A reduction in organ
size would cause the viscera to require fewer nutrients
for maintenance, thereby reducingthe amount of energy
and nutrients needed for tissue maintenance and turn-
over (Reynolds 2002). This would allow additional
nutrients to be available for protein accretion in other
areas of the body without alteringwhole body nitrogen,
carbon, or energy balance. Evidence for this theory was
found by Reeds et al. (1986) who reported that long-
term treatment with clenbuterol reduced liver and
kidney growth in mice. Eismann and Huntington
(1993) reported that clenbuterol increased blood flow
to the liver, PDV, and hindquarter of Hereford steers
with no increase in oxygen uptake by the liver or PDV.
They concluded that clenbuterol treatment resulted in a
lower contribution by the PDV to total body energy use.
Similarly, data from the current research shows that
ractopamine treatment reduced energy use in the
splanchnic tissues by 2243 kJ d/C281. Consideringthat
protein averages 23.8 kJ g/C281, this correlates to a
theoretical maximum of 94.24 gof increased protein
available accretion each day. This theoretical value is
not realistically attainable due to the cost of accretion,
increased turnover, and other mechanisms; however,
it gives an estimate of the protein accretion possible with
ractopamine supplementation.
The current research shows no effect of ractopamine
on whole body oxygen use, carbon dioxide production,
or energy loss as methane. This is in agreement with
previous findings in ruminants. Hereford-Angus steers
fed ractopamine showed no change in whole body O2
utilization (Eisemann and Bristol 1998). Rikhardsson
et al. (1991) also found no change in CH4 energy losses
or RQ in lambs fed cimaterol.
Ractopamine treatment had no effect on total HP in
this experiment. In previous research where sheep were
given cimaterol, they showed an immediate increase in
daily HP; however, by day 10 HP decreased to
pretreatment levels (Rikhardsson et al. 1991). In this
experiment, the steers were on ractopamine treatment
for 16 d before calorimetry data collection was begun.
It is possible that if the calorimetric data collection
occurred earlier in the current study a similar effect
could have been seen. Similar temporal effects have also
been seen in relation to nitrogen retention (Rikhardsson
et al. 1991) and muscle weight gain (Reeds et al. 1986).
However, there are data indicatingthat long-term
calorimetric effects of bAR agonists are discernible.
Williams et al. (1987) found a significant increase in HP
when clenbuterol was administered to veal calves at 20
mgkgBW /C281 for 106 d. However, brown adipose tissue
is known to be present at higher levels in veal calves and
bAR agonists have been shown to raise the energy
expenditure in brown adipose tissue (Blum and Flueck-
iger 1988). Thus, this mechanism is not likely to account
for any changes in HP seen in more mature animals.
Williams et al. (1987) reported a reduction in liver size
(P B0.05) in veal calves fed cimaterol. Pigs fed racto-
pamine also showed decreased weight (P B0.05) of
heart, lung, kidney and pancreas (Yen et al. 1991). As
organs have considerably higher metabolic rates than
skeletal muscle and account for 30/C2860% of whole body
energy needs (Reynolds et al. 1991), this tendency for
a reduction in visceral organ mass may offset the
increased heat production that would normally be
associated with bAR agonist treatment, as a result of
increased muscle mass and weight gain, thus accounting
for the lack of change in heat production values seen
in the current experiment. In addition, a reduction in
visceral organ size, and the resulting reduction in
nitrogen and energy needs for these tissues, may also
account for part of the unchanged nitrogen and energy
retentions seen with the addition of ractopamine.
CONCLUSIONS
The addition of ractopamine to the diet of growing
Holstein steers resulted in no net change in whole-body
energy or nitrogen balance after 21 d of feeding.
However, there was a reduction in energy use by the
splanchnic tissues. This reduction was primarily due to a
decrease in energy use by the portal drained viscera, not
the hepatic tissues. The decline in splanchnic energy use
without alteringwhole-body energy balance may in-
dicate an increase in energy available for use by
peripheral tissues. We postulate that this corresponds
to an increase in muscle tissue accretion. However,
muscle mass was not measured in the present study,
though we did note a slight increase in total energy
retained as protein, while energy retained as fat was
unchanged. Based on this research, whole body energy
utilization is unaffected by ractopamine, while splanch-
nic energy use is decreased, thereby allowing an increase
in energy available for use by peripheral tissues.
