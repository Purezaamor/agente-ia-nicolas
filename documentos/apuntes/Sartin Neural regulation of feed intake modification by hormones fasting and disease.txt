ABSTRACT: Appetite is a complex process that re-
sults from the integration of multiple signals at the hy-
pothalamus. The hypothalamus receives neural signals; 
hormonal signals such as leptin, cholecystokinin, and 
ghrelin; and nutrient signals such as glucose, FFA, AA, 
and VFA. This effect is processed by a specific sequence 
of neurotransmitters beginning with the arcuate nucle-
us and orexigenic cells containing neuropeptide Y or 
agouti-related protein and anorexigenic cells contain-
ing proopiomelanocortin (yielding the neurotransmitter 
α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone) or cells expressing 
cocaine amphetamine-related transcript. These so-
called first-order neurons act on second-order orexi-
genic neurons (containing either melanin-concentrating 
hormone or orexin) or act on anorexigenic neurons 
(e.g., expressing corticotropin-releasing hormone) to 
alter feed intake. In addition, satiety signals from the 
liver and gastrointestinal tract signal through the vagus 
nerve to the nucleus tractus solitarius to cause meal ter-
mination, and in combination with the hypothalamus, 
integrate the various signals to determine the feeding 
response. The activities of these neuronal pathways are 
also influenced by numerous factors such as nutrients, 
fasting, and disease to modify appetite and hence affect 
growth and reproduction. This review will begin with 
the central nervous system pathways and then discuss 
the ways in which hormones and metabolites may alter 
the process to affect feed intake with emphasis on farm 
animals.
Key words:  appetite, cholecystokinin, ghrelin, leptin, neuropeptide Y, satiety

INTRODUCTION
Feed intake is a critical process for the normal growth 
and reproduction of mammals. The process leading to 
alterations in feed intake result from a complicated 
interplay between a large variety of factors, such as 
feed availability and quality, circulating concentrations 
of hormones and metabolites, as well as physical re-
sponses of the gastrointestinal system, all integrated 
by the central nervous system (CNS) to yield the final 
physical act of eating. In addition, there is a critical dif-
ference between grazing ruminant species where nutri-
ent delivery is fairly constant and nonruminant species 
where nutrients are available intermittently. Due to the 
complexity of appetite control and the complications 
added by species differences, this review will attempt 
to overview the major factors regulating feed intake in 
ruminants with notations to interesting and novel fea-
tures of nonruminant farm animals.
CNS MECHANISMS
Hypothalamus
The hypothalamus has a central role in the integra-
tion of the various signals, resulting in activation of 
feeding or inhibition of feeding responses. Early experi-
ments in farm animals found that lesions of the ventro-
medial nucleus (VMN) in pigs caused increased feed-
ing, whereas lesions of the lateral hypothalamic area 
(LHA) reduced feeding (Baile and Forbes, 1974). In

(Baile and Forbes, 1974). Feeding in sheep will increase 
c-Fos activation (c-Fos has been used as an indicator 
of recent neuron activation) in neurons of the supraop-
tic nucleus (SON), whereas fasting increased c-Fos ex-
pression in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), VMN, 
and dorsomedial nucleus (DMN; Chaillou et al., 2000). 
These studies produced a foundation for our current 
understanding of the role of these nuclei in regulating 
feeding in farm animals and suggested the need for fur-
ther studies to determine the role of the hypothalamus 
in appetite regulation in these species.
A major location for the processing of these appe-
tite regulatory signals is the arcuate nucleus (ARC ), 
located at the base of the hypothalamus. Both stimu-
latory and inhibitory signals originate from the ARC 
and influence other secondary appetite neurons within 
the LHA, dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH), VMN, 
and PVN and subsequently other areas of the CNS, 
with those signals emerging from the LHA-DMH being 
stimulatory to feed intake and those from the VMN 
and PVN being inhibitory (Table 1; for a review of 
hypothalamic appetite neurotransmitters, see Sartin 
et al., 2010). Although this model of first-order and 
second-order neurons was developed in the rat and pro-
vides an organization for testing and understanding the 
control of appetite, recent studies suggest this may not 
be appropriate for all species, particularly sheep (Qi et 
al., 2010). In addition to the hypothalamus, the brain 
stem contains pathways that are thought to control sa-
tiety or meal termination, such as the nucleus tractus 
solitarius (NTS). Here, circulating hormones and me-
tabolites signal either directly or indirectly through af-
ferent projections from the vagus nerve or sympathetic 
nerves to the NTS for meal termination. Thus, the hy-
pothalamus and NTS act in concert to integrate signals 
(Figure 1) leading to increased or decreased feed intake 
(for a review of peripheral satiety signals, see Allen et 
al., 2009).
Orexigenic Neurotransmitters
Neuropeptide Y. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) has 
been demonstrated to be a potent appetite-stimulating 
neurotransmitter whose site of synthesis was localized 
to the ARC in sheep (Clarke et al., 2000; Adam et al., 
2002; Pillon et al., 2003; Polkowska et al., 2006) and cat-
tle (Bahar and Sweeney, 2008). Expression of the NPY 
gene was demonstrated to be responsive to short-term 
fasting or longer term undernutrition, both of which 
increased NPY gene expression in the ARC (Henry et 
al., 2000; Polkowska and Gladysz, 2001; Adam et al., 
2002; Archer et al., 2002). Interestingly, NPY gene ex-
pression assessed at different times throughout the year 
indicated that abundance of NPY mRNA correlated to 
voluntary feed intake (Anukulkitch et al., 2009). The 
NPY neuron was also found to express leptin recep-
tors (Williams et al., 1999; Iqbal et al., 2001c), which 
were shown to mediate leptin suppression of NPY gene 
expression at the ARC (Qi et al., 2010). The activation 
of NPY neurons was suggested to stimulate neurons 
in other appetite-controlling regions of the hypothala-
mus, such as the DMN and VMN (Chaillou et al., 2002; 
Polkowska et al., 2006; Qi et al., 2008). The injection of 
NPY into the lateral cerebroventricle of the brain pro-
duced a potent effect to increase feed intake in sheep 
(Miner et al., 1989, 1990; Sartin et al., 2001; El-Haddad 
et al., 2003) and pigs (Parrott et al., 1986; Barb et al., 
2006). The effect of NPY to initiate feeding responses 
was primarily shown to be mediated largely via the 
NPY-Y1 receptor (Clarke et al., 2005). In addition, 3 
SNP in the NPY gene were associated with ADG, BW, 
and feed conversion ratio in cattle [but SNP for cocaine 
and amphetamine-related transcript (CART), proopi-
omelanocortin (POMC), corticotrophin-releasing hor-
mone (CRH), and melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) 
did not show a similar relationship to appetite control], 
further supporting the critical importance of NPY in 
the regulation of appetite (Sherman et al., 2008).
Agouti-Related Protein. Agouti-related protein 
(AgRP) mRNA and protein were shown to be local-
ized to neuron cell bodies in the ARC and, further-
more, were co-localized with NPY neurons (Henry et 
al., 2001; Adam et al., 2002; Archer et al., 2002; Wag-
ner et al., 2004). The expression of the AgRP gene was 
activated by fasting as expected and was accompanied 
by increased numbers of AgRP-positive cells and an in-
crease in AgRP neuron activation as indicated by dual 
labeling with c-Fos (Henry et al., 2001; Adam et al., 
2002; Archer et al., 2002; Wagner et al., 2004). Injec-
tion of AgRP into the lateral ventricle was found to 
increase feed intake in sheep allowed ad libitum con-
sumption (Wagner et al., 2004) but not in pigs (Barb et 
al., 2004). The mechanism for AgRP activation of feed

Melanin-Concentrating Hormone. Melanin-
concentrating hormone (MCH) was described in ro-
dent models as a second-order neuron, mediating the 
effects of first-order neurons, such as NPY, on feed in-
take (Schwartz et al., 2000). The neuron cell bodies 
expressing MCH were localized to the LHA, although 
MCH was also found in other brain regions (Tillet et al., 
1996). The MCH neurons in turn project to higher cen-
ters as well as to the ARC and VMN (Qi et al., 2008), 
indicating the complex nature of the neural integration 
of appetite. Whereas short-term fasting had no effect 
on MCH expression (Chaillou et al., 2003; Whitlock et 
al., 2005), MCH gene expression was increased in sheep 
with thin body condition (Henry et al., 2000), perhaps 
indicative of a long-term effect of nutrient manipula-
tion on MCH gene expression. Finally, a single bolus 
injection of MCH into the lateral ventricle produced 
the expected increase in feed intake (Whitlock et al., 
2005), whereas infusion of MCH for 6 h had no effect on 
feed intake, suggesting that the continuous delivery of 
MCH resulted in a downregulation of the MCH recep-
tor (Whitlock et al., 2005). There have been few studies 
on the MCH receptor in farm animals, although Tan et 
al. (2002) found evidence in Southern blots for the type 
1 and 2 receptor in cattle and pigs.

was found to be localized to the LHA and DMH (Iqbal 
et al., 2001b) and these ORX neurons were found to 
project to higher brain centers as well as reciprocal con-

Nutritional modifications have not demonstrated a 
change in gene expression for ORX, although there is 
a negative correlation between orexin and a fat body 
condition (Anukulkitch et al., 2010). In addition, leptin 
has been shown to directly as well as to indirectly (via 
the α-MSH pathways originating in the ARC) inhibit 
gene expression for ORX (Qi et al., 2010). Intramus-
cular injection of large doses of ORX in pigs increased 
cumulative feed intake at 24 h, whereas intracerebro-
ventricular (ICV) injection of ORX in sheep nearly 
doubled cumulative feed intake in 4 h (Dyer et al., 
1999; Sartin et al., 2001). The ORX receptor, ORX1R, 
has been localized to higher brain centers as well as 
the ARC, LHA, median eminence, and preoptic area 
(Zhang et al., 2005).
Opiates. Opiates have also been implicated in the 
control of feed intake in rodent species, although this 
class of molecules has been much less studied in farm 
animals. The typical molecules regulating hypothalam-
ic appetite mechanisms would be the endorphins and 
enkephalins. Enkephalin was localized to the periven-
tricular nucleus (Pe ), PVN, and the VMN (Henry et 
al., 2000). Prodynorphin was primarily localized to the 
SON, PVN, Pe, VMN, and the thalamus and was co-
localized with orexin in the LHA (Iqbal et al., 2003). 
Enkephalin expression in the PVN and VMN, both 
areas involved in satiety, was reduced in thin as com-
pared with fat ewes (Henry et al., 2000) but long-term 
undernutrition had no effect on prodynorphin mRNA 
in sheep (Iqbal et al., 2003). Injection of the opiate 
antagonist naloxone was found to inhibit feed intake in 
sheep, suggesting a stimulatory role for opiate receptors 
in regulating feed intake (Alavi et al., 1991; Obese et 
al., 2007), though naloxone was ineffective in alteration 
of feed intake in pigs (Baldwin and Parrot, 1985). This 
inhibitory effect of naloxone on feed intake was more 
pronounced in obese sheep (Alavi et al., 1993). Demon-
stration of the effects of opiates on feed intake in sheep 
were based on intravenous injection of an opiate recep-
tor agonist, syndaphyllin (SD33), which produced an 
increase in feed intake that was detected at 24 h and 
continued to at least 48 h after injection. Moreover, its 
effects were blocked by naloxone, indicating the feed in-
take response was mediated by opiate receptors (Obese 
et al., 2007). In a recent study in pigs, Kojima et al. 
(2009) found a small increase in feed intake in pigs 
treated with SD33 just after weaning. These results in-
dicate a possible practical application for opiate recep-
tor agonists in improving feed intake.
Anorexigenic Neurotransmitters
α-Melanocyte-Stimulating Hormone. 
α-Melanocyte-stimulating hormone is an anorexigenic 
neurotransmitter whose cell bodies were localized to 
the ARC based on POMC (i.e., the precursor gene for 
α-MSH) gene expression and α-MSH staining (Henry 
et al., 2000; Sartin et al., 2008). Short-term nutrient 
restriction as well as body condition do not correlate 
with changes in POMC gene expression (Henry et al., 
2000; Adam et al., 2002; Archer et al., 2002), unless 
altered photoperiod was added to the experimental de-
sign (Clarke et al., 2000, 2003; Archer et al., 2004). The 
role of α-MSH is suggested as that of appetite inhibi-
tion and is mediated through actions on the melano-
cortin-3 receptor (MC3R) and MC4R, both cloned for 
sheep and pigs (Iqbal et al., 2001a; Fan et al., 2008a,b). 
In sheep, gene expression for the MC3R and the MC4R 
was not affected by fasting (Iqbal et al., 2001a). In 
pigs, ICV injection of [Nle4, D-Phe7]-α-MSH (i.e., an 
α-MSH analog) resulted in suppression of feed intake, 
whereas AgRP or a pharmacologic antagonist, SHU-
9119, failed to increase feed intake (Barb et al., 2004). 
These pigs were heterozygous for a missense mutation 
in the MC4R receptor, although in other studies (Fan 
et al., 2008b), this receptor mutation expressed in vitro 
was able to bind [Nle4, D-Phe7]-α-MSH and AgRP nor-
mally and activate receptor signaling. Thus the reason 
for the discrepancy in the in vitro and in vivo signal-
ing appears unknown (Tao, 2010), although Fan et al. 
(2009) suggested that interactions between SNP may 
account for the differences between populations of pigs.
CART. The CART peptides function as neurotrans-
mitters in the hypothalamus (Adam et al., 2002) as 
well as in the enteric nerves in sheep (Arciszewski et 
al., 2009). The mRNA for CART was localized to the 
ARC, PVN, VMN, and median eminence (Henry et al., 
2001; Adam et al., 2002; Archer et al., 2002) and co-
localized with POMC in the ARC (Adam et al., 2002). 
In chronically feed-restricted sheep, there was a reduc-
tion in CART expression in the PVN, DMN, and LHA 
(Henry et al., 2001), as well as the ARC (Archer et 
al., 2002). Although CART gene expression correlates 
with nutritional regulation, more evidence is required 
to demonstrate its relevance to appetite control in farm 
animals.
CRH/Urocortin. Corticotropin-releasing hor-
mone was localized to the PVN in sheep and CRH gene 
expression was enhanced by leptin administration (Qi 
et al., 2010), reflecting the role of the PVN as an in-
hibitor of feed intake. An SNP was found in the CRH 
gene that correlated to carcass weight and rib eye area 
(Buchanan et al., 2005). And importantly, both ICV 
and intravenous administration of CRH, urocortin, 
and adrenocorticotropin were found to inhibit feed in-
take in sheep (Weisinger et al., 2000) and pigs (Parrot, 
1990; Whitley et al., 2000). These studies underscore 
the possible importance of the PVN for regulation of 
appetite inhibition signals in farm animals and suggest 
further studies of the PVN neurotransmitters should be 
performed to fully elucidate these appetite inhibitory 
mechanisms.

gastrointestinal tract and are integrated at the NTS 
to initiate meal termination (Schwartz, 2000; Allen et 
al., 2009). Meal termination in farm animals has been 
shown to be initiated by factors such as chemical and 
mechanical signals from the gastrointestinal tract (Al-

nutrient molecules (Allen et al., 2009). The communi-
cation from the periphery to the NTS was shown to 
be transmitted along afferent fibers of the vagus nerve 
(Anil and Forbes, 1988) or sympathetic nerves (Fig-
ure 1). In addition, leptin and glucagon-like peptide-1 
(GLP-1) receptors were found expressed in the brain 
stem, including the NTS, which suggests the possibility 
for direct hormonal interactions to elicit satiety (Mer-
cer et al., 1998). Indeed, a GLP-1 analog was shown to 
inhibit feed intake in pigs (Raun et al., 2007) and the 
effects of leptin to inhibit feed intake are well known. 
A final possibility for regulation of appetite by the 
NTS was found based on localization of NPY recep-
tors to the NTS, providing a possible link between the 
NTS and the mediobasal hypothalamus for integration 
of satiety (Mercer et al., 1998). Although the specific 
mechanisms are extrapolated from work in laboratory 
animals, the data in farm animals for satiety regulation 
were found to be consistent with this mechanism (see 
review by Allen et al., 2009).
PERIPHERAL MECHANISMS
Although it is the CNS that regulates energy ho-
meostasis, the CNS is responding in part to signals 
produced peripherally in proportion to the status of 
adipose tissue reserves, and in response to the presenta-
tion and ingestion of feed and the products of digestion.
Adipose Signals—Leptin
Plasma concentrations of leptin were highly corre-
lated with body fat mass in sheep (Blache et al., 2000, 
Delavaud et al., 2000; Ehrhardt et al., 2000), cattle 
(Ehrhardt et al., 2000), pigs (Ramsay et al., 1998), and 
horses (Buff et al., 2002). Circulating concentrations of 
leptin were also altered by fasting in sheep (Nagatani 
et al., 2000), cattle (Amstalden et al., 2000), pigs (Barb 
et al., 2001), and horses (Piccione et al., 2004). Further-
more, simply altering dietary intake altered circulating 
concentrations of leptin within 48 h in pregnant ewes 
(Thomas et al., 2001). Production of leptin in cultured 
bovine adipocytes has been linked to glucose availabil-
ity and fatty acid synthesis (Kadokawa et al., 2007). 
Therefore, an increase in nutrient availability may al-
ter circulating concentrations of leptin. Indeed, jugular 
infusion of lipid and glucose increased circulating con-
centrations of leptin in late-lactation cows (Chelikani 
et al., 2003). Circulating concentrations of leptin were 
also increased by glucose infusion in sheep (Archer et 
al., 2005). However, insulin could be mediating the in-
crease in leptin in response to glucose administration as 
insulin has been demonstrated to stimulate increased 
circulating concentrations of leptin (Asakuma et al., 
2003). Propionate was not found to be an important 
regulator of plasma leptin concentration in dairy cattle 
(Bradford et al., 2006). Thus, circulating concentra-
tions of leptin in animals serve as a signal of both fat 
stores and level of energy intake. Moreover, such a sig-
nal would be expected to regulate feed intake.
Central administration of leptin suppressed feed in-
take in pigs (Barb et al., 1998) and ovariectomized ewes 
(Henry et al., 1999; Morrison et al., 2001). However, 
ICV administration of leptin did not suppress feed 
intake in severely nutrient-restricted ovariectomized 
ewes (Morrison et al., 2001). Bolus injection of leptin 
into the carotid artery also reduced feed intake in pigs 
(Ramsay et al., 2004). Moreover, in pigs, suppression of 
feed intake after ICV injection of leptin was reversed by 
NPY (Barb et al., 2006).
One site of action for leptin inhibition of feed intake 
is the hypothalamus. In sheep, intravenous leptin ad-
ministration reduced activation of orexigenic neurons 
expressing NPY in the ARC and expressing ORX in 
the LHA, as indicated by c-Fos expression (Qi et al., 
2010). The effect of leptin on ORX neurons was both 
a direct action on the ORX neuron and an indirect ef-
fect mediated via the ARC (suggesting the complete 
dependence of the ARC for second-order neuron control 
as described for rodent models should be reconsidered 
for farm animals). In addition, leptin increased activa-
tion of anorexigenic neurons expressing POMC in the 
ARC and expressing CRH in the PVN (Qi et al., 2010). 
Thus, leptin reduced feed intake by inhibition of appe-
tite-stimulatory processes and activation of appetite-
inhibitory processes at the hypothalamus. A second site 
of action in the brain may be at the NTS. The presence 
of leptin receptors in the NTS suggests a possible direct 
action by leptin on the NTS to reduce feeding (Mercer 
et al., 1998).
If leptin suppresses feed intake and leptin is pro-
duced in relationship to the amount of adipose tissue, 
the question often arises of why fat animals continue 
to eat? One possible explanation is that leptin is more 
efficiently transported across the blood-brain barrier in 
thin sheep than fat sheep (Adam et al., 2006; Adam 
and Findlay, 2010). Interestingly, this only occurs in 
sheep housed under a long day photoperiod and the 
impaired transport of leptin across the blood-brain bar-
rier does not fully normalize after BW loss (Adam and 
Findlay, 2010). Thus, although leptin can reduce feed 
intake, the ability of leptin to reach the brain and sup-
press feed intake appears to be limited in fat animals.
Another level of regulation between the fat cell and 
the brain was suggested by morphologic studies that 
found neurons that arise from the PVN, VMN, AHA, 
POA, ARC, and SON project to porcine adipose tissue 
(Czaja et al., 2003). Nearly all of these neurons express 
the leptin receptor and may represent a novel reciprocal 
control between the CNS (α-MSH pathways) and the 
fat tissue. Moreover, ICV injection of an α-MSH analog

Physical Factors—Gastrointestinal Tract
The neural inputs to the NTS have an important role 
in transient inhibition of feeding responses. Voluntary 
DM intake of forages by ruminants (Allen et al., 2009) 
or in pigs (Oresanya et al., 2008) may be limited by dis-
tention resulting from restricted flow of digesta through 
the gastrointestinal tract. The reticulorumen is gener-
ally regarded as the site in the gastrointestinal tract for 
which distention and retention time limits DMI with 
high-fill diets (Allen, 1996, 2000; Forbes, 2007a,b). Ten-
sion receptors in the muscular wall of the rumen and 
reticulum provide a measure of distension, whereas epi-
thelial receptors provide information on the fibrousness 
of the digesta (Forbes and Barrio, 1992). Furthermore, 
there are mechanoreceptors in the abomasum and duo-
denum that have been implicated in the control of feed 
intake (Forbes and Barrio, 1992).
Gastrointestinal Hormones
Ghrelin. In communication of nutrient needs, it is 
important for the gastrointestinal tract to produce one 
or more signals to enhance feed intake. Ghrelin has been 
proposed as a signal to stimulate feed intake, likely by 
actions on areas of the hypothalamus where the blood-
brain barrier is incomplete (Grouselle et al., 2008). In 
support of ghrelin as a stimulator of feed intake, sheep 
and pigs fed on a regular meal schedule have an in-
crease in circulating ghrelin before the meal (Sugino 
et al., 2002a,b; Reynolds et al., 2010). However, when 
animals are allowed ad libitum access to feed, there 
is no increase in ghrelin associated with feeding. This 
may be due to other factors causing the animal to eat 
or to limitations in ability to detect changes in ghrelin 
to which the animal is sensitive. However, circulating 
concentrations of ghrelin have been demonstrated to 
decrease after feeding in sheep (Takahashi et al., 2010), 
cattle (Hayashida et al., 2001), pigs (Reynolds et al., 
2010), and horses (Gordon and McKeever, 2006). Fi-
nally, active immunization against ghrelin in pigs re-
sulted in reduced feed intake (Vizcarra et al., 2007). 
Thus, changes in circulating concentrations of ghrelin 
are consistent with stimulation of feed intake.
Physiological changes associated with nutrient needs 
induce changes in circulating concentrations of ghrelin. 
Intravenous dextrose infusion in horses and glucose in-
fusion in cattle caused decreased circulating concentra-
tions of ghrelin (Gordon and McKeever, 2006; Roche et 
al., 2008). In sheep, intravenous infusion of AA, but not 
glucose, increased circulating concentrations of ghrelin 
(Sugino et al., 2010). Similarly in pigs, oral ingestion 
of tryptophan increased circulating concentrations of 
ghrelin (Zhang et al., 2007), and dietary supplemen-
tation with zinc increased feed intake and the circu-
lating concentration of ghrelin (Yin et al., 2009). De-
creased rumen fill by removal of the rumen contents 
also resulted in increased circulating concentrations of 
ghrelin (Gregorini et al., 2009). Additionally, circulat-
ing concentrations of ghrelin were increased in short-
term-fasted animals relative to well-fed animals in both 
cattle and pigs (Govoni et al., 2005; Wertz-Lutz et al., 
2006). Circulating concentrations of ghrelin were also 
increased by prolonged, moderate nutrient restriction 
in cattle (Wertz-Lutz et al., 2008). Interestingly, in 
young pigs, circulating concentrations of ghrelin were 
less after 12 h of fasting then increased 36 and 48 h 
after the initiation of fasting (Salfen et al., 2003).
Studies attempting to explore the role of ghrelin ad-
ministration in the stimulation of feed intake in animals 
have had mixed results. Some have observed no impact 
of ghrelin on feed intake in sheep (Iqbal et al., 2006), 
whereas others have observed a moderate increase in 
feed intake in sheep and cattle (Melendez et al., 2006; 
Wertz-Lutz et al., 2006), whereas still others have ob-
served a strong stimulation in response to ghrelin ad-
ministration in sheep (Grouselle et al., 2008). Another 
study in sheep found an effect of ICV ghrelin adminis-
tration only during long day photoperiod (Harrison et 
al., 2008). Interestingly, in pigs, repeated intravenous 
administration of ghrelin resulted in increased BW gain 
and no increase in feed intake (Salfen et al., 2004). Al-
though the mechanism for this effect was unknown, en-
ergy balance could affect the effect of ghrelin on feed in-
take. Bradford and Allen (2008) observed a preprandial 
increase in ghrelin in cows in negative energy balance 
in early lactation but not in cows in positive energy 
balance in late lactation. Another possible explanation 
is due to different diets used. Indeed, Takahashi et al. 
(2008) demonstrated a differential response in circulat-
ing concentrations of ghrelin in sheep fed concentrate or 
timothy hay. Although both groups were fed the same 
amount of energy and CP, the concentrate produced a 
greater decline in circulating concentrations of ghrelin 
than sheep fed timothy hay (Takahashi et al., 2008). 
However, Wertz-Lutz et al. (2010) did not observe any 
difference in circulating concentrations of ghrelin in 
steers offered a high concentrate or high roughage at 
different amounts to achieve a similar energy intake. 
In pigs, consumption of the nonstarch polysaccharides 
guar gum and cellulose increased circulating concen-
trations of ghrelin (Owusu-Asiedu et al., 2006). Thus, 
ghrelin appears to be a physiological stimulator of feed 
intake, although its action may be dependent on condi-
tions that have not yet been fully determined.
Cholecystokinin. Several satiety signals are pro-
duced by the intestine including cholecystokinin 
(CCK), bombesin, glucagon, GLP-1 and GLP-2, apo-
lipoprotein A-IV, amylin, somatostatin, enterostatin, 
and peptide YY (3–36). Of these, CCK is the most 
studied in domestic livestock. In addition to an intes-
tinal source, CCK was also localized in the suprachi-
asmatic region and SON of the sheep hypothalamus 
(Marson et al., 1987). Moreover, CCK concentrations 
in the anterior hypothalamus were increased in fed

sheep and reduced with fasting (Baile and McLaughlin, 
1987). In sheep and pigs, intravenous and ICV infusion 
of CCK reduces feed intake (Grovum, 1981; Baldwin 
and Sukhchai, 1996). Della-Fera and Baile (1979) dem-
onstrated that ICV administration of CCK suppressed 
feed intake in sheep. Moreover, Della-Fera et al. (1981) 
administered a CCK antibody ICV to sheep, result-
ing in reduced central CCK and increased feed intake, 
further establishing CCK as a feed intake regulator. In 
pigs, active immunization against CCK also resulted in 
increased feed intake (Pekas and Trout, 1990). Thus, 
evidence firmly indicates CCK to be a physiological 
suppressor of feed intake in farm animals. Although 
there are other satiety molecules from the gastrointesti-
nal tract, similar evidence in domestic animals to that 
presented for CCK is needed.
Nutrients and Metabolites
Nutrient molecules such as glucose have both di-
rect and indirect actions on the hypothalamus and on 
peripheral activation of nerves to the NTS to control 
appetite in laboratory animals (Schwartz et al., 2000; 
Minokoshi et al., 2004). The link between the brain and 
nutrient molecules in the control of appetite in domestic 
animals is less clearly delineated. Therefore, this review 
will be primarily limited to molecules that have been 
demonstrated to have a neural component to regula-
tion of feed intake. Allen et al. (2009) provides a recent 
review for more detailed information on the general role 
of nutrients in regulating feed intake in ruminants.
The theory that food intake is controlled by a signal 
from the liver to the brain that is stimulated by oxida-
tion of a variety of fuels is called the hepatic oxidation 
theory of the control of food intake and is supported 
by a large body of work (Forbes, 1988; Langhans, 1996; 
Friedman, 1998; Allen et al., 2009). The mechanism 
proposed for this regulatory cascade is based in part 
on the data demonstrating that glucose infusion into 
the portal vein of guinea pigs decreased hepatic vagal 
discharge rates (Niijima, 1982) and severing the vagus 
nerve in sheep prevented the inhibition of feed intake 
induced by propionate (Anil and Forbes, 1988). The 
proposed sequence in ruminants for inhibition of feed 
intake in response to propionate infusion was that pro-
pionate oxidation led to increased ATP production and 
in turn produced hyperpolarization of hepatic cells. 
This results in reduced firing rates on the vagal afferent 
fibers and results in integration at the NTS to reduce 
feed intake (reviewed by Allen et al., 2009).
Intestinal, intravenous, and intraperitoneal glucose 
infusions do not decrease energy intake in ruminants 
(Faverdin, 1999; Allen, 2000). Thus, circulating VFA 
concentrations (Trenkle, 1970; Leuvenink et al., 1997; 
DiCostanzo et al., 1999), AA (Kuhara et al., 1991), 
and lipids (Choi and Palmquist, 1996; Faverdin, 1999; 
Allen, 2000) are more likely than glucose (Sano et al., 
1993; Faverdin, 1999) to be the nutrients stimulating 
regulatory mechanisms in ruminants. In pigs, lipids and 
glucose reduce feeding (Oresanya et al., 2008; Han et 
al., 2009). Differences in appetite effects of glucose ob-
served between ruminants and nonruminants are likely 
because of differences in hepatic oxidation of glucose 
(Allen et al., 2005).
FASTING AND DISEASE
Fasting represents a lack of feed intake that is ac-
companied by an increase in appetite but a reduction 
in metabolic rate to preserve stored fuel molecules. The 
systems as detailed above act in a concerted fashion to 
promote hunger and alter endocrine, metabolic, and re-
productive systems to facilitate survival. When energy 
intake is reduced, plasma leptin concentrations are re-
duced (Nagatani et al., 2000), presumably to remove an 
inhibitory signal to hypothalamic neurons controlling 
feeding. Fasting would also be accompanied by reduc-
tions in circulating nutrients, such as propionate, which 
would alter vagal tone and thus reduce the satiety 
stimulus to the brain (Allen et al., 2009). This is also 
accompanied by increases in circulating ghrelin, which 
should favor a stimulus to appetite (Wertz-Lutz et al., 
2006). Finally, fasting will directly or indirectly alter 
the expression of key hypothalamic neurotransmitters: 
increased NPY and AgRP (Henry et al., 2000, 2001), 
both potent stimuli to appetite.
Disease, on the other hand, results in a reduction 
in appetite but in the face of an increased metabolic 
rate. Thus the effects of disease represent a more com-
plex scenario than a singular consequence of reduced 
feed intake (for a current review, see Grossberg et al., 
2010). Although there are insufficient data on appetite 
neurotransmitter changes with disease in farm animals, 
based on data in laboratory animals, appetite stimula-
tory neurotransmitters such as NPY are reduced or un-
changed and the appetite inhibitory neurotransmitters 
such as POMC are activated with disease. In studies 
with rat models, increased release of IL-1, tumor ne-
crosis factor (TNF-α) and IL-6 have been associated 
with disease responses and these cytokines injected into 
the lateral ventricle of the brain of laboratory animals 
were found to produce fever, decreased feed intake, and 
produce body mass wasting (Grossberg et al., 2010). 
Although the consequence of long-term disease in farm 
animals is also a wasting of lean body mass, the mecha-
nism for this effect on appetite control in farm animals 
has not been studied in depth.
In sheep with parasitic infections (Greer et al., 2009; 
Zaralis et al., 2009), pigs with Salmonella typhimurium 
infection (Jenkins et al., 2004) and in sheep adminis-
tered endotoxin [i.e., lipopolysaccharide (LPS); Soli-
man et al., 2001; Daniel et al., 2003], one hypothesis 
examined was that the disease-associated reductions in 
feed intake were the result of increased plasma leptin, 
which would then decrease feed consumption. In these 
studies, there was little evidence for an increase in cir-

culating leptin concentrations with these disease mod-
els (though the possibility of alterations in leptin access 
to the brain has not been examined).
Injection of LPS into a jugular vein of fed sheep was 
found to inhibit feed intake, but when fasted sheep were 
injected with LPS, feed intake was not different from 
controls (Sartin et al., 2008). Because fasting increases 
gene expression for key neurotransmitters regulating 
fasting, it suggests that either there is an effect of LPS 
on appetite neurotransmitters or that administration of 
key neurotransmitters can overcome effects of appetite 
suppression due to LPS. Indeed, intravenous LPS injec-
tion increased the production of key cytokines in the 
hypothalamic appetite control regions in rodent models 
(Grossberg et al., 2010) as well as IL-1 in the sheep 
choroid plexus (Vellucci and Parrott, 1996). Moreover, 
IL-1 injection into the lateral ventricle of the brain in-
creased body temperature as well as gene expression 
for CRH in the PVN of sheep (Vellucci et al., 1995). 
These results implicate CRH as a link to reduced feed 
intake in disease, though LPS had no effect on PVN 
expression of CRH in the pig (Vellucci and Parrott, 
1998). Based on these data with CRH, infusion of an 
IL-1receptor antagonist should recover feed intake in 
LPS-treated sheep, but the antagonist had no effect on 
recovering feed intake. This suggests that IL-1 may not 
be a major component of the mechanism for appetite 
suppression due to disease in sheep (McMahon et al., 
1999).
Interestingly, ICV infusion of NPY resulted in the 
recovery of LPS-suppressed appetite, which continued 
even after the termination of the NPY infusion (Mc-
Mahon et al., 1999). This result may indicate that LPS 
treatment reduced NPY production in the hypothala-
mus or that NPY stimulation of feed intake was of suf-
ficient magnitude to overcome the suppressive effects of 
LPS. Moreover, these data suggest that neurotransmit-
ters may be useful in manipulating feed intake in sick 
animals.
The melanocortin system in the hypothalamus has 
been implicated in disease-associated inappetance and 
in cancer cachexia in laboratory animals (Grossberg 
et al., 2010). In rodent models, the disease-associat-
ed mechanism for reduced feed intake is related to 
increased POMC gene expression at 4 h after intra-
peritoneal injection of LPS (Sergeyev et al., 2001). It 
should be noted that the time frame for the effects of 
intravenous administration of LPS in the sheep model 
should be faster than intraperitoneal injections of LPS 
in rats and the 2 approaches cannot be directly com-
pared based on time. In sheep, whole hypothalamic 
POMC mRNA abundance were unchanged at 3 or 6 
h after intravenous LPS injection. However, there are 
multiple sources of POMC that are not all related to 
appetite control and thus this approach cannot ade-
quately address POMC changes with disease. Whereas 
AgRP mRNA was unchanged at 3 h after LPS, by 6 
h AgRP mRNA was increased (Sartin et al., 2008). 
Further focus at 6 h after LPS indicated that AgRP 
mRNA was increased and POMC mRNA decreased in 
the ARC, as assessed by in situ hybridization. In ad-
dition, dual-label immunohistochemistry for c-Fos and 
either AgRP or α-MSH supported the mRNA data, 
indicating AgRP expression and neuron activation was 
increased, whereas α-MSH was reduced (Sartin et al., 
2008). The increase in AgRP may result from the pro-
longed feed intake suppression, which would be expect-
ed to occur based on fasting studies, though the effects 
on POMC cannot be similarly explained. Alternatively, 
these data may represent a mechanism to recover ap-
petite after feed intake inhibition or may explain the 
compensatory cumulative feed intake observed at 24 
to 48 h after LPS suppression of appetite (by this time 
both LPS and saline-treated animals have similar cu-
mulative feed intakes). Additional studies are needed to 
address the issue of whether there is increased POMC 
expression preceding appetite inhibition and the rel-
evance of the MC4R to disease suppression of appetite 
in farm animals.
Experiments using a different approach to this ques-
tion found that when AgRP was administered to block 
the MC4R before injection of LPS, AgRP treatment 
prevented the reductions in feed intake (Sartin et al., 
2008). Thus, the effects of disease in ruminants may 
involve the MC4R as a major mechanism for inhibi-
tion of appetite, as has been hypothesized for labo-
ratory animals (Grossberg et al., 2010). Additionally, 
this study suggests that the development of synthetic 
MC4R antagonists, which would mimic the effect of 
AgRP, may have utility in the treatment of disease-
associated cachexia (Sartin et al., 2008; Grossberg et 
al., 2010) as well as possible uses in agriculture and 
veterinary medicine, where an increase in feed intake 
might be beneficial (Sartin et al., 2005).
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
Physiological and pathological alterations in feed in-
take are the result of a complex interplay between cir-
culating nutrient molecules, hormones, and nutrients 
in the gastrointestinal tract and the final actions of 
these factors on the brain stem and hypothalamic neu-
rons culminating in a feeding response. Although there 
are similarities to the appetite regulatory mechanism 
for other models, farm animals represent unique chal-
lenges based on species differences, different digestive 
systems, and nutritional requirements for production 
and thus require individual study of each farm animal 
species to determine the possible mechanisms regulat-
ing appetite as well as metabolic rate that are specific 
to each species. Moreover, there are newer molecules 
hypothesized to regulate appetite in laboratory animals 
that are just beginning to be studied (such as gastro-
intestinal hormones, nesfatin-1, and numerous others) 
in farm animals. The benefit to determining how feed 
intake is regulated in these diverse animals will improve 
our understanding of basic energy intake in farm ani-
mals, improve diet formulations, allow the development

as situations where feed intake has been compromised, 
such as lactation, disease, environmental stressors, and
