Influences of Myogenin Genotypes on Birth Weight, Growth Rate,
Carcass Weight, Backfat Thickness, and Lean Weight of Pigs1
M.F.W. te Pas, A. Soumillion, F. L. Harders, F. J. Verburg, T. J. van den Bosch,
P. Galesloot, and T.H.E. Meuwissen
DLO-Institute for Animal Science and Health, Department of Animal Breeding and Genetics,

ABSTRACT: Lean weight is related to muscle fiber
number. Muscle fiber formation (myogenesis) occurs
only during embryonic development when it is under
the control of the MyoD gene family consisting of
myogenin, MyoD1, myf-5, and myf-6. Myogenin has a
central position within the MyoD gene family because
myogenin expression abrogates myoblast proliferation
potential and regulates the differentiation of single
nucleated myoblasts into multinucleated myofibers.
Thus, myogenin genotype could be related to variation
in the number of muscle fibers formed, leading to
variation in muscle mass and, thus, lean weight. A
polymorphism at the porcine myogenin locus was
associated with birth weight, growth rate, lean weight
at 200 d, and backfat thickness. Yorkshire pigs from
two commercial lines were genotyped, and crosses
between heterozygous pigs and heterozygous and
homozygous pigs were made. Resulting litters were
genotyped, and phenotypic data were collected. Sig-
nificant differences were found between the two
homozygous myogenin genotypes for birth weight,
growth rate, and lean weight, but not for backfat
thickness. Variation at the myogenin locus explained
4% of the total phenotypic variation in birth weight,
growth rate, and carcass weight, and 5.8% of the total
variation in lean weight. We conclude that myogenin
genotype influences porcine growth rate and muscle
mass.

Introduction
Lean weight of cattle, pigs, and other animals is
related to the number of myofibers in their muscles.
Double-muscled cattle have about twice as many
myofibers as other cattle (Swatland and Kiefer, 1974;
Hanset et al., 1982). Pigs with more muscle fibers
grew faster and had greater muscle mass (Handel and
Stickland, 1984, 1988). Mutations disrupting normal
protein function of the cattle myostatin gene seem to
cause the double-muscling phenotype (Grobet et al.,
1997; Kambadur et al., 1997). However, other genes
can also affect myofiber numbers. Disruption of the
myogenin gene (Hasty et al., 1993; Nabeshima et al.,
1993) also affects myofiber numbers. Whether benefi-
cial alleles of these genes exist is unknown.
Myofiber formation in mammals occurs only during
embryonic development and is controlled by the MyoD
gene family consisting of four genes: myogenin,
MyoD1, myf-5, and myf-6 (Olson, 1990; Weintraub et
al., 1991). The Myf-5 and MyoD1 genes are expressed
in proliferating single nucleated precursor cells, called
myoblasts. The Myf-6 gene is mainly expressed
postnatally. Myogenin has a crucial role during
myogenesis. Myogenin is expressed in all myoblasts
from the start of differentiation (i.e., fusion of
myoblasts into multinucleated myofibers), and its
expression continues during cell fusion. Myogenin
expression also marks the end of the proliferation of
myoblasts. Thus, when myogenin is expressed, muscle
fibers develop from the myoblasts that were previously
formed. Therefore, different myogenin function or
timing of expression could have a major influence on
the number of muscle fibers that develop during
myogenesis.

the porcine gene have been reported (Ernst et al.,

Materials and Methods
Animals and Data Collection
Yorkshire pigs from lines marketed by the breeding
companies financing this research were used. Parents
were genotyped for the myogenin locus, crosses
between heterozygous animals and heterozygous and
homozygous animals were made, and the resulting
litters were genotyped. A total of 2,087 pigs was
genotyped. A file containing data measured on 14,199
animals and including pedigree information was
available. Birth weight was recorded within a day
after birth, growth rate was recorded as live weight
daily gain at the end of a 185-d test period starting at
birth, carcass weight was recorded at slaughter age of
200 d, and backfat thickness was the mean of four
ultrasonic measurements at points 5 cm from the
midline distributed at equal distances between shoul-
der and last rib. Lean weight was predicted from
carcass weight and backfat thickness data derived
from data of 1,117 animals. The following model was
used: Lean weight = 32.989 + .00215 × (carcass
weight)2 − .0353 × (backfat thickness)2.
Genotyping
Blood was collected, treated with EDTA, and stored
at −80°C. Genomic DNA was extracted using standard
molecular biology methods (Sambrook et al., 1989). A
353-bp genomic DNA fragment 3′ of the myogenin
gene was PCR amplified (primers: forward 5′-
TCTTGACCTTGTCATTGTGG; reverse 5′-
CTTCCTCACACCACCTTAC; annealing temperature
60°C) and digested with MspI as previously described
(Soumillion et al., 1997). The polymorphism was
previously identified after Southern blotting (Ernst et
al., 1993). The PCR-RFLP test is a two allelic test.
The undigested allele was identified as allele “A,” and
the digested allele as allele “B.”

Results
Allele frequencies were .55 for A and .45 for B, and
genotypic frequencies were .34, .43, and .23 for AA,
AB, and BB, respectively.

Because birth weight is related to subsequent

results because significant effects remained signifi-
cant, but at a lower level (Table 2).
Contributions of the myogenin locus to the total

Discussion
Most traits are expected to be polygenic, with each
gene adding a small contribution to the trait. Identifi-
cation of these genes, unraveling their quantitative
effects, and exploitation of the ones with major effects
will be a major task for geneticists in the near future.
We have focused on the regulation of growth rate and
lean production traits. Lean weight and growth rate
are polygenic traits, both with high economic
relevance. Muscle fiber number is an important
determinant of both traits (Swatland and Kiefer,
1974; Hanset et al., 1982; Handel and Stickland, 1984,
1988).
Myogenin knock-out mice show no muscle fiber
development, and heterozygous myogenin knock-out
mice (i.e., with one functional allele left) show half
the number of muscle fibers of wild type mice (Hasty
et al., 1993). Therefore, we hypothesized that natur-
ally occurring genetic variation in myogenin could
affect muscle fiber numbers and, thus, lean production
characteristics (te Pas and Visscher, 1994). Backfat
thickness is not related to muscle growth and develop-
ment. Because myogenin is not expressed in adipose
tissue, backfat thickness served as a negative control
in this research. As expected, genetic effects on
backfat thickness were not observed.
Our results indicate that myogenin genotype is
associated with birth weight, growth rate, and lean
yield in pigs. Previous reports have failed to show
associations with any of these traits (Ernst, 1994;
Ernst et al., 1994). The difference may be explained
by the different methods used (PCR vs Southern
blotting, see te Pas and Visscher [1994] for reasons
why Southern blotting is less suitable for an associa-
tion study) and the genetic background of the animals
(sire lines selected for these traits vs Chinese × white-
breed crosses).
Our results suggest that the genetic effect of the
MyoD gene family on lean production capacity is at
least partly acting during the muscle fiber differentia-
tion process. Because the myogenin allelic difference is
detected by closely linked genetic markers, the causal
mutation in the myogenin gene remains unknown.
There are two possible mechanisms to explain the
effects of the myogenin genotypes on growth and lean
production traits. 1) The causal mutation is present in
the coding sequences of the gene, giving rise to two
allelic forms of the myogenin protein with different
functional characteristics. 2) The causal mutation is
located in the regulatory sequences of the myogenin
gene, affecting the timing and degree of myogenin

human and mouse myogenin proteins, respectively.
Moreover, comparison of the nucleotide sequences of
1) a PCR fragment amplified on genomic DNA of an
AA Meishan pig, 2) the porcine myogenin sequence
reported by Briley et al. (1995) (EMBL database
entry X89007), and 3) parts of the sequences of two B
allele subclones from a porcine phage genomic DNA
library showed no sequence differences in the coding
region that would affect the amino acid sequence.
These results do not support the presence of different
myogenin protein forms with different functional
characteristics.
If the onset of myogenin gene expression is delayed
because of variation in the regulatory sequences,
myoblast proliferation could continue longer and
increase the number of myoblasts. This mechanism
has already been observed in selection lines of quail
(Coutinho et al., 1993), in which delayed onset of
myogenin expression is associated with an increased
number of myofibers. More myofibers could result in a
greater birth weight; compare for example the greater
birth weight of double-muscled cattle with the
homozygous myostatin mutation (Swatland and
Kiefer, 1974; Hanset et al., 1982). A greater birth
weight and more muscle fibers could lead to a greater
growth rate, and thus a greater carcass weight at the
same slaughter age. Also, more muscle fibers lead to
greater lean yield. Thus, all the obtained data are
consistent with this explanation.
Birth weight has important effects on pig mortality.
Pigs weighing less than 1 kg at birth show greatly
increased mortality (Bereskin et al., 1973; English
and Morrison, 1984; Kisner et al., 1995). Thus, adding
70 g to the average pig birth weight could reduce
mortality and result in greater reproduction rates and
greater productivity of pigs. Increased birth weight is
associated with severely affected delivery in double-
muscled cattle (Swatland and Kiefer, 1974; Hanset et
al., 1982). In our pigs, no adverse effects on delivery
were observed.
The effect of the myogenin genotype on birth
weight, growth, and lean yield traits was 4.0 to 5.8%
of the phenotypic variance in these traits. Because
hundreds of genes could affect these traits, the
influence of the myogenin genotype is important. We
showed that the myogenin locus is associated with a
quantitative effect on birth weight, growth rate, and
lean yield. Therefore, fixation of the BB genotype in
breeding populations should enhance production of
pigs.
Implications
Selection for the myogenin BB genotype increases
pig birth weight, which may reduce pig mortality by
approximately 1%, and increases growth rate and lean
weight of the pig without affecting backfat thickness.
The relatively low frequency of the BB genotype in the
population, which may result from the selection on
multiple traits at the same time, indicates that much
improvement can be made by myogenin-marker-
assisted selection.
