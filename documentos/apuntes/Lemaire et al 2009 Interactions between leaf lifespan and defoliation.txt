Abstract
In continuously stocked swards or pastures the fre-
quency at which individual tillers and individual leaves
are defoliated by ruminant livestock, relative to leaf
lifespan of the grass species within the sward, deter-
mines the proportion of each leaf defoliated before
senescence, and hence the efﬁciency of harvesting of
herbage. In this paper, sets of data obtained in a range
of climatic conditions and with a range of grass species
are used in order to document this relationship. It is
shown that the frequency of defoliation of individual
tillers or individual leaves is closely linked to the
average stocking density used within a period of time
for maintaining a steady state sward or pasture height,
herbage mass or leaf area index. Consequently, any
decrease in herbage growth rate should lead to a
decrease in the efﬁciency of harvesting of herbage and
then to a more than proportional decrease in total
herbage consumption by ruminant livestock. These
effects will be more important for grass species having
short leaf lifespan than for species with long lifespan. In
rotational stocking, the link between herbage growth
rate and frequency of defoliation of leaves can be
broken by controlling the grazing interval, so any
decrease in herbage growth would not be systematically
associated with a decrease in efﬁciency of harvesting of
herbage. Rotational stocking should be more efﬁcient
than continuous stocking in low herbage production
conditions, while in high herbage production systems
rotational and continuous stocking would have similar
efﬁciency. The implications of these conclusions for the
management of swards and pastures to meet different
objectives are discussed brieﬂy.
Keywords: leaf lifespan, defoliation frequency, defolia-
tion severity, herbage use efﬁciency, grazing manage-
ment, stocking density
Introduction
Herbage production in grazing systems is regulated by
environmental variables, morphogenesis of plant spe-
cies and characteristics of the sward or pasture. Plant
tissues accumulate through leaf appearance and elon-
gation, and are subject to ageing and senescence, which
lead to litter accumulation and eventually decomposi-
tion. Leaf tissues are also subject to discrete defoliation
events, the frequency and severity of which affect the
rate of new tissue production. The quantity of herbage
removed is the product of the rate of growth of new
plant tissue and the efﬁciency of the harvesting process
(deﬁned here as the proportion of herbage grown
which is harvested before entering the senescent state).
These two processes are essentially competitive, and
major emphasis in grassland research over a period of
40 years has been in understanding the factors inﬂu-
encing the balance between them with a view to
maximizing the productivity and sustainability of
grazing systems (Lemaire, 1988; Parsons et al., 1988;
Parsons and Chapman, 2000; Fulkerson and Donaghy,
2001). Initially, attention was focused on the supposed
advantages in herbage production of controlled, inter-
mittent stocking systems (rotational stocking) over the
continuous presence of grazing ruminant livestock
(continuous stocking). However, it now seems clear
that there is no essential difference between the two

Lemaire and Chapman (1996) suggested that the
efﬁciency of harvesting herbage, at least in temperate
swards, is closely related to the ratio between defolia-
tion frequency and leaf lifespan but, until recently,
evidence to test this suggestion has been available from
only a limited range of plant species and environmental
conditions. The objective of this review is to examine
the recent evidence on this topic, with particular
reference to its generalization across temperate and
tropical conditions, using the principles of plant mor-
phogenesis (Silsbury, 1970; Lemaire and Chapman,
1996) to deﬁne rates of tissue turnover in grazed
swards.
Leaf and tiller defoliation
The three components of grass morphogenesis, leaf
appearance rate, leaf extension rate and leaf lifespan are
genetically determined, and are also affected by envi-
ronmental variables (Figure 1). Species with high leaf
appearance rate tend to have short leaves with high
tiller density, while species with low leaf appearance
rate tend to have long leaves with low tiller density
(Lemaire, 1988). As pointed out by Parsons et al.
(1988), efﬁcient use of herbage is a trade-off between
optimizing light interception by grass leaves and har-
vesting leaf tissue before senescence. The best compro-
mise should be achieved when each individual leaf is
harvested just before the beginning of its senescence,
after a leaf lifespan that corresponds approximately to
the point when maximum average herbage accumula-
tion rate is achieved (Parsons et al., 1988). The deter-
mination of leaf lifespan for each grass species is of great
importance in order to control the timing of defoliation
to optimize the balance between herbage production
and efﬁciency of herbage utilization.
The components of consumption of leaf tissues by
ruminant livestock in grazed swards have been mea-
sured at the level of the individual tiller using tiller-
labelling techniques (Davies, 1983), with emphasis on
the frequency and severity of defoliation. Frequency of
defoliation is the probability for each plant unit (leaf or
tiller) to be defoliated each day. Its reciprocal, the
defoliation interval, represents the average time elapsed
between two successive defoliations on the same tiller
or individual leaf. Severity of defoliation is expressed as
the ratio between the length of leaf or tiller removed by
grazing in a single defoliation event and the length of
the leaf or tiller before defoliation, corrected, where
necessary, for variations in weight per unit length.
Frequency of defoliation of tillers in
continuous stocking systems
Wade and Baker (1979) used a marked tiller technique
to study frequency of defoliation of tillers at widely
differing stocking densities [live weight of ruminant
livestock (subsequently referred to as animals) per unit
area at a point in time] in a Lolium perenne sward under
continuous stocking, using the defoliation interval
(days between defoliations) as the basis for comparison.
Days to defoliation was related to stocking density with
a negative exponential equation. Wade (1991) used the
same technique to study defoliation dynamics in 5-d
paddocks grazed by dairy cows, which permitted
extrapolation from the previous continuous stocking
data, but used the inverse of defoliation interval, which
is frequency of defoliation as deﬁned above. In addition
to expressing the probability of defoliation, frequency of
defoliation is also an index of the proportion of the total
area grazed in a day: in this way it was estimated that a
dairy cow grazes in the region of 200 m2 d)1 under
these conditions. As demonstrated by Wade (1991),
tillers were grazed several times during a 5-d grazing
period in a paddock, the number of defoliation events
being a direct function of the stocking density used.
As the severity of individual defoliations under the
continuous and paddock systems was found to be
relatively constant (see below), it was also possible to
estimate the number of defoliations occurring in a day
under strip grazing (where a new grazing area is
allocated to animal each day), using the initial and
ﬁnal tiller heights. The values of between two and four
defoliations d)1 obtained at the different levels of
herbage allowance (herbage mass on offer per animal
and per day) were related to the stocking density in the
daily strips (Figure 2). This succession of defoliations,
occurring during a short period of time, can be

the functional signiﬁcance of the general relationship

or the area grazed d)1 increases less than proportion-

paddocks, the grazing-down of the sward was associ-
ated with a proportional reduction of 0Æ50 in the area

stocking, if an increase in stocking density is associ-

height, then the decrease in area bite)1 with shorter
swards (Ungar et al., 1991; Woodward, 1998) could
explain how the area grazed d)1 and the frequency
of defoliation of tillers increase relatively less. When
an increase in stocking density is a response to an
increase in herbage growth rate with a constant
sward or pasture height, such an explanation does
not hold, but the effect may be explained by the
limiting inﬂuence of bulk density of the sward or
pasture on bite area (Hodgson et al., 1994; Woodward,
1998). It is also possible that, as stocking density
increases, spatial competition among animals could
increase to limit bite area.
In continuous stocking systems, when swards or
pastures are maintained in a steady state, characterized
by a constant height, herbage mass or leaf area index,
through adjustments in the number of animals per unit
area, variations in stocking density must follow varia-
tions in rates of herbage growth, whatever their cause
might be (season of the year, climate, N-fertilizer
application or plant genotype), and frequency of defo-
liation on individual tillers follows variations in rates of
herbage growth. This appears to be a common pattern
of behaviour across a range of grass species.
Frequency of leaf defoliation
To analyse defoliation events relative to the dynamics of
leaf appearance and senescence, it is necessary to
measure the frequency of defoliation at the individual
leaf level as well as at the tiller level. Figure 4 reports
data obtained on F. arundinacea swards where fre-
quency of defoliation was recorded at both these levels.
Frequency of defoliation is expressed on the basis of leaf
lifespan. The frequency of defoliation of an individual

Figure 5 presents the probability of defoliation for

heights (Agnusdei, 1999). The probability of defoliation
was calculated for each species during the period of one
phyllochron (interval between the appearances of two
successive leaves). The uppermost fully elongated leaf
was most frequently defoliated. The probability of
defoliation for an individual leaf depends on its position
within the sward canopy, with longer leaves having a
greater probability of being defoliated than shorter ones
(Barthram and Grant, 1984). Expanding leaves are less
frequently defoliated than mature leaves because they
are shorter. When leaves are defoliated, they are
shortened and the probability for a further defoliation
decreases. Hence the probability of defoliation of
senescing leaves becomes very low.

for a given defoliation interval, the number of defoli-

obtained within a relatively small range of grass species
having relatively short leaf lifespan (L. perenne,
L. multiﬂorum, F. arundinacea, P. dilatatum and C. dacty-

frequency of defoliation of individual leaves and stock-

variation in stocking density (variation in herbage
growth rate at constant surface height, or variation of
surface height for a given herbage growth rate).
However, there is a lack of information allowing
extrapolation of such a relationship to taller grass
species and greater surface heights. The preliminary
data obtained on B. brizantha swards (Table 3) indicate
that this tall species would not necessarily ﬁt the same
general relationship. Further studies on other tropical
species are necessary in order to extrapolate the
relationship between frequency of defoliation and
stocking density to a larger range of conditions.
Severity of defoliation
Severity of defoliation is deﬁned here as the amount of
leaf removed, expressed as a proportion of the amount
before defoliation, on a leaf-by-leaf basis. It can be
estimated in different ways. First, as proposed by Wade
et al. (1989), it is possible to determine defoliation at the
tiller level by measuring extended tiller height before
and after a defoliation. Following on this work, Wade
(1991) showed that extended tiller height after defoli-
ation was on average 0Æ65 of the extended tiller height

can be very substantial (e.g. Grifﬁths et al., 2003).
The second approach is to measure leaf blade length
before and after defoliation events on labelled tillers.

sheath length represents about 0Æ33 of the extended
tiller height, 0Æ35 of total tiller height (Wade et al.,

lamina length. In this sense, the evidence indicates a
remarkable consistency in the adjustment of bite depth
resulting in a constant severity of defoliation of leaf
tissue close to 0Æ50.

Grazing efﬁciency
The efﬁciency of the grazing process can be deﬁned as the
ratio between the quantity of leaf tissue removed
by grazing and the quantity of leaf tissue produced
during a given period of time when swards or pastures are
maintained in steady state, and can also be estimated
using conventional tiller-marking techniques, estimat-
ing leaf length removed (frequency of defolia-
tion · severity of defoliation) as a proportion of total
leaf length over a time interval corresponding to a leaf
lifespan. These calculations are illustrated in Table 6 for
three species from the Argentine Pampa (Agnusdei,
1999), taking values for probability of defoliation from
Table 2 and severity of defoliation from Figure 6. The
calculated values of grazing efﬁciency are all relatively
low. During winter, a period of very low herbage growth
and consequently very low stocking density, only 0Æ25 of
leaf lamina length of L. multiﬂorum was removed by
grazing on swards subjected to the L treatment (short
sward ⁄ high stocking density, see above), and on swards
subjected to the H treatment (tall sward ⁄ low stocking
density, see above) only 0Æ03 of the leaf lamina length
was removed. As herbage growth increased in spring, so
did stocking density, and the proportion of leaf lamina
length removed also increased.

showed that N fertilizer increased the rate of removal of

increased with N-fertilizer application and was higher
in spring (period of high herbage growth and, conse-

Figure 7 shows that for a F. arundinacea sward, the
efﬁciency of grazing is strictly related to the probability
of defoliation of individual leaves within the leaf
lifespan interval. This relationship applied to both
different application rates of N fertilizer and different
seasons of the year. Whatever the cause, when herbage
growth increases, the stocking density necessary to
maintain a steady state sward height increases and the
probability of leaves being defoliated before they
senesce increases. When individual leaves are defoli-
ated once on average the estimated herbage use
efﬁciency is 0Æ55, corresponding to the average severity
of defoliation of leaves. With low herbage growth in
winter without N-fertilizer application, the probability
of defoliation declines to 0Æ50 and only 0Æ30 of leaf
tissue is removed by grazing animals while, when
herbage growth increases in spring under an application
of 100 kg N ha)1, the probability of defoliation
increases to 1Æ5 leading to a herbage use efﬁciency of
0Æ80 (Figure 7). The beneﬁcial effects of N application
constitute both an increase in herbage growth and a
corresponding increase in herbage use efﬁciency.
Figure 8 shows the relationships between herbage
growth rate, stocking density and defoliation interval,
and the role of leaf lifespan in determining the
efﬁciency of the grazing process. In continuous stocking
systems maintained in a steady state (sward height,
herbage mass or leaf area index) over a period of time,
any reduction in herbage growth rate, whatever its
cause (N shortage, low temperature, drought, plant
species and genotype), will lead to a corresponding
decrease in stocking density in order to maintain the
speciﬁed target of sward state. This will lead to an
increase in leaf defoliation interval that, depending on
the leaf lifespan of the plant species and genotype
considered, will reduce the average number of defoli-
ation events on a single leaf before it starts to
senescence. Given the relatively constant severity of
defoliation, the proportion of the herbage growth
which is actually consumed by grazing decreases, and
the proportion of leaf tissue senescing and returning to
soil as litter increases, indicating that grazing efﬁciency
is reduced with decreasing herbage growth.

These results conﬁrm the view that patterns of leaf
turnover and tiller morphogenesis are similar in swards
or pastures under continuous and rotational stocking,
and that levels of herbage production and utilization
would be expected to be essentially the same at a similar
leaf area index (Parsons (1994); Parsons et al., 1988;
Parsons and Chapman, 2000). The main distinction is
that frequency of defoliation under continuous stocking
is simply a function of the stocking density required to
maintain constant sward or pasture conditions, but
under rotational management these variables can be
controlled and manipulated independently. This distinc-
tion is unimportant in swards or pastures with high
growth potential (Parsons and Chapman, 2000) but
becomes more important where herbage growth and
frequency of defoliation of leaves are low. Under these
conditions, plants with relatively fast leaf turnover may
be disadvantaged under continuous stocking by loss of
tissue to senescence because of a long defoliation interval
relative to leaf lifespan and a consequent loss of grazing
efﬁciency.
Conventional systems of pasture production in tem-
perate conditions reﬂect the importance of grazing
efﬁciency (Parsons et al., 1988; Parsons and Chapman,
2000), and are based largely on the use of plant species
with short lifespan and high rates of tissue turnover.
However, these may not be the most appropriate plant
characteristics for adaptation to less favourable climates
or for use in more extensive management systems. In
these circumstances knowledge of leaf lifespan and leaf
morphogenesis of alternative native or introduced
species is likely to be important to plant survival and
plant production. Genotypes with short leaf lifespan
should have priority use in favourable conditions for
herbage production with relatively high inputs of N
fertilizer, while species having long leaf lifespan should
be better adapted to low frequency of defoliation, low
herbage production and low stocking density. The fact
that long leaf lifespan may be associated with a
relatively high content of structural tissue (Al Haj
Khaled et al., 2005) implies a trade-off between herbage
quantity and quality in extensive as well as in intensive
production systems. Duru and Hubert (2003) illustrate
the use of information on leaf lifespan in planning the
management of mixed-species native pasture, and
Fulkerson and Donaghy (2001) make effective use of
the same principle in recommendations for a grazing
strategy based on a cyclical rest-period of three leaf
appearance intervals for L. perenne swards under rota-
tional management.
This evidence also provides a rationale for the devel-
opment of grazing management systems over the last
50 years. In general terms, efﬁciency of grazing is a direct
function of stocking density in continuous stocking
systems, and can only be optimized if herbage growth
rates are high. On the other hand, in rotational stocking
systems the efﬁciency of herbage utilization is a direct
function of the interval between grazings, which can be
adjusted in order to allow optimization of grazing
efﬁciency according to the rhythm of plant growth.
As a result, rotational stocking systems are more ﬂexible
and allow for a wider range of adjustments regarding
environmental growth conditions, plant species and
cultivars, and fertilizer policy. In continuously stocked
swards, when herbage growth is reduced, e.g. low N
inputs, the reduction in herbage consumption would be
proportionally larger than the reduction in herbage
production as a consequence of the direct link between
herbage use efﬁciency and herbage growth, as described
in Figure 8. In rotationally stocked swards, since defoli-
ation interval is not directly dependent on stocking
density but is instead a decision made by the system
manager, the decrease in herbage use efﬁciency with
reductions in herbage growth can be largely avoided
(Lemaire and Chapman, 1996; Figure 14Æ6).
Fifty years ago, a typical grazing system in Europe
reﬂected low herbage production, and swards or
pastures were usually subject to continuous stocking
that led to low herbage use efﬁciency. The ﬁrst impact
of research was then to introduce sub-division and
rotational stocking practices, the main effect of which
was to improve herbage use efﬁciency. During the
1960s, the use of increasing rates of N fertilizer led
to large increases in herbage production with a
corresponding proportional increase in herbage
consumption. Then, during the 1980s, researchers
discovered that it was possible to remove fences and
obtain levels of herbage production and grazing
efﬁciency similar to those obtained using rotational
stocking systems (Parsons et al., 1988). This apparent
paradox can be explained by the fact that, in intensive
continuous stocking systems, herbage growth rates
are high enough to support high stocking densities
and, as a result, defoliation interval (10–12 d) is short
relative to leaf lifespan (20–25 d in the case of L. perenne
for example), allowing a grazing efﬁciency of about
0Æ70.

Current emphasis on extensiﬁcation of grasslands
through reduction of inputs of N fertilizer for both
environmental and biodiversity objectives may reverse
these effects. From the point of view of carbon cycling,
extensiﬁcation, using continuous stocking, would
increase the quantity of litter returning to soil for
carbon sequestration in soil organic matter. This could
be beneﬁcial for reducing the effects of greenhouse
gases but, from an animal production point of view, it
would be less efﬁcient. On the other hand, extensiﬁca-
tion through rotational stocking management could
maintain efﬁciency of herbage utilization and optimize
animal production since a greater proportion of the leaf
tissues produced would be grazed but carbon seques-
tration into soil organic matter would be reduced.
Moreover, the higher efﬁciency of herbage use,
obtained using rotational stocking, would be accompa-
nied by a greater release of carbon as CH4 emissions.
There is clearly a trade-off between optimizing efﬁ-
ciency of herbage use and animal production outputs,
and maximizing the role of grasslands for carbon
sequestration in soil organic matter.
Conclusion
This review of published and unpublished data on
frequency and severity of defoliation of different grass
species under continuous stocking conditions provides
an understanding of the interaction between plant
morphological traits and grazing management. Rota-
tional and continuous stocking systems are recognized to
be similar in terms of processes at a tiller level, but a clear
distinction is shown between the two management
systems in their capacity to disconnect the inﬂuence of
stocking density on the frequency of leaf defoliation. This
distinction is important because, as herbage growth rate
declines due to climate change or plant nutrient con-
straints, efﬁciency of herbage use also decreases under
continuous stocking, but can be maintained approxi-
mately constant under rotational stocking.
The main practical consequence is that continuous
stocking should be reserved for relatively high herbage
growth rate conditions where it would be expected to
perform as well as rotational stocking. In low herbage
growth conditions, whatever the cause, it should be
preferable to use rotational management. The second
practical consequence is that the choice between
continuous and rotational stocking should take into
account the morphological characteristics of the grass
species considered. For species with a short leaf lifespan,
such as L. perenne and F. arundinacea, the use of
rotational stocking, where grazing intervals approxi-
mately match leaf lifespan, should allow high efﬁciency
of utilization when plant growth rates are slow.
Conversely, when growth rates are high, and high
stocking densities and defoliation frequencies are
required to ensure high grazing efﬁciency, continuous
stocking may be the preferred management, particu-
larly if a rapid rotation is not a feasible option. As
conditions for herbage growth become restricting,
grazing efﬁciency under continuous management
would be expected to decline sharply. This emphasizes
the importance of good information on the morpho-
logical characteristics of alternative native and culti-
vated species, with particular emphasis on tropical
species, in determining suitability for speciﬁc conditions
of pasture production and management.
