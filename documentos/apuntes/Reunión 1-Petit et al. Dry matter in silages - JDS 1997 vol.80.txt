ABSTRACT
Dry matter (DM) contents of 90 silages were de-
termined by 1) toluene distillation (corrected for
volatile fatty acids and ethanol in the distillate), 2)
gas chromatography, 3) Karl Fisher titration, and 4)
oven-drying at 65°C. The mean DM content of silage
as determined by Karl Fisher titration (33.6%) was
higher than values obtained by other methods. Values
of DM determined by gas chromatography (31.8%)
were similar to those determined by toluene distilla-
tion (31.3%) but higher than those obtained by oven-
drying (31.0%). The highest correlation (r = 0.9928)
among DM values was between those determined by
gas chromatography and those determined by Karl
Fisher titration. However, higher values for DM con-
tent obtained with the Karl Fisher titration method
and the potential health hazard of toluene utilization
suggest that gas chromatography is a better choice to
determine precisely DM content in silages. Karl
Fisher titration is a simple and rapid method that
requires less sophisticated equipment than does gas
chromatography and would be more practical for
some laboratories. Oven-drying could be acceptable to
determine DM in a large number of samples used for
farm purposes.

INTRODUCTION
Determination of DM is important in animal nutri-
tion because imprecise DM measurements result in
inaccurate estimation of DMI, feed efficiency, and
digestibility. Different methods to determine DM in
fermented feeds have been examined by several
researchers. Dewar and McDonald ( 5 ) developed a
distillation technique using toluene ( TD), and this
procedure or its modifications have become recom-
mended standard procedures (2, 14). However, TD is
a time-consuming procedure requiring 1.5 to 8 h of
distillation before consistent results can be obtained
(2). Moreover, toluene is a hazard to human health
because it is potentially carcinogenic.
Fenton et al. ( 8 ) developed a procedure using gas
chromatography ( GC) to measure water in fer-
mented feeds. This GC method involves extraction of
the water with alcohol, followed by the separation of
the water and alcohol using a thermal conductivity
detector. The GC has the advantage of being very fast
and as accurate as TD for determining moisture.
However, GC requires access to a gas chromatograph
with a thermal conductivity detector, which is not
always readily available.
Karl Fisher titration ( KF) is a relatively cheap
and well-established technique for direct measure-
ment of water in pet foods that average 20 to 30%
moisture and contain volatile components (18).
Moreover, KF has produced DM estimates similar to
those obtained with the oven-drying ( OD) procedure
corrected for volatile products (6), which demon-
strates that KF measures the volatile components
separately from water. Kaiser et al. (13) reported
that KF resulted in higher DM estimates than did
TD, although the difference between both methods
varied with silages. No comparison has been found,
however, between KF and GC using TD as the stan-
dard to determine moisture content in silages.
Moreover, experiments comparing methods of
moisture determination have been carried out mainly
with small silos and small numbers of silages (3, 8, 9,
13). The objectives of this experiment were to com-
pare different procedures to determine the moisture
content of silages and to determine whether KF was

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Ninety samples of silage (57 grasses, 18 legumes,
and 15 legume and grass mixtures) were collected
from 20 farms in northwestern Que´bec at the time of
feeding. All samples, which averaged 2 kg, were
thoroughly mixed before being separated in two por-
tions and were kept frozen until analysis. The first
portion was used to determine DM by 1) TD (cor-
rected for VFA and ethanol in the distillate) (5) and
2) OD at 65°C for 3 d. The second portion was used to
determine DM by both KF ( 9 ) and GC ( 8 ) following
some modifications. The same methanol extract from
each silage was used for the KF and GC procedures.
Two replicates per sample were analyzed for each DM
method.
The second portion of silage was chopped with a
Hobart food cutter (model 84145; Hobart Canada
Inc., Don Mills, ON, Canada). Approximately 20 g of
chopped silage were added to a screw-top, 500-ml
Pyrex bottle equipped with autoclavable polypropy-
lene plug-seal caps and drip-free clear polypropylene
pouring rings, and a known weight of anhydrous
methanol (150 ml) was added. The bottles were
sealed, shaken, and allowed to stand for 24 h for
equilibration of the water and methanol. The bottles
were shaken five to six times. Standards, containing 0
to 15% (wt/wt) water, were prepared in the same
methanol that was used for the extractions. Superna-
tant (10 ml) was withdrawn, rapidly injected into a
tube, and centrifuged at 3500 × g for 10 min. One
portion of the clear supernatant (0.5 ml) was with-
drawn using a Hamilton syringe (Fisher Scientific,
Montreal, QC, Canada) and rapidly injected into a
titration vessel of approximately 100 ml. The vessel
contained about 10 ml of anhydrous methanol previ-
ously titrated to eliminate possible traces of water.
Another portion was placed in a 4-ml chromatography
vial and subsequently analyzed according to Fenton
et al. (8).
The KF was controlled by a platinum double elec-
trode (Metrohm model AG-9100 Herisau; Brinkmann
Instruments Ltd., Rexdale, ON, Canada) using an
automatic titroprocessor (Metrohm model 672; Brink-
mann Instruments Ltd.) and a titration stand (model
Dosimat Metrohm 655; Brinkmann Instruments
Ltd.). The end point was indicated by a sharp change
of potential. The water percentage in the silages was
calculated from a calibration curve obtained with
water solutions in methanol of 5, 10, and 15% (wt/
wt) that showed excellent linearity (r > 0.999). The
water values were converted to their complements to
100 (i.e., KF-100) and are reported as such in the
tables.
Separation of water by GC, calculation of silage
water, and calculation of silage DM by difference
were according to the methods described by Fenton et
al. (8). A Fisher gas partitioner (model 1200; Fisher
Scientific, Ottawa, ON, Canada) with a thermal con-
ductivity detector and 80/100 mesh Porapak QS
column were used. A 2-ml sample was injected, and
data output was collected with a Hewlett Packard
integrator (model 3392A; Hewlett Packard, Toronto,
ON, Canada). Triplicate injections of the same ex-
tract were made for each sample. Chemical composi-
tion of silages was determined using the methods
described by Petit and Veira (16).
Results of DM determination were analyzed by
ANOVA; sources of variation were sample and
method of DM determination. The interaction be-
tween sample and method of DM determination was
used as the error term. Differences among treatment
means were assessed by least squares means separa-
tion using Scheffe’s test (17) for a significant F test
( P < 0.05). Correlations between DM methods were
obtained with Pearson correlation test, and regres-
sions to predict DM values were obtained using the
procedures of SAS (17). Significance was declared at
P < 0.05 unless otherwise stated.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Most of the variation in the experiment (data not
shown) was explained by the model used as indicated
by a high r2 value (0.98) and a low coefficient of
variation (4.3%). Samples of silage used to determine
DM content covered a large range of forages usually
fed to animals. The composition of these silages is
presented in Table 1. Some values for DM content,
however, were higher than the range of 25 to 30%
that is usually observed for heap silage (15, 16). In
general, mean values were close to those in the data
reported by Petit and Veira (16) for timothy that had
been ensiled in heap silos under climatic conditions
similar to those of the present experiment.
Values of DM determined by GC (31.79%) and TD
(31.30%) were similar, which agreed with results of
Burdick and McHan (3). Those researchers ( 3 ) com-
pared grass silages containing 28 to 67% DM.
Moreover, Clancy et al. ( 4 ) found no difference be-
tween DM estimates as determined by saponification
and DM estimates of alfalfa silage as determined by
TD and corrected for acids in the distillate and water
extracted with ethanol (12).

Silage DM was higher when determined by KF
than when determined by TD (33.64% vs. 31.30%;
pooled SE = 0.07). Similar results were obtained by
Kaiser et al. (13), although in that experiment the
size of the difference between both methods was
reduced as silage DM increased. Such an effect was
not observed in the present experiment, and the
difference between KF and TD generally was similar
for all samples (Figure 1). Aerts et al. ( 1 ) reported
that the correction of Dewar and McDonald ( 5 ) for
acids in the distillate was based on silages that con-
tained small amounts of ammonia; silages with large
quantities of ammonia overestimated water content
and underestimated DM because a large amount of
ammonia was recovered in the distillate and was
considered to be water. According to Dulphy and
Demarquilly (6), silages with more than 7% of total
N as ammonia are not excellent quality, which might
explain the lower DM content as determined by TD
compared with that determined by KF in the present
experiment; ammonia concentration was high in our
silages, and TD was corrected for ethanol and for VFA
according to the empirical formula of Dewar and
McDonald ( 5 ) without considering ammonia.
However, because DM results were higher for KF
than for GC (33.64% vs. 31.79%; P < 0.05), although
the same methanol extraction was used for both tech-
niques, other unknown factors in silage appeared to
interact with water measurement by KF.
There was no difference in DM values between TD
(31.30%) and OD at 65°C (30.99%), which was unex-
pected because OD usually results in lower values
than TD because of the loss of volatile products (2, 3,
5, 14). However, Kaiser et al. (13) observed similar
DM estimates that had been determined by TD and
OD at 80°C without finding any explanation. No cor-
rections for the ammonia found in the distillate were
made in the present experiment, which could have
contributed to the overestimation of water content as
determined by TD and could have resulted in the
similar DM values for OD and TD. According to
Dewar and McDonald (5), large amounts of ammonia
in the distillate would overestimate water content
and underestimate DM value. Values of DM obtained
with OD (30.99%) were lower ( P < 0.05) than those
observed with KF (33.64%) and GC (31.79%),
although the difference was not significant between
OD and TD (30.99% vs. 31.30%). This result agrees
with the fact that DM estimates determined by TD
were intermediate to those determined by OD and

temperature of 135, 110, and 80°C, respectively. The
mean difference between TD and OD (0.3 percentage

reported by Haigh (10) for a drying temperature of
100°C. Dewar and McDonald ( 5 ) reported differences
ranging from 0.4 to 1.6 percentage units between OD
and TD, which, at the lower end, was close to our
value. Differences in the extent of silage fermentation
(11) and the drying temperature (14) seemed to be
responsible for the size of the difference observed
between TD and OD.
Correlations among methods of DM determination
(Table 2) were all significant ( P < 0.001). The
highest correlation obtained was between GC and KF.
Correlations between TD and OD were also very high,
probably because high amounts of ammonia were con-
sidered to be water with both techniques. High corre-
lations between DM estimates determined by TD and
OD (r = 0.985) were reported by Haigh (10) for 2157
grass silages from bunkers. Correlations between
other methods of DM determination averaged 0.97.
The size of the difference between KF and GC esti-
mates did not depend on the range of DM values
obtained with TD, which remained fairly constant for
all silages (Figure 1).
The high correlations obtained between the esti-
mates determined by KF and GC could have resulted
because both methods involve the extraction of water
with alcohol. In GC, water and alcohol were separated
(8), and, using KF methods, water contained in alco-
hol was titrated (18). Water is measured quantita-
tively by GC ( 8 ) and KF (9, 18) in fermented feeds
containing volatile components other than moisture.
According to Burdick and McHan (3), GC is more
precise than TD for measuring water quantitatively
because volatile products were found with water
when TD was used to determine DM, but only water
was measured when KF and GC were used to deter-
mine DM. Moreover, the use of an empirical formula
for the correction of products in the distillate with TD
may lead to variation in DM determination. Large
amounts of ethanol (2), ammonia (7), and volatile
fatty acids (14) influenced DM determination by TD,
which could explain why the correlations between DM
estimates obtained by TD and KF and those between
DM estimates obtained by TD and GC were not as
high as those between DM estimates obtained by KF
and GC. Loss of volatile products during OD also
could explain why correlations were not as high as
those obtained with methods measuring water more
precisely (GC and KF).
Parameters used to predict DM values from the
different methods are presented in Table 3. The r2
value generally was higher than 0.95, which demon-
strated that most variation was explained by the
method of DM determination. However, the high
correlation (r = 0.9928) obtained between DM esti-
mates by KF and those obtained by GC suggested
that KF is valuable for predicting silage DM value
after corrections with the regression equation, con-
sidering that GC precisely measures water in fer-
mented feeds (8). The KF and OD methods would be
more readily available in laboratories than GC;
however, because KF measures water more precisely

CONCLUSIONS
Determination of DM was similar for TD and GC,
although GC would be a better choice than TD be-
cause toluene is potentially carcinogenic. Compared
with GC and TD, KF resulted in higher DM contents,
suggesting that methanol extract interferes with KF
and needs to be identified if KF is to be used for
accurate DM determination. Research would be
needed to determine why the GC and KF methods
produce different results. Estimates of DM deter-
mined by KF and GC were highly correlated, suggest-
ing that DM determined by GC could be calculated
from data obtained from KF using a regression equa-
tion. The KF method is simple, requires less sophisti-
cated equipment than does GC, and could be more
practical for some laboratories. The KF method allows
DM determination of silage samples in a relatively
short time. Silage DM was similar for OD and TD,
and no difference was found between wet and dry
samples; OD could be an acceptable method for deter-
mining DM in a large number of samples used for
farm purposes.
