ABSTRACT 
Contributions of various biochemical processes to overall energy expenditure in the 
gastrointestinal tract (GIT) and liver have been assessed in this review. The GF and liver 
are responsible for a disproportionately high fraction of whole-body energy utilization. The 
energetic cost of Na+, K+-ATPase, protein synthesis and degradation, substrate cycling and 
urea synthesis contribute substantially to energy expenditure in the ruminant. In the 
splanchnic bed, these biochemical processes account for approximately 22.8% of whole- 
body 02 and, consequently, ATP utilization; they are influenced by several factors, 
including dietary composition, level of intake, age, endocrine status and physiological state. 
In the GIT and liver, the energetic cost of Na+, K+-ATPase is by far the most energetically 
demanding process; it is related to the active transport of substrates and the maintenance of 
ionic homeostasis. The high rate of protein synthesis in the GlT is associated with cellular 
turnover and sloughing, secretion and enzymatic action. In the liver, protein synthesis is 
important in the mediation of hormonal induction, which influences regulation of body 
systems, synthesis of plasma proteins, enzymatic and cellular turnover and detoxification of 
blood. Regulation of these processes and the signals involved in the differential 
contribution of each biochemical event are not well understood. The large contribution of 
these biochemical events in the GF and liver to whole-animal energy utilization suggests 
that their manipulation may alter the energetic efficiency of meat, milk or wool production. 
(Key Words: Digestive Tract, Liver, Ion Transport, Protein Synthesis, Protein Degradation, 
Energy Expenditure.)

static. For example, when intake level was

Physiological state causes substantial 
changes in ancillary energy expenditure, espec-

The large contribution of the GIT to whole- 
body protein synthesis has been established in 
rats (McNurlan et al., 1979) and ruminants

synthesis. The intestinal contribution to whole-

the body (Lobley et al., 1980). This is in 
contrast to the musculature, which contributes

only accounts for 25% of wholsbody protein

tein synthesis is an obvious energetic demand 
on the animal; therefore, the extent of GIT

whole-animal energy use for carcass growth. 
Protein synthesis is affected dramatically by 
level of alimentation, physiological state and 
maturity. Starvation induces a decline in 
intestinal weight as well as a 40% depression 
in the fractional rate of protein synthesis 
(McNurlan et al., 1979) and decreased intesti- 
nal epithelium cell division and migration to 
the villus tip (Lob et al., 1974). This 
depression may be associated with decreased 
secretory protein synthesis, reduced intracellu- 
lar protein synthesis or depressed cellular 
production (McNurlan et al., 1979). All these 
factors ultimately would alter the intracellular 
energy directed toward protein synthesis. 
During the perinatal period, protein synthe- 
sis in the GIT is higher in the gut than in the 
rest of the body (Goldspink et al., 1984). The 
growth of skeletal muscle from weaning to 
sexual maturity is higher, however. This 
illustrates that gut protein synthesis relative to 
whole-body protein synthesis changes with 
growth and maturity (Reeds, 1988). In the 
preruminant lamb, the contribution of the total 
GIT to whole-body protein synthesis is 11.5%, 
with the small intestine itself accounting for 
8.8% of whole-body protein synthesis (Attaix 
and Amal, 1987). In contrast, the values for 
the ruminant lamb (18 to 34%; Davis et al., 
1981) and for cattle (32.1 to 45.9%; Lobley et 
al., 1980) are higher, indicating that as the GIT 
develops, demand for ATP and 02 increases.

Protein Degradation in the 
Gastrointestinal Tract 
Intracellular protein degradation is essential 
to the process of intracellular control, given 
that it ensures turnover of aberrant proteins 
and maintains enzymes and structural compo- 
nents within the cells (Summers et al., 1988). 
There are two major routes for proteins to be 
degraded: non-lysosomal ATPdependent deg- 
radation and lysosomal degradation (Hershko 
and Ciechanover, 1982). The two processes of 
intracellular protein degradation will be 
described briefly and, where possible, the 
inputs of ATP to suppon each process will be 
highlighted. The majority of the research 
defining the molecular basis of intracellular 
protein degradation has been conducted in 
perfused livers or isolated hepatocytes. We 
assume that similar systems for intracellular 
protein degradation are operative in the GIT. 
For a detailed coverage of intracellular protein 
degradation, readers are directed to excellent 
review papers of Mayer and Doherty (1986) or 
Hershko (1988). 
The absolute requirement for metabolic 
energy (Ciechanover, 1987) for the ATP- 
dependent, non-lysosomal degradation of in- 
tracellular proteins was first demonstrated 
when Schweiger et al. (1956) prevented the 
breakdown of endogenous protein in the 
reticulocyte by blocking ATP synthesis. 
Studies indicated that the ATP-requiring con- 
jugation of ubiquitin to intracellular proteins 
has importance in energy-dependent protein 
degradation (Hershko et al., 1984; Finley and 
Varshavsky, 1985). 
Ubiquitin, a 76-residue polypeptide, is 
present in most eucaryotic cells in either a free 
form or covalently linked to many different

After transference of E3 (catalyzed by a second

Lysosomal protein degradation, on the other 
hand, appears to be nonselective (Hershko, 
1988); quantitatively it is the most dominant 
system of intracellular protein catabolism 
during normal (basal) or enhanced (pathologi- 
cal conditions or nutritional deprivation) states 
of protein degradation (Mayer and Doherty, 
1986). Furthermore, both short- and long-lived 
proteins may be degraded in lysosomes. This 
system involves initial sequestering of the 
protein into a membrane-bound vacuole (au- 
tophagosome) followed by fusion with a 
primary or secondary lysosome. Protein degra- 
dation is catalyzed in an acidic environment 
via numerous proteases (Mayer and Doherty, 
1986). The input of ATP to support this 
system is largely indirect and would encom- 
pass formation of the autophagosome and 
lysosome along with maintenance of the high 
H+ concentrations within the lysosome via 
operation of Htpumps. 
There are no estimates of the energy cost of 
protein degradation in the GlT. However, in a 
proliferative tissue such as the GI", as little as 
10% of protein synthesis is required for 
production of new mucosal tissue, indicating 
that protein degradation is an active and 
continuing process (Goldspink et al., 1984; 
Reeds, 1988). Assuming 1 mmol ATP is used 
per peptide bond degraded (Gill et al., 
1989a,b) and 5 mmol AW/peptide bond 
synthesized, protein degradation could account 
for 16.6% of ATP utilization for protein 
mover. Protein turnover in the GlT accounts 
for 4.9 to 5.5% of whole-body energy expendi- 
ture. Protein degradation contributes only a 
small amount to GIT and whole-body energy 
expenditures (Table 2). This estimate contrasts 
with that of Siems et al. (1984), who stated 
that protein degradation in the reticulocyte 
accounted for 13.7% of cellular 02 consump- 
tion. This value should be considered to be a 
maximum, given that reticulocyte protein 
degradation is almost entirely driven by non- 
lysosomal ATPdependent protein degradation.

sis and protein degradation. They account for 
53.0 to 90.4% of total GlT energy expenditure 
or 10.6 to 17.9% of whole-body @ utilization 
in the ruminant (Table 2), yet these tissues

Protein Synthesis in 
the Liver 
The energy cost of protein synthesis in the 
liver, as determined by microcalorimetry and 
cycloheximide blocking of protein synthesis, 
accounts for 15.5 and 24.4% of total hepatic 
energy use in euthyroid and hyperthyroid 
sheep, respectively (McBride and Early, 1989; 
Table 5). These estimates are similar to values 
reported for isolated rabbit hepatocytes using 
stoichiometric relationships between protein 
synthesis and coupled aerobic metabolism (5 
mol ATP per mole of amino acid incorporated 
and 1 mol of oxygen used in the synthesis of 6 
mol ATP). Using these criteria, protein synthe- 
sis accounted for 26.5% of total hepatocyte 
ATP use (Reeds, 1987). Lower estimates for 
the energy cost of protein synthesis in liver 
have been reported for liver slices (3 to 5% of 
total 02 use; Ismail-Beigi et al., 1976); 
however, rates of protein synthesis in this 
preparation were extremely low. In contrast, 
the fractional rates of protein synthesis for 
isolated hepatocytes (McBride and Early, 
1989; Table 5) were similar to in vivo 
estimates of liver protein synthesis (Lobley et 
al., 1980; Early et al., 1988b).

protein synthesis remains relatively constant 
compared to the energy costs associated with 
Na+, K+-ATPase activity. This reflects the 
tight regulation of the inputs of ATP into 
protein synthesis and suggests that the ener- 
getic efficiency of protein accretion is dictated 
by other variables such as the extent and

Protein Degradation 
in the Liver 
Intracellular protein degradation in the 
hepatocyte occurs via the ubiquitin-mediated 
system and through lysosomal protein degrada- 
tion. The dominant system of protein degrada- 
tion likely changes depending on the nature of 
the protein degraded and the intracellular 
signalling process induced. The consumption 
of 02 to support the two major protein 
degradation processes also may be different. 
For example, the energetic inputs into the 
lysosomal system of protein degradation are 
difficult to estimate stoichiometrically but 
include maintenance of a H+ gradient across 
the lysosomal membrane, endocytosis and 
formation of the autophagic vacuole. In liver, 
this system is under multiphasic control, being 
chiefly modulated by seven regulatory amino 
acids (Leu, Try, Gln, Pro, Met, His and Trp), 
along with alanine, insulin and glucagon 
(Mortimore et al., 1987. 1988). The amino acid 
balance reaching the liver can drastically alter 
the extent and components of hepatocytic 
protein degradation. Perfusion of the liver with 
normal plasma concentrations of the seven 
regulatory amino acids vs no regulatory amino 
acids resulted in a shift in the distribution of 
smooth and rough endoplasmic reticulum, 
mitochondria and other organelles within au- 
tophagic vacuoles. The appearance of normal 
concentrations of regulatory amino acids fa- 
vored sequestering smooth endoplasmic reticu- 
lum by four- to five-fold compared to similar 
perfusions with no regulatory amino acids. The 
degree of complexity and control of the 
lysosomal protein degradation system would 
imply the indirect use of ATP to support the 
system. 
In contrast, the ubiquitin system of protein 
degradation has direct inputs of ATP as 
discussed previously. In reticulocytes, 
mitochondrial protein breakdown occurs via 
the ATP-ubiquitin process. In this specialized 
cell-type, mitochondrial proteins dominate the 
proteins degraded intracellularly. Siems et al. 
(1984) estimated that intracellular protein 
degradation within reticulocytes accounts for 
13.7% of total cellular energy expenditure. 
This would transpose into a stoichiometry of at 
least 1 mol ATPipeptide bond cleaved 
(Rapoport et al., 1985). Others have estimated 
that the cost of aberrant protein degradation 
may be as high as 2 or 3 ATP/peptide bond 
cleaved (Menon and Goldberg, 1985). Regard- 
less of the protein degradation system em- 
ployed, the ATP cost per peptide bond cleaved 
may vary across cell type and with different 
intracellular induction. In our estimates for the 
liver, we assumed that protein degradation 
occurs at a minimal cost of 1 ATP/peptide 
bond degraded. Using this stoichiometry, Gill 
et al. (1989a,b) estimated that protein degrada- 
tion accounted for 3.4% of hepatocyte 02 
consumption.
