Summary 
During the past 25 yr, animal scientists 
have learned much about the effects of environ- 
mental factors, nutritional treatments, the use 
of anabolic agents and genetic selection on 
growth of animals. Progress has been made 
in terms of body and carcass composition 
as well as a more fundamental understanding 
of growth at the cellular level. Current emphasis 
is directed towards action of hormones on 
tissue metabolism, factors regulating hormone 
secretion, mechanisms of synthesis and deg- 
radation of proteins and the energetic effi- 
ciency of protein and fat synthesis. The overall 
objectives are to control growth of animals 
with respect to rate and composition of gain 
and to improve the efficiency of growth. 
(Key Words: Growth, Animal, Muscle, Adipose, 
Hormone.) 
Introduction 
The study of animal growth and develop- 
ment is often discussed and described as the 
integration of all facets of animal science 
including nutrition, genetics or animal breed- 
ing, physiology and meat science. Through 
interdisciplinary research, animal scientists 
have discovered many of the mechanisms 
that contribute to the general regulation of 
the growth process, but many specific regula- 
tory mechanisms remain unknown. 
The earlier literature on growth and de- 
velopment of farm animals has been thoroughly 
reviewed by Brody (1945) and Palsson (1955). 
Hammond and his colleagues at Cambridge 
made a significant contribution in quantifying 
the changes in anatomy related to growth 
of farm animals. Berg and Butterfield (1976) 
refined the anatomical approach for studying 
growth of muscle, fat and bone and clarified

growth process have been the studies of the

toward cellular mechanisms responsible for 
growth, studies of energetic efficiency of 
growth and the potential of genetic engineering 
to improve livestock production. 
The volume of literature related to the 
study of growth and development of animals 
has increased considerably since Andrews 
(1958) reviewed the physiology of growth 
and it is obvious that not all relevant research 
can be reviewed in depth in this brief com- 
munication. Our objectives are to summarize 
many of the major contributions during the 
past 25 yr by citing pertinent reviews and 
to indicate areas of contemporary interest 
and importance by citing selected research 
papers. We will relate these findings to the 
growth of the industry and future research 
needs. 
Concepts of Growth and Growth Curves 
Animal growth has long been characterized 
by observing the change in body weight per 
unit of time or by plotting body weight against 
age. The former provides values that can be 
used to easily compare the effects of treat- 
ments or to describe the rate of growth of 
animals. The latter method results in the 
production of growth curves that have been 
employed to describe the patterns of growth 
of animals or tissues. 
A significant portion of growth-related 
research reported during the past 25 yr has 
been based on the concept of relative growth 
rather than the analysis of growth curves. 
Although not as complex as analyzing growth 
curves, relative growth rates expressed as

An extension of the use of growth curves 
to compare animals across species, breeds 
or sexes has been termed genetic size scaling 
(Taylor, 1980a,b). Through size scaling, growth 
curves can be calculated for different species 
and standardized for degree of mature body

spe'cies revealed sigmoidal growth curves of 
a similar shape, but significant deviations 
from the mean species curve were noted and 
it was indicated that all species do not reach 
the same fraction of their mature size at a 
given metabolic age (Taylor, 1980c). This 
relatively new method of examining growth 
data is expected to be of significant value in 
future selection programs and in the study of 
the energetics of growth. 
Compositional Changes During Growth 
The initial sampling point to detect com- 
positional changes in animals is usually a 
few days after birth. This is not to imply 
that prenatal growth has been ignored by 
animal scientists, but suggests that additional 
emphasis should be placed on understanding 
the mechanisms responsible for both em- 
bryonic growth and fetal development. The 
primary components of the body at birth 
include the skeleton, muscles and internal 
organs with fat comprising less than 2% of 
body weight. Of these tissues, fetal develop- 
ment of the musculature has received the 
major emphasis and the histological informa- 
tion describing the orderly fusion of myoblasts, 
the formation of myotubes and the maturation 
to muscle fibers has been described in detail 
(Swatland and Cassens, 1973; Stromer et al., 
1974; Allen et al., 1979b). Additional emphasis 
on fetal growth and its control appear justified 
in view of the strong relationship between 
birth weight and postnatal survivability and 
growth rate. 
Postnatal growth is characterized by con- 
tinued growth of the skeleton, organs and 
musculature until the animal reaches approxi- 
mately 50 to 60% of its mature weight at 
which time skeletal and organ weight gain 
has slowed and fat deposition has increased 
to a modest rate. Researchers examining 
growth and body composition during the 
finishing period have used different end points 
resulting in some problems in interpreting 
data during the past. Some have examined 
the responses of animals to nutritonal treat- 
ments for a specific time and have slaughtered 
animals at a fixed age or after a prescribed 
number of days on feed. Other researchers 
have elected to feed animals to a constant 
weight (e.g., 105 kg for swine or 500 kg for 
cattle) without consideration for the geno- 
type of the animal and its corresponding ex- 
pected mature size. As a result, many growth- 
related experiments that should have produced 
information on feeding protocols and effi- 
ciency of growth to typical market conditions 
have yielded inconclusive results because 
animals were taken off experiments at various 
stages of physiological maturity. This problem 
is not as severe among swine studies as for 
cattle studies because the typical market 
weight has not varied appreciably for swine 
(e.g., 90 to 105 kg). Cattle, however, have 
undergone changes in the past 25 yr and 
the typical weight at which cattle are capable 
of bringing the maximum return has increased 
due to a shift toward animals that are later 
maturing and of much larger frame size. A 
lack of appreciation of the expected mature 
size of cattle can result in cattle being mar- 
keted at an immature point on their growth 
curve such that they have not displayed their 
genetic potential for muscle growth or fat 
deposition. These problems can often be 
avoided by planning experiments to feed 
cattle to a given degree of body composition 
(i.e., 10 mm of subcutaneous fat or approxi-

Although the degree of maturity of muscle 
fibers at the time of birth varies among live- 
stock species, it is generally accepted that 
the number of muscle fibers in a muscle does 
not increase appreciably after a few weeks 
of age. There are, however,~ differences in 
the number of muscle fibers as well as dif- 
ferences in muscle fiber diameter among 
animals of the same species, breed and sex 
during the early postnatal period and variation 
in both fiber size and number continue through 
the growth phases to maturity. In a compari- 
son of fast and slow maturing cattle, Herefords 
were found to have larger muscle fiber diam- 
eters at an earlier age than Charolais, but the 
Hereford cattle reached their mature muscle 
fiber diameter at a younger age than the 
Charolais (Guenther, 1974). Related research 
has shown that the rate of growth of muscle 
can be related to the rate of increase of the 
number of muscle nuclei through increased 
division of satellite celIs. Therefore, animals 
with a greater number of muscle fibers and 
a greater total amount of muscle DNA can 
be expected to have a greater rate of growth 
(Miller et al., 1975; Fowler et al., 1980). 
Rapid growth in the early growth phase 
is a time of great change in the skeletal system. 
During this time, bones are continually being 
remolded or reshaped to meet the stresses 
applied by the greater muscle mass and body 
weight. Animal scientists have acknowledged 
the importance of structural soundness in 
livestock, but have largely ignored skeletal 
growth and have concentrated more on the 
growth of muscle and the deposition of fat. 
However, as animals are placed into confine- 
ment systems and as genetic selection produces 
animals that grow even more rapidly, skeletal 
growth and the associated turnover of minerals 
and connective tissues must be considered 
in addition to the more traditional emphasis 
on muscle and fat. For extensive descriptions 
of the processes of bone growth and its control, 
readers are referred to reviews by Zobrisky 
(1969) and Raisz and Kream (1981). 
Adipose tissue shows relatively little change 
until the animal reaches approximately the 
middle of its rapid growth phase or when 
it attains approximately one-half of its physio- 
logical maturity. This is not to say that adipose 
tissue is undeveloped prior to this time because 
adipocytes differentiate early in life, but do not 
begin to fill appreciably until nutrient availabil- 
ity exceeds the capacity for skeletal and muscle 
growth. The dramatic increase in body fat 
associated with the later stages of growth 
represents a period of increased retention 
of energy by livestock (Allen et al., 1976; 
Mersmann, 1979). It is, however, often energy 
that is wasted in that excess subcutaneous 
fat is removed from carcasses during marketing 
and more is lost during the cooking process. 
The livestock industry, however, has signifi- 
cantly reduced the amount of fat on market 
cattle, swine and lambs during the past quarter 
century without a reduction in the eating 
quality of carcasses. 
Attempts to alter the shape or conforma- 
tion of animals and to shift the muscle mass 
toward the portions of the carcass that have 
greater economic value is an area of contro- 
versy that has intrigued animal scientists. 
The research of Butterfield (1963) and Berg 
and Butterfield (1976), however, has demon- 
strated that the weight of a given muscle 
remains a constant proportion of the total 
muscle mass even though differences in ap- 
parent conformation may exist among animals. 
Similar results have been reported by Cole 
et al. (1976) and Kempster et al. (1982) who 
concluded that conformation provides little 
information about the proportion of total 
lean in the higher priced cuts of carcasses 
from pigs, cattle and sheep. Although the 
conclusions have been examined often and 
supported by others, this will undoubtedly 
remain a topic of discussion for livestock 
and carcass show judges and participants. 
The sex of animals is an important factor 
affecting body composition during growth. 
It has long been accepted that intact males 
have leaner carcasses than females among 
cattle, swine and sheep, but barrows are 
typically fatter than gilts while steers and 
wethers are leaner than heifers and ewe lambs. 
Androgens and estrogens obviously play an 
important role in the differential growth 
patterns by males, females and castrates but 
the inconsistent ranking of the barrow as com- 
pared with the steer and wether is not easily 
explained. Although researchers have estab- 
lished many of the interactions among the 
metabolic hormones such as insulin, growth 
hormone, glucagon, thyroid hormones, adrenal 
cortical hormones and catecholamines, as

The scope of experiments designed to 
study factors affecting body composition 
often has been limited in the past by the 
lack of adequate techniques for assessing 
body composition of live animals. The avail- 
able methods have been reviewed (Cross, 
1982), but mention should be made of new 
techniques that appear to offer promise to 
animal scientists in the near future. The prob- 
lem of accurately estimating the amount 
of muscle in a live, growing animal has been 
of interest for years and has led to the proposi- 
tion of a number of techniques including 
prediction equations based on physical mea- 
surements, ultrasonic measurements, 4 o K count 
rates, isotopes and dye dilution techniques 
and the analysis of circulating metabolites. 
Few methods have withstood the test of 
time and the search continues for the ideal, 
noninvasive method that can accurately predict 
muscle and(or) fat mass in the live animal. 
An extensive amount of effort and interest 
is currently being expressed in methodology 
based on the dilution of deuterium oxide 
in the circulation of animals to predict changes 
in body water and hence estimate body muscle 
and fat content. A technique to estimate 
body composition of live animals would be 
especially valuable in many nutrition and 
genetic experiments. 
Cellular Aspects of Growth 
Growth and development of the whole 
animal is dependent on processes occurring 
at the cellular level. Growth of all tissues 
includes an increase in cell number (hyper- 
plasia) and an increase in cell size (hyper- 
trophy). Most of the hyperplasia, but not 
all for every tissue, occurs prenatally. At 
about the time of birth for most species, 
there is a period of hyperplasia and hyper- 
trophy, followed by a period of hypertrophy 
as animals mature (Robinson, 1971). 
Hypertrophy and hyperplasia determine 
to what extent an animal increases in weight 
and size and development determines the 
shape of an animal along with various functions 
of organs. The distinction between growth 
and development, however, is not absolute 
because the two usually do not occur indepen- 
dently. Development, such as sexual maturity, 
is often related to growth, but animals tend 
to develop even though growth may not be 
maximum. 
Growth impairment of young animals 
by undernutrition results in reduced hyper- 
plasia rather than a marked reduction in cell 
size, whereas weight loss is associated with 
a reduction in cell size rather than cell number 
(Winick and Noble, 1966). Recovery or re- 
establishment of cell size alone is faster follow- 
ing a period of weight loss than is the combined 
hyperplasia and hypertrophy associated with 
weight gain of young animals of a similar 
weight, which contributes to compensatory 
gain. 
Skeletal Muscle. The early stages of muscle 
development are fairly well understood and 
have been extensively reviewed elsewhere 
(Stromer et al., 1974; Allen et al., 1979a). 
Briefly, there is a proliferation of mono- 
nucleated cells, the presumptive myoblasts, 
which differentiate into myoblasts; groups 
of these fuse to form multinucleated myotubes 
which then acquire myofibrils to become 
muscle fibers. Very little is known about 
the cells which precede myoblasts. Myoblasts, 
and ultimately muscle cells, differentiate 
from precursor cells of the embryonic meso- 
derm. It has been postulated that several 
states of differentiation exist between meso- 
dermal cells and the differentiated myoblasts. 
The mononucleated cells preceding myoblasts 
have the ability to divide and thereby increase 
the population of myogenic cells. The non- 
dividing myoblasts have the capability of 
synthesizing and assembling myofibrillar pro- 
teins and the molecules for cell fusion to 
form myotubes and eventually muhinucleated 
skeletal muscle fibers. The number of fibers 
in a muscle is fixed at birth or soon after 
and the growth of muscle occurring in young 
animals is the result of hypertrophy of the 
fibers. Nuclei within muscle fibers do not 
divide but determination of DNA in growing 
muscle has demonstrated a considerable in- 
crease in the number of nuclei within muscles 
after birth in rats (Enesco and Puddy, 1964), 
chickens (Moss, 1968), swine (Robinson, 
1969), sheep (Rattray et al., 1975) and cattle 
(Trenkle et al., 1978). Additional nuclei are 
added to the fibers by mitosis of partially 
committed myogenic cells (satellite cells) 
that are small mononucleated cells residing 
between the plasmalemma and the basement

Adipose Tissue. Research into the mecha- 
nisms of differentiation of adipocytes and 
the growth and distribution of adipocytes 
has been extensive during the past 25 yr (Allen, 
1976; Hausman, 1978; Johnson, 1978; Leat 
and Cox, 1980). Adipose tissue has a lobular 
structure with fat cells developing in clusters 
around a capillary bed with the clusters of 
fat cells being supported in a matrix of con- 
nective tissue. Fat cells appear to arise from 
mesenchymal cells, but the precise pathway 
of differentiation leading to adipocytes is 
not established (See Allen et al., 1976 for 
a review of the histogenesis of adipose cells.). 
When the cells first start to accumulate lipids 
there are many lipid droplets within the cells. 
With continued filling, the small droplets 
coalesce into a large single droplet charac- 
teristic of mature adipocytes. Fat ceils with 
a single lipid droplet are usually seen in animals 
at or within a few days after birth (Leat and 
Cox, 1980). 
Adipose tissue grows initially by a com- 
bination of cellular enlargement and cellular 
proliferation. In a study with pigs (Anderson 
and Kauffman, 1973), the increase in adipose 
tissue between 1 and 2 mo was due primarily 
to the introduction of new cells. As the animals 
matured , hyperplasia was diminished and 
hypertrophy of existing cells was responsible 
for the increase in adipose tissue. In a similar 
study with cattle (Robelin, 1981), there was 
an increase in number of adipocytes up to 
8 mo of age (20% of mature body weight), 
remained constant up to 40% of mature weight 
and then increased between 45 to 55% of 
mature weight (13 and 16 mo of age). Fat 
cell size increased steadily from 4 to 13 mo 
of age. The cellularity of adipose tissue seems 
to be that number of cells reach a plateau 
early in life while cellular hypertrophy con- 
tinues until a maximum cell size is reached. 
At this point if there is need for continued 
deposition of fat (greater energy intake than 
required by the lean body mass), there seems 
to be an induction of hyperplasia as new small 
fat cells begin to appear (Robelin, 1981). 
It is not known if the new fat cells arise from 
cell division or represent development of 
dormant preadipocytes that were generated 
in an earlier growth phase. With current tech- 
niques, fat cells are only recognized after 
fat accumulation has begun or the cells reach 
a minimum diameter. Overall, cell hyper- 
trophy seems to contribute more than hyper- 
plasia to the increase in mass of adipose tissue 
in most growing animals. 
There are differences in growth patterns 
of various adipose tissues (Allen et al., 1976). 
For example, hyperplasia is more important 
to the growth of subcutaneous and perirenal 
adipose tissue as compared with other anatomi- 
cal locations in cattle. Intramuscular fat cells 
are smaller in size and contain more water 
than cells located subcutaneously or peri- 
renally. Hyperplasia seems to be more pro- 
longed and contributes more to the accumula- 
tion of lipids in intramuscular adipose than 
in other fat depots. 
Experiments have been conducted to study 
the effects of breed, sex and nutrition on 
cellularity of adipose tissues (Allen et al., 
1976). In general, if the mass of the tissue 
is altered there is a corresponding change in 
cell number and(or) cell size. Intact males 
usually contain less fat than castrated males 
or females and tend to have slower develop- 
ment of the cellularity of adipose tissues. 
The change in cellularity of adipose tissue 
caused by nutrition depends upon age or 
stage of development of the animal. Under- 
nutrition of the very young reduces hyper- 
plasia and hypertrophy in later maturing 
adipose tissues, but seems to have little effect 
on cell numbers in the early developing tissues. 
Turnover of Body Tissues 
The turnover of body tissues or their con- 
tinual synthesis and degradation allows muscle 
and adipose tissues to function as a reservoir 
of nutrients, primarily energy, in times of 
limited feed intake. In the well-fed animal 
turnover of body tissues, especially proteins,

of knowledge on factors controlling protein 
synthesis, there is limited information on the 
mechanisms of protein degradation. In addition 
there have been only a few studies of fractional

fractional rate of protein synthesis in organs

proteins (Buttery and Vernon, 1980). While 
it most certainly would not be desirable to

be advantages to reducing it in certain tissues 
such as muscle. Treatment of rats with the 
synthetic androgen, trenbolone acetate, reduces 
the rate of protein degradation (Vernon and

be possible to control protein degradation. 
There is a critical need for techniques that 
can be used with large animals to learn how 
to control protein degradation or to be used 
for selection of animals that might have lower 
degradation rates. 
Endocrine Control of Growth 
Numerous advances have been made in 
understanding the endocrine control of growth 
of animals. Much of the stimulus for this 
research was early studies demonstrating that 
extracts of the anterior pituitary or purified 
growth hormone could produce gigantism in 
rats and that exogenous hormones can be used 
to increase growth of cattle and sheep. The use 
of radioimmunoassay for measuring concen- 
trations of hormones in blood was a significant 
development for many of the recent studies. 
The use of natural and synthetic hormones as 
anabolic agents in animals has been reviewed by 
others (Preston, 1975; Galbraith and Topps, 
1981). 
Many hormones are involved in growth 
and development, so it has not been possible 
to establish a definite role for each hormone. 
Correlations between concentration of a 
hormone in blood and growth (rate of gain, 
protein or fat accretion, et cetera) for the 
most part have been low (Keller et al., 1979; 
Ohlson et al., 1981). Most likely hormones 
acting in concert, other growth factors, cellular 
receptors for hormones and metabolic clearance 
of hormones are also involved. Because ana- 
lytical techniques have been limited, most 
studies reported in the literature are too narrow 
in scope to be conclusive. In many studies 
only one or two hormones were measured, 
blood sampling was too infrequent to establish 
secretory patterns and consideration was not 
given to cellular receptors or metabolic clear- 
ance of hormones. Suitable methods have 
been developed for studying insulin receptors 
in adipose tissue of cattle and swine (Etherton, 
1982), but not in other tissues or for other 
hormones in these species. There continues 
to be a need for refinement of techniques 
and development of new techniques for study- 
ing the endocrine control of growth. 
Considerable interest in growth hormone 
has developed during the past 10 yr. Though 
there has not been enough purified growth 
hormone available for extensive studies with 
large animals, a few studies (Davis et al., 1970; 
Machlin, 1972; Moseley et al., 1982) indicate 
that exogenous growth hormone will produce 
an anabolic response in cattle, sheep and 
swine. The availability of growth hormone 
by use of the techniques of recombinant 
DNA will allow more studies to be conducted 
to determine the potential value of this hor- 
mone in animal production. Regulation of 
the secretion of growth hormone from the 
anterior pituitary has been studied in cattle, 
sheep and swine. Estrogens that have been 
used in cattle and sheep as feed additives 
or implants increase secretion of growth hor- 
mone (Preston, 1975). Somatostatin inhibits 
the secretion of growth hormone as well 
as hormone secretion from the pancreas. 
Recently a peptide that is'active as a growth 
hormone releasing factor was isolated from 
a pancreatic tumor (Rivier et al., 1982) and 
is considered to be similar to the growth 
hormone releasing factor of the hypothalamus. 
When the physiology of growth hormone 
secretion is understood, it may be possible 
to alter growth by regulating production 
of endogenous growth hormone. Another 
approach to changing the growth hormone 
status of growing animals has been to intro- 
duce foreign DNA into the mammalian genome 
(Palmiter et al., 1982). In these studies the 
DNA fragment containing the promoter of 
the mouse metallothionein-1 gene was fused 
to the structural gene of rat growth hormone 
and injected into the pronuclei of fertilized

The endocrine control of protein metabo- 
lism and muscle growth has been reviewed 
(Young and Pluskal, 1977; Buttery and Vernon, 
1980). Growth hormone increases protein 
synthesis and growth of muscle and the growth 
hormone status of steers has been reported 
to be positively correlated with total muscle 
mass (Trenkle and Topel, 1978). It has not 
been possible, however, to demonstrate that 
physiological concentrations of growth hor- 
mone stimulate hyperplasia of muscle cells 
in culture, which suggests that it is not acting 
directly on muscle cells. Ewton and Florini 
(1981) demonstrated that somatomedins, 
which are peptides produced by the liver 
and possibly other tissues under the influence 
of growth hormone, do stimulate hyperplasia 
of muscle cells in culture. Other related pep- 
tides, "insulin-like growth factors", are also 
found in serum and are considered to be 
among the factors regulating growth (Daugha- 
day, 1982). Somatomedin-like activity is 
highest in lambs (Falconer et al., 1979) and 
pigs (Gregory et al., 1977) that have the highest 
growth rate or are leaner. The development 
of techniques to measure concentration of 
these peptides in blood and receptors for 
them in tissues will help define their biological 
significance in growth and development. 
Insulin stimulates protein synthesis by in- 
creasing the intracellular supply of amino 
acids (Jefferson et al., 1977) and increasing 
the activity of the initiation factor of protein 
synthesis (Rannels et al., 1976). Protein gain 
of cattle has been observed to be positively 
correlated with concentrations of plasma 
insulin (Eversole et al., 1981). Androgens 
appear to have a direct anabolic effect upon 
muscles by combining with specific cyto- 
plasmic receptors (King and Mainwaring, 
1974) and act by changing the rate of tran- 
scription and translation. The role of thyroid 
hormones in protein synthesis is less clear. 
They promote muscle growth in hypothyroid 
animals but are catabolic at high concentrations 
(Bernal and Refetoff, 1977). The glucocorti- 
coids cause catabolism of proteins in muscle 
following reaction with receptors in the cyto- 
plasm (Young and Pluskal, 1977). Lengthening 
the photoperiod is associated with increased 
growth rate (Schanbacher and Crouse, 1980) 
and increased secretion of prolactin (Forbes 
et al., 1979), but there is no defined role 
of prolactin in muscle growth. 
Several aspects of intermediary metabolism 
and endocrine control of lipid metabolism 
were reviewed by Bauman (1976). From 
studies with rats it appears that insulin plays 
a dominant role in fatty acid synthesis and 
accumulation of lipids in adipose. Receptors 
for insulin have been shown to be present 
on fat ceils from cattle and pigs (Etherton, 
1982) and Vernon (1978) has shown insulin 
to be necessary for fatty acid synthesis in 
ovine adipose tissue cultured in vitro. The 
endocrine control of the release of fatty acids 
from adipose tissue has also received attention 
(Mersmann, 1979). It has been observed from 
studies with pigs (Wangsness et al., 1977; 
Gregory et al., 1977) that genetically lean 
animals do not have lower concentrations 
of plasma insulin, but are more resistant to 
insulin and have lower secretion of the hor- 
mone in response to provocative stimulation. 
The lean pigs also had significantly higher 
concentrations of plasma growth hormone 
and somatomedins. Eversole et al. (1981) 
and Trenkle and Topel (1978) found positive 
correlations between concentrations of plasma 
insulin and quantity of adipose tissue in fat- 
tening cattle. Prior and Smith (1982), how- 
ever, have not been able to demonstrate a 
major function of insulin in regulation of 
fat metabolism in cattle. 
Hormones can affect growth of animals 
in ways that would not be readily apparent 
from in vitro studies of action of hormones 
on tissues. Growth results from the uptake 
of nutrients from the blood for synthesis of 
the macromolecules of body tissues. Skeletal 
muscle and adipose tissue are quantitatively 
important in use of absorbed nutrients because 
of their mass, but the liver also has a dominant 
role because of its high metabolic activity 
and its anatomical location with respect to 
absorbed nutrients. A current view is that 
a major function of the endocrine system is 
integration of the metabolism of different 
tissues and regulation of the partitioning 
of nutrients among tissues of the body (Bines 
and Hart, 1978; Bauman et al., 1982).

Animal scientists have related these new 
areas of research to the process of animal 
growth and have stimulated an awareness 
of the basic concepts of growth and de- 
velopment among livestock producers who 
are now more interested in mature size 
of animals and genetic potential for growth 
as well as carcass composition. Within the 
next 25 yr it seems that it will be possible 
to exert more control of animal growth. 
At this time, fruitful areas of research appear 
to be micromanipulation of embryos to 
alter eventual hyperplasia and hypertrophy 
of muscle and adipose tissues; manipulation 
of the endocrine system to enhance growth 
rate and efficiency of growth; and reduction 
in turnover of body tissues, especially pro- 
tein, to improve the efficiency of growth. 
As more basic knowledge of growth is accu- 
mulated, animal scientists and livestock 
producers will be more able to identify 
animals with superior genetic potential 
in the conversion of grain and forage to 
body tissues.
