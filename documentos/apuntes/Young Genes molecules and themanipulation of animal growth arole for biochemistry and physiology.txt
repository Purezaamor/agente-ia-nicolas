Introduction 
A key objective in animal production is to 
achieve maximum growth, or some other pro­
ductive output such as milk or wool, within the 
context of an efficient use of feed and other 
necessary resources and inputs. To reach this 
objective in a rational and effective way we 
need, as illustrated, in part, by the organization 
and conduct of this Biennial Growth Sympo­
sium, to explore and understand fully the 
biology of animal growth. This challenge en­
compasses a broad range of biological inquiry; 
at one end, we need, for example, to unravel 
the details about the organization of DNA at 
the gene level and about the mechanisms 
responsible for differentiation. However, there 
is little argument that we must also extend our 
explorations through to the biologically more 
complex level and to problems of organ and 
tissue growth and the regulatory mechanisms 
that control the dimensions to which they grow 
(e.g., see Goss, 1986 for a review); we must 
likewise study the quantitative impact of exo­
genous, as well as endogenous, factors that 
might serve to modulate not only the growth of 
individual organs and tissues, but also the size 
of the body as a whole. 
The fundamental importance of the genetic 
make-up of the organism as a determinant of 
the growth and the body composition charac­
teristics of the animal has, of course, long been 
recognized. Indeed, this knowledge has been 
exploited through implementation of breeding

with the development of approaches for chro­

technology, we are, perhaps, on the verge of 
having the potential to manipulate the animal's 
genetic composition and its expression by 
totally new methods. 
Furthermore, we are now discovering fur­

of gene transcription, RNA splicing and trans-

2) identifying the participation of peptide

normal and abnormal cell growth (e.g., Rudland

more about the mechanism of action of the

Rasmussen, 1986), the products of the turn­

as inositol triphosphate and diacyglycerol 
(e.g., Cohen, 1985) and the eicosanoids (Reeds 
and Palmer, 1986). All this exciting new knowl­
edge promises to expand considerably our 
understanding of the biology of cell and organ 
growth. However, to exploit this research effec­
tively and more rapidly, we do need to give 
equal attention to the biochemical and physio­
logical aspects of animal growth. Indeed, it is 
equally important, as pointed out earlier by 
Porteous (1983) and Waterlow (1985), that the 
quantitative movement of molecules through 
the metabolic pathways, or fluxes, the sensi­
tivity of fluxes to changes of different kinds, 
the ways in which the various pathways interact 
and the in vivo factors responsible for the flow

Because of my limited expertise as a nutri­
tional biochemist, with a particular interest in 
the area of the physiology of protein and amino 
acid metabolism in the intact organism, espe­
cially in humans, I have decided to consider 
some selected examples of our own research 
findings in order to raise various points that I 
believe might be useful as we continue to 
attempt to apply new knowledge in the funda­
mental areas of biology to the practical prob­

remarks might encourage an expanded and 
closer collaboration between those concerned 
with the disciplines that include the more basic, 
molecular biological interests, on the one hand, 
and those scientists in the more applied areas of 
biochemistry and physiology, as well as in ani­
mal production, on the other. It seems to me 
that such a collaboration is now, more than 
ever, highly desirable. 
How Much and What Form of Enhanced 
Growth Can  Be Achieved? I begin by returning 
for a moment to the genetic level and to the 
work of Hammer et al. (1984). These investi­
gators injected the metallothionein-rat growth 
hormone fusion gene (MT-rGH) into eggs ob­
tained from a strain of mice,  little,  and accom­
plished a considerably higher rate of growth in 
the transgenic mice, relative to the characteris­
tic dwarfism of the mutant strain. As summa­
rized in table 1, body weight of the transgenic 
mice at 16 wk was approximately threefold to 
fourfold that of the mutant mice, who have a 
defect in GH mRNA production, and trans­
genic mice surpassed the weight of normal 
littermates by 30 d. Because the growth hor­
mone introduced in this way was synthesized 
by many organs, such as the liver, and achieved 
very high concentrations in circulating plasma, 
these transgenic mice might be considered 
"abnormal"; in any event, the regulation of the 
transferred gene was not under usual control. 
Possibly, of course, vectors can be designed for 
transferring genes, with all the genomic regula­
tory signals necessary to produce controlled 
expression of genes in specific target cells. 
However, subsequent studies by this group of 
investigators involved establishment of trans­
genic mice containing a fusion gene including 
the promoter/regulatory region of the mouse 
metallothionien I (MT-1) gene and the coding 
region of the human growth hormone-release 
factor gene (Hammer et al., 1985; table 2). 
Under these conditions increased growth was 
again achieved, with raised circulating concen­
trations of mouse growth hormone and the 
presence of human GRF, but the weight re­
sponse was less than in transgenic mice ex­
pressing rGH (Hammer et al., 1984). This might 
suggest that growth hormone may indeed be 
rate-limiting under conditions of normal 
growth, although this is difficult to judge 
because, e.g., we do not yet have a clear picture 
of the physiological significance, in functional 
terms, of a given plasma hormone concentra­
tion. Integration of the fusion gene (MT-hGH) 
into the pronuclei or nuclei of eggs from super-
ovulated rabbits, sheep and pigs has also been 
accomplished (Hammer et al., 1985b), but the

These examples of gene transfer are exciting, 
and there are others (e.g., see Palmiter and 
Brinster, 1985; Anderson, 1984). These are 
significant new findings, but they raise some 
fundamental physiological issues that should be 
explored. First, on the basis of the excessive 
growth responses observed in these transgenic 
mice, we should learn more about the conse­
quences of these manipulations on relative 
organ weights and body composition. This 
would improve our understanding of the 
genetic factors that may be responsible for the 
partitioning of nutrients among the body 
organs and tissues, especially those associated 
with deposition of substrate as protein rather 
than as adipose tissue. We must attempt to link 
gene to organism; there is a challenge, then, for 
an examination of the consequences of these 
forms of gene transfer on such basic, or classi­
cal, measurements as nutrient and energy intake 
and retention, basal metabolic rate and the 
partitioning of metabolizable energy for gain of 
fat as compared to lean. The studies by Calvert 
and coworkers (1984, 1985) and Bernier et al. 
(1986), who have explored the effects of an 
autosomal recessive gene (hg) for high growth 
on the chemical composition and energetics of 
growth, offer examples along these lines. Simi­
larly, in relation to the investigations of Calvert 
and his collaborators, it would now be worth­
while to attempt to identify the molecular 
process that is responsible for the altered 
energetic efficiency in their hg/hg genotype, as 
has been pointed out (Bernier et al., 1986). 
Only through additional metabolic and physio­
logical studies will it become clear where it 
might be most productive to probe for the 
molecular explanations and mechanisms that 
are causally responsible for altered growth 
characteristics; measurements of tissue or 
plasma concentrations of metabolites or hor­
mones will not necessarily provide a sufficient 
initial step. 
A Chronic Alteration in Exposure to  Hor­
monal Factors.  From the above observations 
we are lead to another intriguing physiological 
problem: the exposure of tissues to sustained 
higher concentrations of factors, such as hor­
mones, that have a potential for promoting cell 
growth and nutrient retention. After all, each 
gene of an organism has not evolved in isola­
tion, but in the context of other genes with 
which it has had to interact continuously. 
From allometric scaling we now appreciate 
that the "normal" size of an organ can be 
reasonably predicted as a given fraction of the 
size of the total body, although Smith (1984) 
has cautioned us against general adoption or, at 
least, uncritical application of an allometric 
method to better understand organ growth and 
size. Nevertheless, organ sizes generally appear 
to be adjusted to the functions that they are 
required to carry out (Goss, 1986), and thus, 
regulatory mechanisms have been built into 
cells during their evolution in order to allow 
them to best maintain their function in the face 
of changing environmental conditions. Per­
sistent exposure of cells to high concentrations 
of hormones might be expected, therefore, to 
trigger mechanisms that alter cellular responses 
and, in the context of animal growth and pro­
duction, this could conceivably lead to less 
favorable changes in nutrient uptake and 
utilization, despite a possible net increase in

Although I am aware of some of the pub­
lished studies of farm animals that have re­
ported increases in weight gain, favorable 
changes in carcass composition and in milk out­
put in response to chronic administration of 
growth hormone (e.g., Bauman et al., 1982; 
Eisemann et al., 1986) and )3-adrenergic agonists 
(e.g., Ricks et al., 1984; Jones et al, 1985), we 
should be particularly attentive to this issue of 
changes in hormone balance and concentration. 
Certainly it is a problem faced by clinicians 
during the long-term hormonal therapy of 
human patients, and various biochemical 
studies have revealed that persistent stimulation 
of target cells by various hormones can decrease 
subsequent cellular responses to the hormone. 
For example, catecholamines induce homolo­
gous hormone resistance via a functional un­
coupling of receptors from adenylate cyclase 
that can either proceed (Su et al., 1980; Horn-
burger et al., 1980) or occur concomitantly 
(Mukherjee et al., 1975; Stadel et al., 1983) 
with a decrease in the concentration of |3-adre-
nergic receptors (for review see Sibley and 
Lefkowitz, 1985). Also, as a further example, 
chronic exposure to insulin induces receptor 
and postreceptor defects in vitro for a variety 
of insulin actions (e.g., Chang and Polakis, 
1978; Amatruda et al., 1982). Recently, Garvey 
et al. (1986) found in primary cultured adipo­
cytes that exposure to high insulin induces 
progressive resistance to the hormone, with 
sequential effects at multiple sites, including 
decreased coupling efficiency between occupied 
receptors and stimulated glucose transport, 
receptor down-regulation and, as shown in table 
3, decreased insulin responsiveness of the 
glucose transport system. 
These time-dependent responses of tissues 
to hormones are reminiscent of the negative 
growth effects on skeletal muscle to chronic 
glucocorticoid administration that we observed 
a number of years ago (Tomas et al., 1979). 
Thus, I will discuss and expand briefly these 
earlier findings, especially because they help 
me to introduce additional questions furthet 
emphasizing a need to study physiology and 
metabolism in concert with the molecular 
biology of cell and tissue growth.

there is general agreement that glucocorticoids 
depress protein synthesis in muscle, it has been 
less clear whether they affect protein break­

The studies that have shown stimulation of 
muscle proteolysis have relied primarily on the 
measurement of urinary excretion of 3-methyl­

index of muscle protein breakdown. 3-MH is an 
amino acid formed by posttranslational modi­

myosin and is released into tissue free amino 
acid pools during protein breakdown. It cannot 
be reincorporated into protein, and it is quanti­

the myofibrillar proteins are the only source of 
3-MH, the presence of this amino acid in urine 
reflects myofibrillar protein degradation. De­
spite the fact that muscle contains the largest 
reserve of protein-bound 3-MH (Haverberg et 
al., 1975), there is an uncertainty about the

such as the skin and the gastrointestinal mucosa

Interactions Among Hormones In Vivo. The 
time-dependent response of muscle myofibrillar 
protein breakdown may, of course, be due to 
indirect effects of the administration of gluco­
corticoids on the endocrine system. If so, this 
strengthens the argument that we must con­
tinuously be concerned with integration of cell 
and organ metabolism within the intact orga­
nism. For example, the elevations of insulin 
concentrations in rats treated with gluco­
corticoids (Tomas et al., 1979) might affect the 
response of muscle to the steroid. In fact, myo­
fibrillar proteolysis, as reflected by urinary 
3-MH excretion, was found to be more elevated 
in diabetic rats treated with corticosterone 
(Tomas, 1982; Tomas et al., 1984), suggesting 
that insulin may modulate the proteolytic con­
sequences of continued corticosterone exposure 
by muscles. To further complicate the picture, 
Grunfeld et al. (1981) have demonstrated that a 
glucocorticoid-induced insulin resistance in glu­
cose metabolism develops within a time period 
of 3 to 4 d. Although a resistance has not been 
demonstrated to occur in skeletal muscle 
(Munck, 1971), the responsiveness of proteoly­
sis to insulin may be regulated independently of 
glucose uptake and metabolism. 
Our recent studies have also shown that the 
attenuation of muscle myofibrillar protein 
breakdown to chronic glucocorticoids occurs in 
diabetic rats (A. Kayali, M. Goodman and V. R. 
Young, unpublished data). It seems, therefore, 
that the qualitative nature of the response of 
muscle proteolysis to glucocorticoids is not 
dependent on changes in insulin balance. How­
ever, our observations do suggest that insulin 
helps minimize the catabolic effects of chronic 
corticosterone administration. Furthermore, 
our findings show that insulin plays an im­
portant role in modulating the effects of 
glucocorticoids on nonmyofibrillar protein 
breakdown, because in the absence of insulin 
these steroids promote total protein break­
down. Thus, the importance of in vivo inter­
actions should be clear and must be examined 
in the intact host.

1986; table 4), although the temporal change in

Quantitative Effect  of  Hormones  in  Vivo. 
In addition  to the  complexity  of  interactions 
among hormones  in  vivo, there  is  also relatively 
little information  on the  quantitative relation­
ship between changes  in  hormonal balance  and 
availability, tissue metabolism  and  growth  re­
sponses. For  example, in  spite of the  consider­
able evidence indicating  a  protein anabolic 
function of  insulin,  it is  only recently that 
studies have reported  the  quantitative effects  of 
changes in  insulin concentrations  on  various 
aspects of  amino acids  and  metabolism  in the 
intact otganism. Specifically,  we  have explored 
the acute effects  of  changes  in  circulating 
insulin concentrations  on  blood amino acid 
concentrations and  leucine and  alanine kinetics 
in man  (Fukagawa  et al., 1985,  1986). First, 
our findings extend earlier observations  on the 
influence of  insulin  on  plasma amino acid con­
centrations (Marchesini  et al, 1983;  Schauder 
et al., 1983;  Forlani et al.,  1984);  in addition 
they indicate that insulin decreases plasma 
leucine flux  in a  dose-dependent manner,  due 
to a  reduction  in  whole-body protein break­
down. Second,  the  important relationship  be­
tween insulin  and  leucine metabolism  is  well 
established. In man, for  example, insulin acce­
lerates the  disposal  of an  exogenous load  of 
leucine (e.g., Eriksson  et al., 1983) and  hyper-
insulinemia inhibits  the  release of  leucine from 
muscle (Pozefsky  et al.,  1969). In  vitro studies 
have demonstrated that insulin enhances  the 
uptake of  leucine  by  muscle (Manchester, 
1970), adipose tissue (Goodman,  1964) and 
liver and kidney (Dawson  et al.,  1967), and that 
insulin promotes muscle  and  liver protein syn­
thesis (Fulks etal., 1975; Jefferson, 1980).  Our 
findings, however, suggest  a  more important 
role of  insulin  in  reducing protein breakdown 
than that  of  stimulating protein synthesis.  It is 
reasonable to  question whether insulin indeed 
exerts its  major action  on net  protein gain  in 
vivo via  protein synthesis  or via  breakdown or 
both.

makes collagen synthesis so tightly coupled to

this regard? An answer to this question would 
be important if the new knowledge of the 
molecular biology of collagen metabolism is to 
be fully exploited for manipulation of growth

Another comment concerning protein turn­
over to be made here is whether the high rates 
of protein degradation, that usually parallel 
high rates of protein synthesis during rapid 
growth, can be reduced without affecting pro­
tein synthesis rates. If they could, then net pro­
tein gain would be enhanced. Also, high rates of 
protein breakdown are associated with high 
requirements, that is, increased losses of amino 
acids. The high rates of protein turnover in the 
rapidly growing organism presumably are of 
functional significance and are related, in part, 
to the needs for extensive tissue remodelling. 
Thus, it is desirable to achieve a reduction in 
protein turnover as a basis for improving the 
efficiency of growth? A problem we face is that 
we do not yet understand the significance for 
growth or function of a given rate of protein 
tunover, at least within a relatively broad range 
of turnover. 
Studies by Vernon and Buttery (1976, 1978) 
suggest that, in trenbolone-acetate-treated rats, 
increased growth and feed efficiency might be 
achieved under conditions of reduced rates of 
skeletal muscle protein synthesis and degrada­
tion. This seems to be a rather unique case; we 
need to know much more about the relation­
ship between body protein status in the intact 
host and protein turnover before we can judge 
whether it is desirable to reduce protein turn­
over as a means of manipulating animal growth. 
A recent study conducted in our laboratories 
in human subjects might be instructive in this 
context. 
In this investigation we (Young et al., 1987) 
explored, in young men, the effects of varying 
test intakes of leucine that were either low, 
within the submaintenance level or close to 
physiological requirements, for a 3-wk period. 
These test intakes followed a brief control 
period at an adequate or generous leucine 
intake. We then assessed the dynamics of leu­
cine metabolism after 1 wk and at the end of 
this 3-wk period, using a continuous isotope 
infusion of l-13C-leucine (Matthews et al., 
1980). The subjects were then immediately 
given a generous level of leucine for a 1-wk 
repletion period, during which we measured 
body protein status, in part, by monitoring 
nitrogen balance. As summarized in figure 7, 
for the low and physiological requirement levels

the low leucine intake; it also declined between 
1 and 3 wk, in comparison with the response 
observed at the higher or physiological require­
ment levels of leucine intake. Of particular

that although the major flow of endogenous 
amino acids within the body pools is associated 
with protein synthesis and turnover, the quan­

is not necessarily driven immediately by their 
role as substrates for the biochemical machin­
ery responsible for polypeptide chain synthesis. 
Rather, the requirement may be determined by 
those metabolic processes responsible for amino 
acid oxidation. It follows, then, that it would 
be highly desirable to obtain a more detailed 
understanding of the regulation of amino acid 
catabolism in the various tissues and organs and 
in the body as a whole, under various condi­
tions of growth. Indeed, a more effective utili­
zation of feed inputs might well be achieved 
through manipulation of the oxidative proces­
ses of amino acid metabolism.

sufficient to meet the needs for optimal tissue 
function. As Christensen (1982) has pointed

these fit into the functioning of the intact host.

bridges between the biological disciplines, be­

effectively the contributions of our efforts to 
advances in animal production. I believe that 
there is a tendency these days by many of us,

deciding the distribution of funds for research, 
that it is the more basic and mechanistically

useful purpose in debating the point that 
research with a more cellular or molecular 
focus is better than that which might be cate­

"applied" area. To resolve the complex prob­

we must avoid the tendency to categorize 
research in these terms. Rather, we should 
judge the research on the basis of whether it is a 
critical exploration of a hypothesis and whether 
the results of the investigation will likely add 
significantly to the body of knowledge required
