production, and some rumen and plasma
characteristics. In Experiment 1, cows
consumed alfalfa silage ad libitum and
one of three barley-based concentrates
with either soybean meal (a rapidly ru­

ten meal (a slowly degraded protein
source), or an equal mixture of the two,
fed at the rate of .36 kg/kg of milk
produced. In Experiment 2, cows were
fed total mixed diets based upon alfalfa
silage, barley, and either soybean meal,
com gluten meal, or a mixture of soybean
meal and whey powder (a protein source
very rapidly degraded in the rumen). In
sacco incubation procedures were used to
estimate degradability of protein in all
diets. All diets exceeded Agricultural Re­

well as NRC recommendations for
degraded protein. However, one to three
of the six total diets, depending upon
assumed ruminal turnover rates, did not
meet NRC recommendations for un­
degraded protein. Production parameters,
including OM! as well as milk yield and
composition, were not influenced by diet
in either experiment. Results do not sup­
port NRC recommendations for ruminally
undegraded protein for midlactation dairy

(30% of daily total) and 1500 h (70%). Orts
were weighed between 1100 and 1200 h. The
amount of feed offered was adjusted daily to
provide approximately 5% orts.
Experiment 2. Twelve multiparous Holstein
cows in midlaetation were utilized. Cows were
tethered in stalls and had free access to water.
They were allowed free access to a mixed diet
(Table 4) of alfalfa silage (Table 3) and a
concentrate mixture (Table 2). The mixed diet
was offered and weighed back as described for
Experiment 1.

groups of three based upon pre-experiment

randomly to treatments. All cows were fed the

Analytical Procedures
Dry matter of all feed ingredients except
alfalfa silage and mixed diets was determined
at 10S·C. Other assays were as described by
Robinson and Burgess (5).
Samples of alfalfa silage and concentrates
from each period in Experiment 1 as well as
samples of mixed diets from period 2 of Ex­
periment 2 were used to estimate rumen N
degradability (3). In brief, samples ground to
pass a 3-mm screen were placed in Nitex bags
(B. and S. H. Thompson, Scarborough, ON,
Canada; 53-J.Ul1 pore size) and incubated on
three separate occasions in the rumen of a
nonlactating cow fed timothy hay. Bags were 9
x 17 cm and contained 4 g of sample. Residues
were determined after 0, 1, 3, 6, and 24 h of
rumen incubation. Residues were dried at 60·C,

ground with a mortar and pestle, and assayed
for DM and N by procedures described by
Robinson and Burgess (5). Residues were fitted
to a two-fraction exponential model described
by Robinson et al. (7), incorporating a third
soluble fraction.
Statistical Analysis
Experiment 1. All parameters were analyzed
as a split-plot design (10) as previously
described (5).
Experiment 2. All parameters were analyzed
by ANOVA procedures, as described by Steel
and Tome (10), with block, treatment, and
covariate as factors using the statistical package
SAS version 5.16 (9). When treatment effects
were significant (P < .05), differences among
means were determined with Tukey's procedure

Experiment 2
Composition of the mixed diets was similar
in terms of their concentrations of CP, fiber,
and energy (Table 4). Dry matter intakes of
cows in this experiment were somewhat lower
than in Experiment 1, reflecting the lower BW
of cows in this experiment (fable 8). Milk
yield and its protein and lactose proportions
were not influenced by diet. Cows fed whey
powder (WHP) had a higher milk fat propor­
tion and tended to have a higher milk fat yield
(P = .12).
Replacement of SBM with COM or WHP
had no effect on rumen pH. However rumen
ammonia N concentration was numerically
lower for both (Table 9) and is reflected by
reduced plasma urea N (P < .05), especially for
the cows given the diet containing WHP.

Protein available for maintenance, growth,
and lactation is derived from digestible protein
that escapes the forestomachs. Dairy cattle have
no known requirement for ruminally un­
degraded protein per se, because it supplements
net rumen microbial protein yield from the
rumen. Under almost all circumstances, net mi­
crobial protein yield is assumed to be insuffi­
cient to meet animal total metabolizable protein
requirements (1, 4). Thus, some supplementary
dietary protein that escapes degradation in the
rumen is necessary to allow lactating cattle to
express their full production potential. Studies
such as those reported here have limitations,
because digestible protein delivery to the intes­
tine was not measured. This makes interpreta­
tion of results in terms of why the various diets
were or were not able to meet the animals'
metabolizable protein needs speculatory. Al­
though true, this same limitation applies to the
fundamental approaches of both the NRC (4)
and ARC (1) in estimating the types of proteins
(i.e., degraded or undegraded) required in the
diet. Both councils estimate the protein require­
ments of the animal and the rumen microbial
protein escape separately. The difference be­
tween the two is allocated to the ruminally
undegraded fraction. The problem is that rumen
microbial protein escape, a value of obvious
importance in estimating undegraded protein
requirements, is estimated from energy intake.
Although broadly accurate over a wide range of
intake levels and diets, it is not an accurate
estimation of rumen microbial yield under spe­
cific conditions (8). Thus, one of the assump­
tions used to calculate the values presented in
Tables 7 and 10, that microbial protein escape
in the rumen would be the same for all diets
within each experiment because energy intake
was the same and rumen microbial N require­
ments were less than intake of ruminally
degradable protein, is unlikely to be true. This
is noticeable in Experiment 2, in which rumen
ammonia N and plasma urea N declined as very
rapidly ruminally degraded whey protein
replaced soybean meal protein. It seems likely
that this was associated with faster, more effi­
cient, or both, rumen microbial growth and
escape, resulting from the rapidly fermentable
energy in WHP, leading to lower supplemen­
tary (i.e., undegradable) protein requirements
and animal performance equal to the SBM diet,
in which undegraded dietary protein delivery
appears deficient. Another assumption used to
calculate the values presented in Tables 9 and
10, that ruminally degraded protein from vari­
ous sources had equal value to rumen micro­
organisms, ignores the probable influence of
the different peptides released during protein
degradation on the efficiency of rumen micro­
bial growth and escape (2).
Midlaetation dairy cows producing about 30
kg/d of milk were able to achieve their produc­
tive potential on diets that appeared to provide
