ABSTRACT  
Problem: The Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) contains a sunflower model based 
on CROPGRO. This model assumes some parameters values out of the crop species variability. Besides, this model 
has not been assessed for simulating grain yield and grain oil content in contrasting environments. 
Objective: The main goal of this study was to generate and test a revised CROPGRO-Sunflower model. In addition, 
we used the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower to quantify crop responses to environmental and management 
variables. 
Methods: Three sunflower models: a revised CROPGRO-Sunflower, the original CROPGRO-Sunflower and the 
OILCROP-SUN were calibrated and evaluated across contrasting environments. We compared the revised 
CROPGRO-Sunflower with the original CROPGRO-Sunflower and OILCROP-SUN in terms of ability to simulate 
crop development, growth, grain yield and grain oil content. Crop responses to soil depth, sowing date, and El 
Ni˜no-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) effects were quantified using the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower in combination 
with climatic records for 37 growing seasons to simulate yield in two contrasting environments of Argentina: 
Balcarce and Reconquista. 
Results: Crop growth, grain yield and grain oil content were better simulated by the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower 
than by OILCROP-SUN. Simulated yield had a root mean square error (RMSE) of 48 g m-2 with revised 
CROPGRO-Sunflower and of 119 g m-2 with OILCROP-SUN. Moreover, RMSE for simulated grain oil concen-
tration was 2% for revised CROPGRO-Sunflower and 11% for OILCROP-SUN. Deep soils and late sowing dates 
resulted in higher grain yield at Balcarce. Sowing date did not affect grain yield at Reconquista. An effect of the 
ENSO phases on sunflower grain yield was found. "La Ni˜na" phase was associated with the lowest grain yields at 
both sites. 
Conclusions: Modifications made to the original CROPGRO-Sunflower improved model performance. The revised 
CROPGRO-Sunflower model can be utilized to simulate crop phenology, growth, grain yield and grain oil con-
centration over a wide range of environmental conditions. 
Implications: This calibrated and evaluated crop simulation model will allow to advance in the quantification of 
yield gaps and to study the impact of other management practices on sunflower crop production.   
1. Introduction 
Sunflower is an annual crop grown in temperate environments and is 
the fourth largest source of vegetable oil in the world (USDA, 2022). 
Argentina is the fourth main sunflower producing country and the crop 
is sown all across it cropping area (Hall et al., 1992; Mercau et al., 2001; 
Chapman and de la Vega, 2002; USDA, 2022). Sunflower sowing date 
extends from July in the northern region to mid-December in the

southern region exposing the crop to different photoperiod, tempera-
ture, radiation and precipitation regimes (Casta˜no, 2018), making 
Argentina an excellent place to evaluate a crop model. 
Assessment of potential extra production (i.e., yield gap) for sun-
flower requires robust estimates of the grain yield. Crop simulation 
models are suitable tools to quantify yield gaps and to study the impact 
of different management practices on crop production (Boote et al., 
1996; Grassini et al., 2015; He et al., 2015). The Decision Support Sys-
tem for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) is a collection of programs 
that operate together using shared databases of climate, soil, manage-
ment practices and genotypes (Jones et al., 2003). Two distinct types of 
crop models can be delineated within DSSAT (i) the CROPGRO models 
and (ii) the CERES-style models, which share the same soil-water and 
nitrogen routines. Both types include a model to simulate sunflower, 
CROPGRO Sunflower (Tariq et al., 2018) and OILCROP-SUN (Villalobos 
et al., 1996), respectively. It is relevant to point out that other sunflower 
simulation models have been developed but are not available in DSSAT 
(Kiniry et al., 1992; Chapman et al., 1993; Pereyra-Irujo and Aguir-
rez´abal, 2007; Casadebaig et al., 2011). 
The CROPGRO is a generic model in the sense that there is one 
common FORTRAN code to predict the growth of a number of different 
grain legumes (Boote et al., 1998a, 1998b), Boote et al. (2002), Hart-
kamp et al. (2002a), (2002b)) as well as non-leguminous crops (Schol-
berg et al., 1997; Singh et al., 2016). However, the current 
CROPGRO-Sunflower parameterization, used to simulate this crop in 
particular environments (Tariq et al., 2018; Awais et al., 2020; Gurkan 
et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021), assumes some values out of the crop 
species variability. Some examples are: maximum specific leaf area 
(SLA) of 580 cm2 g–1, when the maximum sunflower reported SLA is 210 
cm2 g–1 (Trapani et al., 1988); maximum size of the leaf (SIZLF) is set at 
200 cm2 (soybean value), when for sunflower this varies between 1200 
and 1500 cm2 (Villalobos et al., 1996); and a parameterization of root 
growth that underestimates sunflower ability to use resources in deep 
soil horizons (Dardanelli et al., 1997; Andriani, 2003). It is therefore 
necessary to update CROPGRO-Sunflower parameters based on current 
understanding of sunflower ecophysiology; and evaluate the ability of 
CROPGRO-Sunflower to predict grain yield and grain oil content in 
contrasting environments. 
The OILCROP-SUN model for sunflower is a CERES-type model with 
important process modifications needed to simulate the sunflower crop 
(Villalobos et al., 1996). This model has a parameterization based on 
radiation use efficiency and to improve its performance a reparamete-
rization of the model based on photosynthesis and respiration as two 
separate processes is needed. This model simulates crop growth and 
yield (Grassini et al., 2009) but has a very simple and poorly functional 
form of predicting grain oil content, an essential component of sun-
flower yield, for which it generally gives poor predictions (Mercau and 
Hall, pers. comm., 2019). Furthermore, OILCROP-SUN is available in 
DSSAT 4.6, as an older DOS-based legacy model, currently not coupled 
with the new DSSAT software features (Singh et al., 2016; G. Hoo-
genboom, pers. comm., 2019) precluding its extensive use among re-
searchers and managers. 
As in other similar crop models, variations in process parametriza-
tion of CROPGRO- Sunflower could affect the simulated results and may 
lead to different results and decisions (Mercau et al., 2007; Monzon 
et al., 2012). Sunflower grain yield interannual variability in rainfed 
systems across Argentina are conditioned by soil, crop management, 
climatic variability and their interactions. In the southern sunflower 
region of Argentina soil depth has a strong effect on availability of water 
and grain yield (Sadras and Calvi˜no, 2001), while in the northern sun-
flower region, the availability of water in the soil prior to sowing has a 
strong impact on grain yield (Mercau, 2010). Sowing date is a man-
agement decision with a big effect on yield. When the optimum sowing 
date is delayed, sunflower yield is reduced (de la Vega and Hall, 2002a; 
de la Vega and Hall, 2002b). Sowing date modifies crop water avail-
ability and is used as a management practice to avoid water deficits that 
cause yield losses (Otegui and L´opez Pereira, 2004; Cerrudo et al., 2013; 
Rotili et al., 2019). El Ni˜no – Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is a climate 
phenomenon that occurs across the tropical Pacific Ocean characterized 
by variations in the temperature of the ocean surface and in the air 
surface pressure in the tropical western Pacific with impacts on rainfall 
patterns across the world. Agronomically important aspects across 
cropping regions of the world are related to ENSO (Podest´a et al., 2002; 
Monzon et al., 2012; Aramburu Merlos et al., 2015), but it impact has 
not been explored yet for sunflower cropping areas of Argentina. 
A rigorous evaluation and adjustment of the current DSSAT crop 
models for sunflower is missing. Here we calibrated and tested the 
revised CROPGRO-Sunflower and compared it with the original 
CROPGRO-Sunflower to assess the impact of the modifications made to 
the model. Then we compared revised CROPGRO-Sunflower with 
OILCROP-SUN in their capacities to simulate crop development, growth, 
grain yield and grain oil concentration. We used a detailed database 
including ad-hoc experiments across a wide range of environments in 
Argentina. Finally, with the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower, we quantified 
crop responses to environmental and management variables. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Weather and soil data to run models 
Daily maximum and minimum temperature, dew point, wind and 
precipitation data were derived from INTA (http://siga2.inta.gov.ar/) 
and SMN (National Weather Service; http://www.smn.gov.ar/) weather 
stations. Data from NASA-POWER (http://power.larc.nasa.gov/) were 
used as source of daily incident solar radiation. Evaluations of the NASA- 
POWER solar radiation data indicate good agreement with ground- 
measured solar radiation data for Argentina (Aramburu Merlos et al., 
2015). 
Functional soil properties required to run crop simulation models (e. 
g., water content at field capacity and wilting point) were derived from 
soil series descriptions of Ritchie and Crum (1989), according to the 
revisions made by Gijsman et al. (2002). 
2.2. Calibration and error evaluation of CROPGRO-Sunflower 
In this study, we opted to use the term error evaluation instead of 
validation, based on the definitions presented in Oreskes et al. (1994). 
2.2.1. Model calibration approach 
We addressed the calibration procedure for three sunflower model 
versions: i) a revised CROPGRO-Sunflower (this article), ii) the original 
CROPGRO-Sunflower (Tariq et al., 2018), and iii) the OILCROP-SUN 
(Villalobos et al., 1996). Three cultivars (SYN3970; DK3880; Paraiso 
68) were used for all the models. 
The genetic attributes of CROPGRO-Sunflower are contained in 3 
files, namely species (SUGRO047. SPE), cultivar (SUGRO047. CUL) and 
ecotype files (SUGRO047. ECO). The species file contains all parameters 
and parameterized relationships for the sensitivity of different processes 
(leaf appearance rate, rate of reproductive progress, photosynthesis, 
respiration, leaf area expansion, protein mobilization, seed number 
determination and seed growth rate) to temperature, chemical compo-
sition of plant organs and tissues, and many other parameters (Boote, 
2019). The parameters of the phenology and the yield definition strategy 
of each cultivar of sunflower are in the cultivar and ecotype files of 
CROPGRO-Sunflower. Both files (cultivar and ecotype) contain a set of 
parameters that allow the model to simulate the behavior of the different 
cultivars, maintaining a fixed set of parameters in the species file. 
In the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower parameters in (SUGRO047. 
CUL), (SUGRO047. SPE) and (SUGRO047. ECO) were adjusted using our 
dataset (Table 1) and literature data using a trial-error approach which 
allowed us to minimize errors in the prediction of observed data. First, 
values such as base, optimum and maximum temperature for some

2.3. Quantification of crop responses to soil depth, sowing date, soil water 
content at sowing and the effects of El Ni˜no-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) 
The revised CROPGRO-Sunflower was used in combination with 
climatic records from 1983–2019 to simulate rainfed grain yield in two 
contrasting environments representative of the extremes of the wide 
sunflower production regions in Argentina: Balcarce, in the southwest of 
Buenos Aires (37◦45’ S, 58◦18’ W; 130 m a.s.l.) and Reconquista, in 
northern Santa Fe (29◦08’ S, 59◦38’ W; 46 m a.s.l.). The Balcarce 
environment is classified as humid temperate without a dry season, with 
a mean annual temperature of 13.8◦C and mean annual precipitation of 
950 mm. Most common soils are typic petrocalcic argiudol soils, with 
soil depth limited by a caliche layer (Pazos and Mestelan, 2002). The 
Reconquista environment is classified as humid, with an average annual 
temperature of 20◦C and a mean annual precipitation of 1250 mm. 
Most common soils are typic argiudoll with no soil depth restrictions 
(Panigatti, 2010). 
In Balcarce a combination of two sowing dates (early, October 8, and 
late, November 25) and 6 soil depths (60, 80, 100, 120, 140 and 180 cm) 
were simulated. In all cases, the simulations began with the soil water 
content set at field capacity at the time of sowing. In Reconquista a 
combination of two sowing dates (early, August 10, and late, September 
15) and three different soil water contents at sowing (WP, 50% and 
100% of field capacity) were simulated. For all soil water contents, we 
assumed that the upper 20 cm of soil was at field capacity at sowing to 
ensure crop emergence. We used for both sites a plant density of 6 plants 
m-2. 
In order to assess the influence of the ENSO on crop performance, 
simulated years were classified in: Neutral, El Ni˜no, and La Ni˜na phases, 
based on the Oceanic Ni˜no Index of the Climate Prediction Center from 
NOAA’s National Weather Service (ERSST v.3b, www.cpc.ncep.noaa. 
gov). Simulated grain yield differences between the ENSO phases were 
evaluated using ANOVA and Tukey test procedures using the R statis-
tical software (RStudio Team, 2020). 
3. Results 
3.1. Impact on revised CROPGRO of the modifications associated with 
calibration and evaluation errors 
The revised and original models predicted accurately the time of the 
R5 and R9 stages (Table 2). The revised model decreased the RMSE of 
the LAI, intercepted PAR and root growth simulations for the calibration 
dataset in comparison with the original model. Regarding root growth it 
is important to note that the original model only simulated root growth 
up to 1.2 m depth (Fig. 2). The revised model also simulated ADM, grain 
yield and grain oil content with a lower RMSE than the original model 
for both the calibration and evaluation datasets (Table 2). 
3.1.1. Cardinal temperatures and phase durations for vegetative and 
reproductive development 
The species file of the CROPGRO-Sunflower model requires four 
points of cardinal temperatures, base temperature (Tb), first optimum 
(Topt1), second optimum (Topt2) and maximum (Tmax) to calculate 
crop development (Boote et al., 1998b). The cardinal temperatures for 
the calculation of thermal time in crop development of the model were 
those estimated by Villalobos and Ritchie (1992) i.e. 4, 28, 30 and 40◦C 
were used as Tb, Topt1, Topt2, and Tmax (Table S1). 
Sunflower has a juvenile phase, in which it is insensitive to

photoperiod (Marc and Palmer, 1978, 1981), and a phase of photope-
riodic sensitivity for the floral initiation process (Marc and Palmer, 
1981; Rawson and Hindmarsh, 1982). The duration of the juvenile 
phase varies between 250◦ and 450◦Cd according to Villalobos et al. 
(1996), this information was taken into account for the calibration of the 
V1-JU coefficient in the ecotype file (time required from first true leaf to 
end of juvenile phase) (Table S2). Time required for floral induction 
(JU-R0) was calibrated at 3 photothermal days according to Villalobos 
et al. (1996). since it refers to the minimum number of days for floral 
induction under optimal temperature and daylengths. We assumed that 
the cultivars has a long-day plant response, and maximum day length 
was set to 15 h (Andrade et al., 2000) for all cultivars (Table S3). 
Our first objective was to predict as accurately as possible the 
phenology of each sunflower cultivar, because if the phenology is not 
predicted correctly, this will simulate with error all growth variables. 
The model was calibrated to predict with the lowest possible error the 
emergence, starburst, anthesis and physiological maturity stages. 
Anthesis is the most important phenological stage in sunflower, since the 
critical period for yield determination is located around it (Connor and 
Sadras, 1992). That is why this stage should be simulated with the 
lowest possible error. Crop phenology was well simulated by both 
models, slightly better for anthesis than for physiological maturity 
(Fig. 1 and Table 3). 
3.1.2. Dynamics of leaf area index and PAR intercepted 
Leaf growth parameters in the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower species 
file were modified to improve canopy leaf area expansion. We modified 
specific leaf area (SLAREF) and size of the leaf (SIZREF) from 240 to 
210 cm2 g–1 and from 200 to 1000 cm2 respectively. Upper (SLAMAX) 
and lower (SLAMIN) limit of specific leaf area (against limiting low and 
saturating high solar radiation, respectively) were set at 280 and 
210 cm2 g–1, respectively, according to Trapani et al. (1988) and Villa-
lobos et al. (1996) (Table S4). 
Additionally, the ecotype file was modified. In revised CROPGRO- 
Sunflower phyllochron is 43◦Cd, from leaf 1 to leaf 6 and 23 ◦Cd, 
from leaf 7 and upward (Villalobos and Ritchie, 1992). As a thermal day 
(TD) is equal to a 24◦Cd (Topt1 = 28◦C and Tb = 4◦C), those values are 
equivalent to a rate of leaf appearance on the stem (TRIFL) of 
0.5–1.0 leaf per thermal day (TD), respectively. As CROPGRO assumes a 
constant value of TRIFL, it was set at 0.6 leaf per TD, based on the 
number of leaves observed in Exp. 1 (Table 1). The time from first flower 
to last leaf on stem (FL-VS) was set to terminate the leaf development on 
the stem at 19 photothermal days, in R5 stage (Schneiter and Miller, 
1981) (Table S2). 
Lastly, in the cultivar file the SLA of cultivar (SLAVR) was calibrated 
to 240 cm2 g–1 and size of the full leaf (SIZLF) was calibrated to 
1000 cm2 to achieve a good fit for the leaf area index. According to 
Villalobos et al. (1996) maximum size of the full leaf (SIZLF) can vary 
between 1200 and 1400 cm2 depending on the cultivar. End of leaf 
expansion after first flower (FL-LF) was set to 23 photothermal days, 
between R5 and R6 (Table S3). The LAI was more accurately simulated 
by revised CROPGRO-Sunflower than by OILCROP-SUN (Table 3), 
largely due to poor simulation of the last sowing date at Balcarce 
(Table 1 - Exp. 1; Fig. S1). After LAI calibration the radiation intercep-
tion also was more accurately simulated by revised 
CROPGRO-Sunflower than by OILCROP-SUN (Table 3; Fig. S2).

3.1.3. Root growth dynamics 
Modifications were introduced to the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower 
species file to mimic sunflower root growth in soils with no depth re-
striction (Dardanelli et al., 1997; Andriani, 2003). In the root parameters 
section the rate of advance of rooting front (YRTFAC) and the leaf 
number from which YRTFAC is applied (XRTFAC, Table S4), were 
modified to set the rate of advance of rooting front at 4.8 cm per thermal 
day (Tb= 4◦C) according to Villalobos et al. (1996). This estimate was 
based on measurements of advance of the water extraction front made 
by ´Alvarez del Toro (1987) and of rates of rooting depth advance made 
by Sadras et al. (1989). We also modified the RWUEP1 parameter, which 
is defined as the relationship between water available in the roots and 
atmospheric demand, setting it at 2.5 according to what was observed by 
Villalobos et al. (1996). Among others, the effect of this parameter on 
the simulations of leaf expansion is reduced when the ratio (water 
available in the roots/ atmospheric demand) falls below the RWUEP1 
value (Table S4). 
After the modifications, revised CROPGRO-Sunflower simulatesd 
root growth more accurately than OILCROP-SUN (Table 3). We high-
light the facts that OILCROP-SUN does not explore root growth to depths 
greater than 2 m and that the original CROPGRO-Sunflower only sim-
ulates root penetration up to 1.2 m (Fig. 2).

According to Villalobos et al. (1996) the R3 stage (Schneiter and 
Miller, 1981) occurs 180 ◦Cd (7.5 TD) before R5 stage. This information 
was considered for the calibration of the interval between starburst and 
R3 stage “FL-SH” in the cultivar file (Table S3). The parameter FL-SD 
(Time between starburst to anthesis) was calibrated to reflect the in-
terval between starburst and R5 stage (Schneiter and Miller, 1981). The 
interval between the R5 and physiological maturity stages (SD-PM), was 
adjusted based on the duration of the grain filling period for each 
cultivar. The parameter SDPDV, that in soybean refers to average seed 
number per pod under standard growing conditions, was modified in 
sunflower from 2 to 1, because sunflower only has one seed per achene. 
Time required for a cultivar to reach final achene number load under 
optimal conditions (PODUR) also was calibrated to reflect the most 
common duration between R5 and R6 reported for sunflower (Red de 
evaluaci´on de cultivares). After the modifications mentioned above, 
yield was very well simulated by the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower and 
improved with respect to OILCROP-SUN predictions (Fig. 3B, Table 3). 
The sunflower grain has an oil content between 42% and 56% and a 
protein content between 9% and 20% of the dry weight of the grain. The 
weight of the grain ranges between 35 and 70 mg (Passarella and Savin, 
2004). We therefore modified the fruit tissue composition parameters 
within the cultivar file to reflect these limits. We changed seed lipid 
(SDLIP) and seed protein (SDPRO) values based on observed data in Exp 
1 and Red-INTA-ASAGIR (Table 1) and published literature (Diovisalvi 
et al., 2018). SDLIP changed from 43-45% to 48–58% and SDPRO from 
14% to 18% (Table S3). After these modifications, the revised 
CROPGRO-Sunflower predictions for grain oil concentration improved 
with respect to OILCROP-SUN predictions (Fig. 3C and Table 3). 
3.1.6. Impact on grain yield of sowing dates and soil depths at Balcarce, 
Buenos Aires, Argentina 
Soil depth and sowing date affected sunflower grain yield (P < 0.01 
and P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A), but no interaction between these two factors 
was found (P = 0.43). Soil depth explained more of the grain yield 
variation than sowing date at Balcarce and in this context [sum of 
squares for soil depth= 24%, sum of squares for sowing date= 1.6% 
(percentage respect to the total sum of squares)]. Grain yield decreased 
from maximum soil depth (180 cm) to minimum depth (60 cm) by 56% 
at the early sowing date and by 49% at the late sowing date. Deep soils 
and late sowing date resulted in a higher grain yield. Lower variability in 
grain yield was also observed for the late sowing date compared to the 
early sowing date, for all soil depths except for 60 cm, where variability 
is higher at the late sowing date (Fig. 4A). It is important to note that this 
analysis would have given a different result by not incorporating the 
modifications in rooting depth dynamics (Figs. 2 and S4). 
The effect of the ENSO phases on grain yield was also significant 
(P < 0.01) (Fig. 4 B) and an interaction was found between ENSO phases 
and sowing date (P = 0.03), but no interaction was found between ENSO 
phases and soil depth (P = 0.70). In the early sowing date, the lowest 
grain yield was obtained in the La Ni˜na phase, which differed signifi-
cantly from the Neutral phase. For that sowing date, the grain yield of 
the La Ni˜na phase was on average 35% lower than the grain yield of the 
Neutral phase. In the late sowing date, the lowest grain yield was also 
obtained in the La Ni˜na phase. At this sowing date, the grain yield of the 
La Ni˜na phase was on average 6% and 30% lower than the grain yield of 
the El Ni˜no and Neutral phases (respectively) (Fig. 4 B).

3.1.7. Impact on grain yield of sowing dates and soil water content at 
sowing at Reconquista, Santa Fe, Argentina 
No effect of sowing date on grain yield was detected (P = 0.72), but 
the effect of soil water content at sowing on grain yield was evident 
(P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A). The grain yield at WP soil water content at sowing 
was the lowest compared against the 50 or 100% of FC soil water content 
at sowing. For both sowing dates, the grain yield when soil water con-
tent set at sowing at WP was 37.5% lower than the grain yield obtained 
with soil water content set at 100% FC at sowing. 
The effect of ENSO phases on grain yield was significant (P < 0.01) 
and an interaction between ENSO phases and soil water content at 
sowing was found (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). When soil water content was set 
at sowing at WP and 50% FC the lowest grain yield were obtained with 
La Ni˜na phase w, but when soil water content was set at 100% FC there 
were no differenes between ENSO phases (Tukey test, α= 0.05, Fig. 5 
B). 
4. Discussion 
Our extensive and heterogeneous dataset for the calibration and 
evaluation of sunflower crop models allowed us to explore a wide range 
of environmental conditions including: latitudes from 29◦8’ S to 38◦18’ 
S, photoperiods from 10.5 to almost 16 h, mean monthly temperatures 
from 12.5◦C to 27◦C and soils with available soil water holding ca-
pacity from 70 to 300 mm. 
The revised CROPGRO-Sunflower and OILCROP-SUN models accu-
rately predict anthesis (R5 stage) and physiological maturity (R9 stage) 
(Fig. 1). The revised CROPGRO-Sunflower can capture the main features 
of seasonal dynamics for crop LAI, intercepted PAR, root growth, and 
ADM (Figs. 2 and S1, S2 and S3). Previous studies focussed on estimating 
phenology (Tariq et al., 2018), and when estimating LAI they used 
values for rate of leaf appearance, of specific leaf area, and of leaf size 
that are outside the range of sunflower variability for these variables 
(Awais et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021). 
The revised CROPGRO-Sunflower outperformed the original model 
and OILCROP-SUN when estimating root growth. Original CROPGRO- 
Sunflower underestimates sunflower’s ability to explore deep horizons 
when the soil has no impedances and OILCROP-SUN underestimates 
sunflower’s ability to explore deep horizons (>2 m, Fig. 2). 
Revised CROPGRO-Sunflower simulated ADM with a low error, as a 
result of the modifications made in dynamics of leaf area index and PAR 
intercepted, photosynthesis parameters in cultivar file, vegetative par-
titioning parameters and root growth. Also, Zhang et al. (2021) accu-
rately estimated the ADM of sunflower but they used coefficients out of 
the species biological range. 
After modifications in the species and the cultivar file (see Section 
3.6), the revised CROPGRO-Sunflower outperformed the original 
CROPGRO-Sunflower and OILCROP-SUN when simulating grain yield 
and grain oil concentration. Grain yield was simulated with original 
CROPGRO Sunflower only by Awais et al. (2020), Gurkan et al. (2020) 
and Zhang et al. (2021) but they did not mention modifications similar 
to those made by us. Furthermore, these authors did not explore a wide 
range of environmental conditions. 
In Balcarce, sunflower. Grain yield decreased as soil depth decreased. 
A similar effect of soil depth on sunflower yield was described by Sadras 
and Calvi˜no (2001). Interestingly, late sowing dates appear as a man-
agement practice that can allow farmers to increase grain yield and to 
reduce yield variability in comparison to the usual early sowings

diseases (for example, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum in Buenos Aires southeast 
(Pereyra and Escande, 1994)) at late sowing dates in sunflower crops has 
been a limitation to adopt it. Simulated grain yield in Balcarce was 
lowest during La Ni˜na years; these results did not coincide with those of 
Podest´a et al. (2002), who observed that sunflower grain yield at the 
farm level did not show a significant response to ENSO phases. In La 
Ni˜na years (dry scenarios) it is likely that a lower disease pressure 
counterbalanced the negative effect of water shortages (Chapman and 
de la Vega, 2002; Quiroz, 2015). It should be emphasized that the 
CROPGRO-Sunflower model does not take into account the impacts of 
crop diseases. 
In Reconquista, no effect of sowing date on grain yield was detected. 
These results did not agree with what was observed by Mercau (2010) 
using the OILCROP-SUN, who reports that higher grain yield were ob-
tained at the early sowing date than at the late sowing date. However, 
the effect of water at sowing on sunflower grain yield that was found 
here coincides with that reported by Mercau (2010). Grain yield 
response to ENSO phases in Reconquista was mediated by soil water 
content at sowing. The grain yield response to ENSO phases was highest 
when soil water content at sowing was at WP. 
In the El Ni˜no phase, sunflower crops kept free from diseases also 
showed reduced yields when exposed to waterlogging in these latitudes 
(Grassini et al., 2007). Also, in the El Ni˜no phase the increase of rainfalls 
during November and December affects the grain filling period, espe-
cially in hybrids of long cycle life (Moschini et al., 2002). 
5. Conclusions 
Modifications made to the original CROPGRO-Sunflower improved 
model performance. Revised CROPGRO-Sunflower outperformed 
OILCROP-SUN especially when simulating grain yield and grain oil 
concentration. Revised CROPGRO-Sunflower can be used to simulate 
crop phenology, growth, grain yield and grain oil concentration over a 
wide range of environmental conditions.
