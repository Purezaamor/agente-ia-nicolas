ABSTRACT 
The relationship between postweaning DNA accumulation and body weight was calculated for a 
reference growing steer. Accumulation of DNA (hyperplasia) was calculated from patterns of 
protein accretion derived from literature data and protein-to-DNA ratios (Pro/DNA) measured in 
samples of muscles from chuck, round and plate, hides, intestines, rumens and livers of steers 
slaughtered at body weights ranging from 150 to 700 kg. Amounts of protein relative to DNA 
found in muscles from chuck, round and plate were Used to estimate Pro/DNA changes in carcass. 
Corresponding values from intestines, rumen and liver were used to estimate Pro/DNA patterns for 
viscera. This ratio increased in carcass until animals reached body weights of approximately 300 kg 
and leveled off thereafter. No cell enlargement was evident in viscera. Thus, postweaning protein 
accretion in carcass results from both cell enlargement (hypertrophy) and DNA accumulation 
(hyperplasia) until body weights of 300 kg are attained, subsequent growth appeared to be hyper- 
plastic in nature. In viscera, hyperplasia was the primary determinant of protein accretion. 
(Key Words: Steers, Growth, Body Weight, Proteins, DNA.) 
Introduction 
Modeling analyses of causality between 
animal performance and metabolism must be 
based on equations representing underlying 
mechanisms (Thornley and France, 1985). 
Baldwin and Black (1979) pointed out the need 
for development of mechanistic models of 
growth to improve the accuracy of management 
models and to be utilized in support of research 
programs. Their modeling analyses indicated 
that concepts accommodating DNA accumula- 
tion or hyperplastic growth, and protein per 
unit of DNA or hypertrophy (Allen et al., 
1979) are required to simulate tissue growth. 
Ohjen et al. (1986) utilized these concepts in 
an aggregated model of animal growth, which 
improved the accuracy of the National Research 
Council system for beef cattle. Their modeling 
analysis indicated a significant increase of DNA

Alien et al. (1979) but contrary to data on 
growing rodents (Enesco and Leblond, 1962) 
and cattle (Trenkle et al., 1978), indicating that 
later stages of growth were hypertrophic. This 
model does not always yield acceptable esti-

These observations led us to evaluate the 
relative importance of hyperplastic and hyper- 
trophic growth in steers to support development

This objective was achieved in several stages. 
The first required experimental evaluation of 
DNA accumulation and protein to DNA ratios 
during postweaning growth in steers, and 
quantitative definition of body weight, body 
components and hyperplastic and hypertrophic 
growth in a (typical) reference steer. 
Materials and Methods 
Sampling. Samples from different tissues 
and livers of steers and bulls were taken during 
the winter and spring of 1985 at the University 
of California Meat Laboratory, located in Davis.

DNA and Protein Analysis. For DNA analysis 
the samples were homogenized at 0 C in .2 N 
perchloric acid (PCA) using a Polytron homog- 
enizer 7. DNA was measured in homogeriates 
using the method described by Munro and 
Fleck (1966). Briefly, this involves removal of 
acid soluble molecules by washing with .2 N 
PCA; digestion of the precipitate with .3 N 
KOH for 1 h at 37 C to hydrolyze RNA; 
precipitation and washing of DNA with PCA; 
and, development of a color reaction with DNA 
by treatment with 6 N HC1 and 4% indole for 
12 rain at 100 C (Ceriotti, 1952). Amount of 
DNA was estimated from absorption at 490 nm 
using calf thymus DNA 8 as standard. Protein 
was estimated as Kjeldahl N times 6.25. 
Protein and Fat Accretion in a Typical 
Growing Steer. To calculate accumulation of 
DNA, protein and fat with body weight or age, 
a typical (reference) steer composed of three 
body components, namely: body (B), viscera 
(V) and fat (F), was considered. Empty body 
weight was the sum of protein (in body and 
viscera) and fat, plus water associated with 
protein. This representation accommodates the 
minimum number of body components required 
for achievement of the long-term modeling 
objective stated earlier. Body was considered to 
be comprised of hide, skeleton, muscle, brain 
and some minor tissues; viscera included blood, 
liver, digestive tract, heart and lungs; fat was 
the sum of all adipose tissues in the body.

Statistical Methods. Regression equations 
were derived from published and experimental 
data using Minitab (Ryan et al., 1976). Pro/ 
DNA in muscles were regressed against carcass 
weight (n=18). The data were best fit by a 
third-degree polynomial equation. In livers, 
rumens and intestines, Pro/DNA did not vary 
with carcass weight, so data were expressed as 
mean and SE (n=12, except liver n=16). 
Results 
No cell enlargement during growth was 
found in liver (figure 1), rumen and intestines. 
ProlDNA were quite different among these 
protein pools, with rumen having the highest 
ratio, followed by liver and the intestines. Small 
and large intestine had similar concentrations of 
protein relative to DNA (table 2). 
In muscles and hides Pro/DNA increased 
with body weight. The three muscle samples 
studied had similar Pro/DNA patterns. Protein 
to DNA ratios increased until the animals 
reached carcass weights of 150 to 200 kg (270 
to 345 kg of body weight), and thereafter 
remained approximately constant (figure 2). 
However, estimates leveled off at different 
values. Maximum Pro/DNA were, approximate- 
ly, 450 in chuck, 900 in round and 1,300 in 
plate muscles (n=lS, SD: 53, 113 and 211, 
respectively). The minimum and maximum 
values found in hide did not differ significantly 
from those of round muscles (452, SE = 30 and 
768, SE = 62, n=12).

Discussion 
Since Moss (1968) postulated that a given 
cytoplasmic volume was controlled by a given 
nucleus, there have been several attempts to 
quantify the ratio of protein to DNA in cattle-- 
as in other species-under different nutritional 
and physiological conditions. However, variance 
among data reported in the literature precluded 
clear definition of this parameter. Furthermore, 
this ratio was affected by body weight (Trenkle 
et al., 1978), undernutrition (Robinson and 
Lambourne, 1970) and, in some but not all 
cases, by type of diet (Eversole et al., 1981). 
No effect of breed has been reported (Eversole 
et al, 1981). These data, taken together, sug- 
gest that the amount of protein controlled by 
each unit of DNA is variable and affected by 
many conditions, including weight, physio- 
logical state, nutritional state and type of tissue 
or structures within a tissue. 
In formulating aggregate Pro/DNA estimates 
for further modeling purposes, the following 
assumptions were made: 
1) A reference steer composed of three aggre- 
gate components, two of which contain DNA 
(body and viscera), is adequate for modeling 
studies compatible with our objectives. 
2) Total accretion of protein (body and 
viscera) follows the pattern described by the 
equation developed by Fox and and Black 
(1984; table 1). 
3) Weight of viscera (VW) is published by 
Jenkins et al. (1981) is typical. They reported 
pooled weights of blood, heart, liver, lungs, 
spleen, kidneys and empty gut from bulls of 
different weights. Also, amounts of protein in 
viscera (PV= VW  .16) can be estimated from 
average contents of protein in rumen, liver and 
intestines (table 2).

essentially constant over this range of body 
weights. In body, DNA remained relatively con- 
stant at 54 g when body weights were below 
220 kg. This implies that hypertrophy was the 
main determinant of protein accretion (figure 2) 
during this period. Thereafter, DNA and protein 
increased in parallel to a total DNA values of 
approximately 112 g when rates of both DNA 
and protein accretion leveled off (figure 3). 
According to these observations, the increase 
of mass in body probably occurred in three 
phases. Early postweaning growth was mainly 
hypertrophic because Pro/DNA increased more 
than DNA up to 200 kg of BW. Between 200 to 
350 kg of BW, growth was due to DNA accumu- 
lation and cell enlargement, indicating both 
hyperplasia and hypertrophy. At weights above 
350 kg, growth was hyperplastic, as indicated 
by accumulation of DNA and no further 
increases of Pro/DNA (figure 2). The slow 
accumulation of DNA during early growth was 
not expected, although it was also evident in 
data reported by Powei1 and Aberle (1975) for 
swine (figure 4). In this case, the weight of 
biceps muscle increased from 35 to 51 g without 
any change in DNA, which remained at 77 mg. 
The relative importance of hyperplasia and 
hypertrophy were also illustrated in this study, 
because as weight of muscle increased 151 
times, DNA increased 35 times and Pro/DNA 
only fourfold. This points out that the contri- 
bution of hyperplasia or increment in DNA to 
postnatal muscle growth is proportionally larger 
than hypertrophy or increases in Pro/DNA. 
Campion et al. (1981) reported that although 
the percentage of satellite cell nuclei in trans- 
verse sections of two muscles from pigs de- 
creased with age, myonuclei increased. Data of 
Tulloh and Gabbedy (presented by Black, 
1984) similarly indicate that in lambs ranging in 
body weight from 10 to 25 kg, Pro/DNA was 
relatively constant; hence, muscle growth was, 
largely, hyperplastic rather than hypertrophic. 
Also, in the data for cattle published by Trenkle 
et al. (1978), this trend is evident, although to a 
lesser extent (figure 4). In this case, as muscle 
increased fourfold in weight, DNA increased 
2.6-fold and Pro/DNA only 1.6 times. The 
differences may be explained because Powell 
and Aberle (1975) used pigs ranging in age from 
1 to 210 d, whereas Trenkle et al. (1978) used 
animals over a much narrower range of weights 
(110 kg to approximately 500 kg). Our results, 
subject to the assumptions stated earlier, 
indicated that as body protein increased sixfold, 
DNA increased twofold and Pro/DNA increased 
approximately twofold (figure 2). 
The data discussed suggest that hyperplastic 
growth or increases in DNA due to satellite cell 
proliferation and fusion with muscle cells is at 
least as important to postnatal muscle growth 
as cell enlargement. From the point of view of 
formulating mechanistic models of growth in 
cattle, it is important to note that during later 
stages of growth (above 350 kg of BW) hyper- 
plasia is dominant. This is in contrast to data 
for rodents in which hypertrophy is the pri- 
mary cause of muscle growth in later stages of 
growth (Enesco and Leblond, 1962). Our 
observations are in agreement with Allen et la. 
(1978), who concluded that DNA accumulation 
is muscle parallels protein accretion, and that 
the rapid phase of protein accretion corresponds 
with the greatest accumulation of DNA or 
myonuclei. 
Postnatal growth of viscera was the result of 
hyperplastic growth, in accordance with data 
reported in the literature.
