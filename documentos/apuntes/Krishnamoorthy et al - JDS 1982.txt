Nitrogen Fractions in Selected Feedstuffs 
U. KRISHNAMOORTHY, T. V. MUSCATO, C. J. SNIFFEN, and P. J. VAN SOEST 
Department of Animal Science 
Cornell University 
Ithaca, NY 14853 
ABSTRACT 
Nitrogen in selected feedstuffs 
was fractionated based on solubility 
in mineral solvents and detergent 
solutions. Six solvents with either a 
continuous mixing procedure at constant 
temperature or an intermittent mixing 
procedure at room temperature were 
compared for nitrogen solubility. Nitro- 
gen solubility varied with type of solvent. 
Borate-phosphate buffer was more stable 
than other solvents. Borate-phosphate 
buffer had a correlation coefficient 
of .92 with insoluble nitrogen obtained 
with autoclaved rumen fluid. Insoluble 
nitrogens obtained by continuous or 
intermittent mixing techniques were not 
significantly different. The latter pro- 
cedure is simple and avoids the need for 
special extraction equipment for estima- 
tion of nitrogen solubility. In most 
feedstuffs, a large fraction of soluble 
nitrogen was in the form of nonprotein 
nitrogen. Oats and peanut meal contained 
a significant amount of soluble true 
protein (43.3 and 24.3% of total nitro- 
gen). Neutral detergent insoluble nitrogen 
and acid detergent insoluble nitrogen 
content of feed samples varied among 
feedstuffs and were not a constant 
proportion of neutral detergent or acid 
detergent fiber. 
INTRODUCTION 
In spite of significant progress in under- 
standing the mechanisms of dietary nitrogen 
(N) utilization by ruminants, there are no 
suitable methods to evaluate dietary N in terms 
of meeting animal requirements. Solubility of 
Received December 8, 1980. 
dietary N has been considered to be a factor 
influencing protein degradation in the rumen 
(1, 5, 14, 21, 22, 25). Attempts to correlate 
solubility of dietary N with degradation in 
viable rumen fluid resulted in an inconsistent 
relationship between N solubility and rate of 
ammonia release (4, 10). 
Solubility of proteins need not be related 
necessarily to its susceptibility to enzymatic 
degradation (13). Therefore, use of N solubility 
as an estimate of rumen N degradability has 
shortcomings even though correlation between 
protein solubility in mineral buffer and extent 
of digestion in the rumen has been high (8). 
Techniques for measurement of actual degrada- 
tion of feed proteins by rumen microbes (3, 12) 
are fairly complex. A simple laboratory system 
that would reflect indirectly the extent of N 
that is degraded rapidly in the tureen is essen- 
tial. 
Nitrogen solubility offers one such possibil- 
ity (8). The solvent systems proposed by Wohh 
et al. (25), Crooker et al. (4), and Waldo and 
Goering (24) have an unstable pH in a labora- 
tory environment. Pichard (15) proposed a 
simpler method of extraction of soluble nitro- 
gen (SN), but the solvent used had an unstable 
pH. 
The detergent system (7) for fiber analysis 
offers another possibility. Nitrogen insoluble in 
acid detergent solution can be considered as an 
estimate of indigestible N (6). There is pre/imi- 
nary evidence that there is a positive correlation 
between neutral detergent insoluble nitrogen 
(NDIN) and a slowly degradable pool of feed N 
estimated with bacterial protease (15) on forage 
samples. 
The objectives of this study were to 1) 
develop a simple, pH stable buffer and extrac- 
tion technique for estimation of insoluble 
nitrogen in feedstuffs and 2) investigate the 
variability of N fractions in feedstuffs which 
have been claimed by researchers to have some 
biological significance in ruminants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Comparison of Solvents 
Five feedstuffs (soybean meal, peanut meal, 
dried brewers grain, oats, and timothy) were 
evaluated. Six solvents were compared: bi- 
carbonate-phosphate buffer (BCP), McDougall's 
artificial saliva (MCD), Burrough's mineral 
mixture (BMM), .15 M NaCI (NaC1), borate- 
phosphate buffer (BP), and autoclaved rumen 
fluid (ARF). The composition of these solvents 
is in Table 1. The pH's of all solvents were 
adjusted to 6.8 by bubbling carbon dioxide or 
by adding sodium bicarbonate• Autoclaved 
rumen fluid was prepared as described by 
Crooker et al. (4) except that the rumen liquor 
was collected only once from a rumen fistu- 
lated cow fed on a timothy hay diet. 
Nitrogen Extraction Procedures 
Two procedures were examined: 
Modified Crooker et al. (4). A .5 to 1.0 g air 
dry sample was weighed into a 70-ml cylindrical 
tube fitted with a screw cap. Sixty-five milli- 
liters of BCP buffer were added, and the tubes 
were closed tightly and incubated in a metabolic 
shaker for 60 rain at 39vC. The shaker was 
rotated at 110 to 120 rotations per rain. After 
60 min, samples were removed from the water 
bath and filtered on Whatman No. 54 filter 
paper, and the residue washed with 250 to 300 
ml cold distilled water. The N content of the 
residue was estimated by macro-Kjeldahl (17). 
Picbard (15). A .5 g sample was weighed into 
a 100-ml beaker. Five milliliters 10% t-butyl 
alcohol (wetting agent) and 5 ml BCP were 
added. The sample was soaked at room temper- 
at-are with stirring every 10 min for 1 h and 
filtered on Whatman No. 54 filter paper• 
Residue was washed with 50 ml buffer and 250 
ml distilled water• Residual N was estimated 
by macro-Kjeldahl (17). 
Soluble N for both methods was calculated 
as the difference between total N and insoluble 
insoluble N.

Soluble True Proteins 
Soluble N was extracted with BCP buffer. A 
suitable aliquot was taken, and protein was 
precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TEA, 
final concentration 10%) (2). Precipitated 
protein was estimated by Lowry analysis (11) 
with serum albumin as standard. 
In the above feed samples, neutral and acid 
detergent fiber were estimated according to 
Goering and Van Soest (7), with filter paper 
substituted for crucibles as proposed by 
Robertson et al. (20). Nitrogen in the residue 
was estimated by macro-Kjeldahl (17). The 
NDF in concentrate ingredients and corn silage 
was estimated by the amylase modification as 
proposed by Robertson and Van Soest (19). 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Comparison of Solvents 
Quantities of insoluble N remaining unex- 
tracted from various feedstuffs by BCP, MCD, 
BMM, NaC1, BP, and ARF are compared in 
Table 2. Insolubility of N varied among feed- 
stuffs and among solvents. Differences in 
insoluble N obtained with different solvents 
could be due to differences in chemical composi- 
tion of solvents (Table 1) and also type of nitro- 
genous material being solubilized. These data 
along with other reports (4, 10, 24, 25) confirm 
that the amount of nitrogen extracted from a 
feedstuff can vary over a wide range depending 
on the solvent and type of feed. Under these 
circumstances, it might seem reasonable to use 
ARF as a preferred solvent (24). However, it 
has the disadvantage of variable composition, 
laborious collection and processing, as well as 
difficult filtration. The NaC1 and BMM are not 
desirable because NaC1 has no buffering capac- 
ity and BMM has a short shelf life. Even though 
BCP is simple to prepare, it has the disadvan- 
tage of unstable pH. Fluctuation in pH of 
solvents can affect protein solubility in feed- 
stuffs to a considerable extent (24). Therefore, 
a solvent with a stable pH is essential. Because 
BP maintains a stable pH over prolonged 
storage, this buffer appears promising.

Crooker et al. (4) was modified as explained 
earlier. These modifications simplified the 
procedure by avoiding special extraction 
apparatus. However, modified method required 
a water bath with reciprocal shaker, and during 
incubation there was a problem of leakage of 
samples from breaking of screw caps. This prob- 
lem was prevented by rubber caps. Insoluble N 
thus obtained was compared with that obtained 
by extraction proposed by Pichard (15) (Table 
3). Analysis of data by t-test revealed that the 
mean insolubility obtained by two methods was 
not statistically different. In the extraction 
procedure proposed by Pichard (I5), there was 
a problem of particles sticking to sides of the 
beaker because of the small quantity of solvent 
added (10 ml) and frequent stirring with a glass 
rod. Such stirring does not seem to be necessary 
because with modified Crooker method, 
shaking the incubated samples continuously at 
110 to 120 rotations per minute did not 
increase N solubilization compared with 
Pichard's procedure wherein the samples were 
stirred with a glass rod only six times 
during the incubation. Therefore, Pichard's 
procedure was modified slightly by adding 25 
ml solvent to the samples instead of 5 rnl. 
Samples were stirred thoroughly with a glass 
rod with an initial 25 ml of solvent, and the 
remaining 25 ml of solvent was added. Samples 
were allowed to stand for 1 h and stirred again 
thoroughly just before filtration. By this 
modification, the extraction procedure pro- 
posed by Pichard works more smoothly and 
avoids the need for water bath and automatic 
stirrers. 
The extraction device in this experiment is 
entirely different from that used by Crooker et 
al. (4). Stirrers used by Crooker's group have 
fitted blades which may facilitate nitrogen 
solubilization. Because the significance of 
solubilization with these solvents in relation to 
total rumen degradation is debatable (8, 13), 
such a marginal increase in N solubilization 
from stirring may not be of practical signifi- 
cance.

Fractions of N Based on Solubility 
in Detergent Solutions 
Neutral detergent insoluble nitrogen (NDIN) 
includes N associated with cell wall that is 
insoluble in neutral detergent solution. Acid 
detergent insoluble nitrogen (ADIN) is that 
fraction which is insoluble in acid detergent 
solution. The ADIN includes lignified nitrogen 
and Maillard products and is largely unavailable 
to the animal (6). Acid detergent insolu- 
ble nitrogen has been suggested as a sensitive 
assay for nonenzymatic browning from heating 
of feeds (7). There was a considerable variation 
in NDIN and ADIN among the feed samples 
(Table 6). 
The biological significance of NDIN has not 
been demonstrated clearly. Chemical analysis 
indicates that the N content of this fraction is 
variable across samples. In corn gluten meal 
and soybean meal ADIN exceeded NDIN 
(Table 6). This may be because of precipitation 
of some neutral detergent soluble components 
by acid detergent solution, and vice versa. 
These interfering substances could be Maillard 
products of small molecular weight which are 
soluble at neutral pH but insoluble at acidic pH.

Pichard and Van Soest (16) classified feed N 
into three pools based on its rate of solubiliza- 
tion with a protease enzyme from Streptomyces 
griseus. Pichard (15) reported a positive correlao 
uon between the slowly solubilizable pool of N 
and NDIN in forage samples. Plant cell walls 
contain glycoprotein (extensin) as one of the 
constituents attached covalently to cellulose 
fibrils (9). Because cell wall N is a structural 
component, it is likely to be more protected 
from enzymatic degradation. However, the pos- 
sibility of using NDIN as an index of a slowly de- 
gradable N pool still needs further investigation.

CONCLUSION 
Nitrogen solubility can vary with type of 
solvent and feedstuff. Borate-phosphate buffer 
may be the preferred solvent because of its 
stability on storage, and Pichard's method is 
preferred because of its simplicity. However, 
the meaning of any estimate so obtained in 
relation to rumen degradability is not known. 
Until a reliable practical technique for 
estimation of degradable N is available, buffer 
soluble N and soluble TP could be used to 
evaluate dietary N in ruminant feedstuffs. 
Nitrogen soluble in neutral detergent solu- 
tion is of interest. Over 30% of total N in 
forages and fermented grains Was insoluble in 
neutral detergent. The nature of this protein 
and its susceptibility to enzymatic degradation 
need to be studied to understand its biological 
significance.
