Improving fertility in beef cow recipients
C.R. Looney a,*, J.S. Nelson b, H.J. Schneider a, D.W. Forrest b
a Ovagenix LP, P.O. Box 3038, Bryan, TX 77805, USA
b Department of Animal Science, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77845, USA
Abstract
In the 1970s, bovine embryo transfer (ET) shifted from research in a laboratory environment to
commercialization of this technology for beef producers. With the quarantine requirements and
expense of importing Continental breeds of cattle from Europe, embryo transfer became the logical
means to reproduce greater numbers of these animals at a lower cost. The ET industry grew very
rapidly and soon would become what it is today, a common practice utilized by select ranchers and
breeders. Research over the years has primarily focused on methods to increase the number of
ovulations and fertilized ova from the donor female, but the total number of transferable embryos has
not changed markedly in the last 20 years. More recent advances have been in the area of in vitro
production of embryos that allow for greater numbers of embryos to be produced and easier
accessibility to incorporate technologies such as sexed sperm, sperm injection, or transgenics. This
paper will focus on the second part of the equation, the recipient, and decisions that will enable both
the customers and practitioners to most efﬁciently utilize embryos from superovulation, in vitro
production, or nuclear transfer, so that the maximum number of pregnancies can be produced.

upkeep of recipients. Recipient females that become pregnant after ﬁrst transfers are

Proper nutritional management and post-partum intervals are basic, but important factors
that are the foundation for ET to be successful. Once these basic requirements are met or
exceeded, there are multiple other components of the procedure that can have positive
or negative effects on the transfer of embryos. Synchrony of donors and recipients is a vital
element of ET that cannot be overlooked. Recent developments in synchronization
protocols using progesterone supplementation with estradiol and prostaglandin (PGF)
enable more efﬁcient use of recipients due to tighter synchrony of ovulations and the ability
to transfer embryos without estrus detection [1,2]. Several studies have evaluated the
effects of supplementation of recipients with various pharmaceutical agents, hormones, or
nutritional additives. Finally, the ability and competence of the technical staff is as
important as any of the previous factors. All of these processes that begin with identifying
recipients to ﬁnally achieving pregnancy and parturition are steps that must be performed
as correctly and accurately as possible to ensure success with ET. This paper will address
the management, embryo, recipient, and transfer factors that may alter pregnancy rates.
2. Management factors
Two management factors that strongly inﬂuence the success rate of an embryo transfer
program are post-partum interval and nutrition. Cows entering our synchronization
program must have a post-partum interval of at least 50 days, and the ﬁrst-calf heifer must
have an interval from calving that is greater than 50 days before synchronization.
Post-partum intervals in beef cattle are inﬂuenced by many factors. Cattle that experience
no problems at parturition and are maintained on an adequate nutritional plane should
usually be cycling 50 days post-partum or shortly thereafter [3]. Nutrition is a key factor in
all aspects of reproduction and is especially critical for recipient females. Energy, protein,
vitamins, minerals, and water, and the balance among these elements are important. Body
condition score and energy are major factors regulating reproductive successes and/or
failures in recipients. Mapletoft et al. [4] reported that body condition inﬂuenced
pregnancy rates, whether embryos were transferred surgically or non-surgically. Pregnancy
rates in recipients with a body condition score (BCS) (scale 1–5; 1, extremely thin; 5,
extremely fat) of 3 (55%; n = 633) and 2 (53%; n = 460) were signiﬁcantly higher than for
recipients with a BCS of 1 or lower (44%; n = 230). Females with a BCS of 4 or higher
(47%; n = 175) had pregnancy rates that were intermediate. Lowman [5] reported that a
generalized target would be a BCS of 2.5 at calving and at least a 2 at time of transfer; those
not at the target condition scores should be fed to improve condition scores and be on a
positive energy balance. Superior results cannot be expected in either donors or recipients
if there is a deﬁciency in one or more of the required nutrients.
3. Embryo
The developmental stage and quality grade of the embryo at transfer has long been
known to inﬂuence the outcome of embryo transfer. Transfer of early morulae result in

signiﬁcantly lower pregnancy rates than transfer of more advanced stage embryos [6].
Embryos graded excellent or good result in higher pregnancy rates than transfer of lower
quality grade embryos [6–8]. Good or excellent embryos at the development stages of
compact morula to blastocyst produce the highest pregnancy rates with both fresh embryos
and after transfer of previously frozen embryos. Pregnancy rates did not differ among
embryo stages from late morula to the late blastocyst stage when using fresh transfer [6,8]
and the same is true for the transfer of frozen-thawed embryos [8]. Extensive studies have
shown that transfer of fresh embryos produces pregnancy rates that are 8.5–14 percentage
points higher than transfer of frozen-thawed embryos [8–13]. Transfer of in vitro-produced
and cloned embryos generally results in lower overall pregnancy rate when compared to in
vivo-produced embryos [14]. In vitro-produced embryos are also associated with more
rapid development of male embryos, a greater accumulation of intracellular lipid,
a decrease in surface microvilli, alterations in cytoplasmic mitochondrial density, and
incomplete compaction or less organized development of the inner cell mass [15]. These
factors and perhaps the addition of others could be responsible for compromised fertility of
in vitro-produced embryos compared to those produced in vivo.
Techniques to increase pregnancy rate by manipulation of embryos and use of
trophoblastic vesicles have been evaluated. Heyman et al. [16] performed co-transfer of a
frozen embryo and trophoblastic vesicle and believed that the addition of one or two
trophoblastic vesicles could increase pregnancy rate. Further studies are needed to conﬁrm
the potential beneﬁt of the trophoblastic vesicle co-transfer technique. Although zona
dissection and assisted hatching have been studied with human embryos, the limited
investigation of these techniques with bovine embryos has failed to demonstrate a
beneﬁcial response [17]. However, while the work by Palasz and Mapletoft [18] was not
designed to evaluate assisted hatching, the authors demonstrated that thawing frozen
embryos for a shorter interval in the air before thawing in a water bath increased pregnancy
rates. The higher pregnancy rate was attributed to an increased incidence of cracked zona
pellucidae which likely facilitates the hatching process.
4. Recipient
McMillan et al. [19] developed a model to separate the contribution of the embryo and
the recipient to embryonic survival through day 60 of gestation. The variation in a
recipient’s ability to carry a pregnancy to term rather than the ability of the embryo to
survive is the major source of variation in pregnancy rate after ET. This variation in
recipient quality does not appear to be a signiﬁcant factor after 60 days [19]. These results
conﬁrm that selection of recipients is critical to ET success.
While fertility in dairy cattle has been compromised over the last several years due to
increased milk yields [20,21], this has not been the case regarding beef cattle. Higher
pregnancy rates have been shown for dairy heifers compared to dairy cows, and this
difference is mainly attributed to the exceptionally high milk yields of lactating cows
[9,22], while the same is not always true for beef cows [8]. No beneﬁt in pregnancy rate of
recipients is obtained by using beef heifers when compared to beef cows [8], and in some
cases, it can be a disadvantage to use heifers as recipients (Table 1).

The suitability of a recipient female is determined by time of estrus expression and
presence of a functional corpus luteum. Spell et al. [12] demonstrated that pregnancy rates
tended to be greater when recipients were in estrus coinciding with the donor or 12–24 h
before (synchrony +12, +24, respectively) and were lower in recipients in estrus 12 h after
the donor [6,23]. Hasler et al. [23] suggested that the slight difference in pregnancy rate
may be attributed to the embryos from superovulated cows being more advanced than those
from untreated cows. However, several reports have been in agreement that there are no
signiﬁcant differences in pregnancy rates when donor–recipient synchrony is within 24 h
[10,13,24,25].
The ability to prolong and shorten the luteal phase with PGF can result in estrus and
ovulation within a 4-day period. Prior to the recent development of new synchronization
protocols, such a treatment was usually acceptable for embryo transfer recipients. Accuracy
of visual estrus detection can be very important in matching the stage of the embryo with the
time of estrus. The variability in the time at which females initiate estrus can make accurate
detection very difﬁcult. Newer protocols incorporating the control of both the luteal and
follicular status have increased the precision of synchronization to permit ﬁxed-time embryo
transfer without detection of estrus [1,26]. Treatments consist of insertion of a progesterone-
releasing intravaginal device and administration of estradiol benzoate (2.5 mg) and 50 mg of
progesterone (i.m.) on day 0 (to synchronize follicular wave emergence), PGF at the time of
device removal on day 8 (to ensure luteolysis), and 24 h later, treatment with a low dose of
estradiol benzoate to synchronize ovulation [26]. This method allows for very tight
synchrony of the recipient females. However, under the inﬂuence of estradiol, some animals
may ovulate smaller follicles and not be suitable for transfer. Higher pregnancy rates can be
achieved with the one-injection PGF method, but the lower transfer rate results in fewer total
pregnancies (Table 2). The duration of exposure to progesterone by CIDRs may affect
fertility of recipients because using CIDRs for 7 and 8 days tended to be more beneﬁcial than
use for 5 or 6 days (Table 3). At Ovagenix, it appears our most consistent results involving
synchronization of recipient females involve the use of CIDRs in addition to PGF.
Studies have been performed to treat recipients with appropriate additives, hormones, or
drugs to increase embryo survival. Some of these include supplemental progesterone,
equine chorionic gonadotropin (eCG), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), recombinant
bovine somatotropin (bST), GnRH, ﬂunixin meglumine, and propylene glycol.

More recently, studies were designed to evaluate the potential for administration of bST
to increase pregnancy rate. No signiﬁcant beneﬁt has been detected in non-lactating cows
or heifers in two separate studies [33,34]. The use of ﬂunixin meglumine by Purcell et al.
[35] and Schrick et al. [36] has indicated that it may be beneﬁcial in increasing pregnancy
rate when administered at time of transfer or shortly before. The belief is that the PGs are
released by the manipulation of the uterus and are detrimental to the viability of the
embryo. Flunixin muglumine is a non-steroidal anti-inﬂammatory that blocks
cyclooxygenase and therefore inhibits PG synthesis.
Recipients that fail to become pregnant after the ﬁrst transfer are generally recycled for
second and even third attempts for transfer. As early as 1980, Nelson et al. [37] concluded
that for surgical transfers, there was a decrease in fertility from ﬁrst transfer to second and
from second to third of 13 and 16 percentage points, respectively. Similar results were
obtained from recent data at Ovagenix with a 12 and 12 percentage point decrease from
ﬁrst to second and from second to third transfers, respectively (Table 5).
5. Transfer
Nearly all embryos are currently transferred non-surgically. Experience of the operator
remains important as the operator determines not only the site of deposition of the embryo
within the selected uterine horn, but also the degree of trauma inﬂicted during the transfer
[13]. Schneider et al. [6] reported drastic differences in results among technicians of
varying skill levels. Embryo survival rates following non-surgical transfer have been
reviewed by Seidel [25] who suggested that factors such as ejection site of the transferred

Single embryo transfer should always be performed ipsilateral to the corpus luteum.
Within the ipsilateral uterine horn, differences have been demonstrated in depth of
transfer as Beal et al. [38] achieved higher pregnancy rates with deeper transfers than with
shallow transfers regardless of embryo grade. An even more important skill is the ability to
determine if a viable and acceptable CL is present. Spell et al. [12] reported pregnancy rate
was not reduced when using recipients with corpora lutea containing ﬂuid-ﬁlled lumen, as
previously believed. In addition, when using recipients with progesterone concentrations
from 0.58 to 16.0 ng/mL, no differences were detected in pregnancy rate [12]. Previous
reports have stated that the optimum range of circulating progesterone was between 2.0
and 5.0 ng/mL [39,40]. Spell et al. [12] concluded that the ﬂuctuation of progesterone
reﬂected a combination of different rates of CL development and the ﬂuctuation of
progesterone secretion during the early luteal phase. Corpora lutea that are at least 10 mm
in diameter are, by our company’s standards, acceptable in size. We have not observed
differences in pregnancy results as the CL increases in size above 10 mm in diameter and
earlier reports fail to indicate any correlations between CL size and pregnancy rates
[2,23,40,41].
6. Conclusion
The maintenance of recipients is a major economic component of producing ET calves.
If higher pregnancy rates are achieved by recipients, more efﬁcient use of embryos would
occur and ﬁnancial savings could be realized through reductions in costs associated with
transfer of embryos and maintenance of non-pregnant recipients. Essentials for a proﬁtable
ET program include excellent nutrition and management in conjunction with skilled
personnel to perform all aspects of recipient manipulation from synchronization of estrus
to transfer of the embryos. Many attempts have been made to identify modiﬁcations to
these programs that can consistently improve results, and we have attempted to outline
many of these modiﬁcations in this paper. However, without the appropriate attention to
aforementioned essential components, success of an ET program will not be likely.
