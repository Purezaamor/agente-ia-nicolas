ABSTRACT 
Regulation of whole-body flux rates and in vivo tissue metabolism of specific nutrients is 
discussed in relation to three developmental stages: prenatal, birth to weaning and postweaning. 
The marked decline in relative growth rate and weight-specific uptake of oxygen, glucose and 
amino acids of the fetus between mid and late gestation may be due in part to recently demon­
strated limitations in placental transport capacity during late pregnancy. In fetal, as in neonatal and 
weaned, animals, insulin is an important regulator of metabolic homeostasis, but evidence for its 
role in chronic regulation of nutrient partitioning and growth at any stage of development is 
equivocal. Somatotropin-dependent regulation of skeletal growth, mediated by insulin-like growth 
factor I, does not develop until soon after birth, but in fetal and postnatal animals somatotropin 
depresses lipid deposition in adipose tissue by antagonizing stimulation of lipogenesis by insulin, 
thereby sparing utilization of lipogenic nutrients and promoting net mobilization of nonesterified 
fatty acids. These and other long-term actions, including effects on mineral metabolism, are con­
sistent with the postulated role of somatotropin as an important homeorhetic regulator of post­
natal metabolism and growth of key tissues. The complex integration of tissue metabolism and its 
regulation in vivo must be studied systematically in meat animals at different stages of develop­
ment, grown at optimal and suboptimal rates. Existing concepts and methodology provide a 
foundation for beginning this task; further progress will be impossible without significant con­
ceptual and technical innovation. 
(Key Words: Nutrient Flux, Prenatal Growth, Postnatal Growth, Homeostasis, Homeorhesis.) 
Introduction 
Animal growth can be simply defined as the 
progressive net accretion of nutrients and their 
metabolites, which begins at conception and 
continues as long as nutrient supply exceeds 
requirements for the oxidative metabolism and 
regeneration of existing tissues. Meat animal 
production has traditionally been assessed in 
terms of rate, efficiency (feed:gain ratio) and 
composition of body or carcass weight gain. 
Several recent reviews have considered the 
endocrine regulation of meat animal growth in 
these terms, at whole-animal, tissue and cellu­
lar levels (Bauman et al., 1982; Trenkle and 
Marple, 1983; Etherton and Kensinger, 1984). 
In contrast, long-term regulation of the com­
plex and dynamic metabolic processes that lead 
to these commercially important, static end 
points of growth is poorly documented, largely

The continuum of growth and development 
can be arbitrarily classified into many different 
stages. In this review we will separately discuss 
three major phases: 1) prenatal, 2) birth to 
weaning and 3) postweaning. These phases are 
characterized by very different patterns of 
major nutrient supply that, in turn, affect rates 
and patterns of tissue metabolism and chemical 
deposition and opportunities for endocrine 
regulation.

has important influences on perinatal survival

Nutrient Supply to the Conceptus 
Gestational Changes. Recent technical ad­
vances have allowed measurement of uterine 
and umbilical exchanges of oxygen and key 
nutrients as early as mid gestation in chroni­
cally catheterized ewes (Bell et al., 1986) and 
cows (Reynolds et al., 1986). At this stage the 
fetus has reached only 5% of its birth weight 
(Rattray et al., 1974; Ferrell et al., 1976) and is 
truly immature relative to the much more fre­
quently studied late-gestation fetus, which is 
rapidly preparing for postnatal life. However, 
the mid-gestation fetus is growing at a relative 
rate several times that of the mature fetus. 
Fetal oxygen uptake in sheep increases 
dramatically between mid and late gestation, 
although at a somewhat slower rate than body 
growth (figure 1; Bell et al., 1987a). This con­
trasts to some extent with an observation of little 
change in weight-specific fetal oxygen uptake 
during bovine gestation (Reynolds et al., 1986), 
but in both sheep and cattle, relatively small ges­
tational changes in metabolism expressed in 
terms of wet body weight are disguised by the 
substantial decline in fetal body water content 
between mid and late pregnancy (Rattray et al., 
1974; Ferrell et al., 1976). Thus, in both 
species, fetal oxygen consumption per kg dry 
weight is two- to threefold greater in mid than 
in late pregnancy (figure 1), which parallels 
differences in relative growth rate and, in the 
sheep at least, rate of whole-body protein 
synthesis (Meier et al., 1981; Kennaugh et al., 
1987). These declines in relative growth and 
metabolic rates begin well before the likely 
imposition of placental limitation of nutrient 
supply (Mellor, 1983) and the major fetal 
endocrine changes in late gestation (Challis et 
al., 1984; Gluckman, 1984). The means by 
which they are regulated is unknown. 
Fetal glucose and lactate metabolism also 
increase markedly with gestational age in sheep 
(Bell et al., 1986) and cattle (Reynolds et al., 
1986). However, at no stage of gestation is the 
umbilical uptake of these substrates sufficient 
to support more than about 70% of oxidative 
metabolism, even assuming their complete oxi­
dation. It is thus apparent that in mid, as in 
late, gestation, the fetus relies extensively upon 
amino acid catabolism as a source of energy 
(Battaglia and Meschia, 1978), notwithstanding 
its very high rate of protein synthesis. 
In contrast to fetal growth, placental growth 
ceases or slows considerably soon after mid 
gestation (Bell, 1984; Etherton and Kensinger, 
1984). However, as previously discussed, pla­
cental transport (i.e., umbilical uptake) of 
oxygen and glucose continues to increase with 
fetal growth, and this is accompanied by simi­
lar increases in urea transport (Kulhanek et al., 
1974) and placental clearance of highly  dif­
fusible solutes (Bell et al., 1986; Reynolds and 
Ferrell, 1987). Uteroplacental oxidative metab­
olism proceeds at a rapid rate throughout this 
period, such that even in late gestation, when the 
fetus is many times larger than the placenta, 
the uteroplacental tissues consume 35 to 50% 
of uterine oxygen uptake in sheep (Sparks et al., 
1983) and cattle (Reynolds et al., 1986). The 
basis for the comparatively high rate of placental 
oxygen consumption in mid gestation, when 
fetal demands are small, is not known. Relatively 
greater gestational changes occur in net utero­
placental glucose uptake and apparent gly­
colysis in placental tissues in vivo (Bell et al., 
1986; Reynolds et al., 1986), indicating a 
changing pattern in placental metabolism con­
sistent with gestational variation in activities of 
placental enzymes (Zamiri and Blackshaw, 
1979). Thus, placental glucose consumption 
accounts for at least 70% of uterine glucose 
uptake, even in late pregnancy. Contrary to 
popular belief,  therefore, it is the placenta, 
rather than the fetus, which has the major im­
pact on maternal carbohydrate metabolism, 
although it must be recognized that part of the 
placental glucose requirement is met by extrac­
tion from the fetal circulation (Hay et al., 
1984b; Bassett, 1986).

recently been studied in ewes in which placen­
tal weight was experimentally reduced by pre-

The relative importance of oxygen, glucose 
and possibly other nutrients as limiting factors 
in placentally-induced fetal growth retardation 
is difficult to determine. Robinson et al. (1983) 
concluded that glucose supply is more likely to 
be limiting for fetal growth in carunclectomized 
ewes. Although Vileisis (1985) was able to 
slightly offset fetal growth retardation by in­
ducing maternal hyperoxia in pregnant rats 
with uterine arterial ligation, the same model 
also has provided strong indirect evidence for 
the importance of placental glucose supply 
(Vileisis and D'Ercole, 1986). However, mater­
nal hyperoxia is not necessarily a reliable way 
of ensuring normal fetal oxygenation in pla­
cental insufficiency, and a clear answer must 
await the development of techniques for long-
term, direct replenishment of fetal oxygen. 
Regulation of Placental Growth. The basis 
for the wide natural variation in placental size 
within breeds and species of domestic livestock 
is poorly understood. Much of this variation 
may be ultimately attributable to nutritional, 
environmental and other factors such as litter 
size (Alexander, 1978; Bell, 1984), but the hor­
monal or other mediation of these effects is 
obscure. Sex steroids may directly influence 
placental growth in small, polytocous species, 
but there is little evidence that they have other 
than a permissive role in placental development 
after early pregnancy in larger species (Alexan­
der, 1978). The possible role of other maternal 
hormones needs to be examined. 
The possibility that the fetus at least partly 
controls placental growth and functional de­
velopment and, thus, its own future growth, 
should be seriously considered. Various lines of 
evidence point to a role for fetal insulin in the 
regulation of conceptus growth (Hill and 
Milner, 1985). Prolonged administration of in­
sulin to fetal monkeys (Susa et al., 1979b, 
1984) and rabbits (Bassett and Fletcher, 1982) 
increased placental weight, and short-term 
insulin infusion into fetal sheep increased pla­
cental protein synthesis (Young et al., 1982). 
Further evidence for fetal-placental communi­
cation via insulin comes from experiments on 
pigs in which fetal decapitation caused fetal 
hyperinsulinemia (Martin et al., 1984) and a 
number of changes in placental composition 
(including increased protein content) and 
metabolism (Ramsey et al., 1985) consistent 
with effects of insulin on placental tissue ob­
served in vitro. Insulin's effects on placental 
growth may not be direct, but mediated by 
the insulin-like growth factors (lGF)/somato-
medins. The sheep placenta has a much greater 
affinity for IGF-II than for insulin (Owens et 
al., 1980; Read et al., 1986). In contrast to IGF-
I, IGF-II is present in ovine fetal plasma in 
much higher concentrations, and appears to be 
sensitive to fetal nutrient supply (Gluckman 
and Butler, 1983, 1985). 
The previously cited relationship between 
uteroplacental blood flow and placental size 
does not necessarily imply maternal vascular 
control over placental growth and is certainly 
not incompatible with the notion of long-term 
metabolic regulation by the conceptus as postu­
lated by Bassett (1986). Thus, it has been sug­
gested that decreased uterine blood flow in 
undernourished ewes is a consequence, rather 
than a cause, of the associated depression in 
placental metabolism (Chandler et al., 1985). 
Not surprisingly, placental size and fetal per­
fusion (umbilical blood flow) are also closely 
related in ewes of similar gestational age. Again, 
we would suggest that blood flow is responding 
to, rather than directing metabolism and 
growth of, the trophoblastic tissues, but as with 
the maternal placenta, definitive evidence is 
lacking. As noted earlier, macroscopic growth 
of the sheep placenta ceases soon after mid 
gestation, but umbilical capillary hyperplasia, 
possibly regulated by specific growth factors, 
appears to continue in approximate relation to 
the major increase in umbilical blood flow 
during late gestation (Teasdale, 1976). 
Fetal Gluconeogenesis. A controversial issue 
in fetal metabolism has been whether or not 
fetal gluconeogenesis makes a significant con­
tribution to the glucose requirements of the 
conceptus. Certainly, fetal lamb (Warnes et al., 
1977b) and calf livers (Prior and South, 1977) 
have the enzymatic capacity for gluconeogene­
sis in vitro. However, conflicting results have 
been obtained in experiments on fetal sheep in 
vivo. On one hand, Warnes et al. (1977a), 
Sparks et al. (1982) and Kitts and Krishnamurti 
(1982) found no significant incorporation of 
intravenously administered [14C] lactate into 
[14C]glucose in the whole fetus; more specifi­
cally, there appears to be almost no conversion 
of [14C]lactate or [14C]alanine to [14C]glucose 
in the fetal liver in vivo (Gleason and Rudolph, 
1985). Also, Anand et al. (1980) found identi­
cal glucose specific activities in maternal and 
fetal blood during maternal infusion of tracer,

Acute Regulation of Fetal Metabolism 
This section will consider only fetal supply 
and metabolism of glucose and amino acids, 
because placental transport and fetal utilization 
of fatty acids is small in pigs (Elphick et al., 
1980), sheep (Elphick et al., 1979) and cattle 
(Comline and Silver, 1976). Regulation of fetal 
mineral metabolism and its influence on skele­
tal growth, while extremely important, is 
beyond the scope of this paper and is reviewed 
elsewhere (Care and Ross, 1984). 
Placental Transport, Placental transport of 
glucose in sheep and other species occurs by 
facilitated diffusion (Widdas, 1952). Therefore, 
it should be indirectly affected by any endo­
crine or other influences upon maternal or fetal 
glycemia and thus, the maternal to fetal glucose 
concentration gradient. This scheme is compli­
cated by the fact that the placenta not only 
transports, but also consumes, large amounts of 
glucose (Sparks et al., 1983; Hay et al., 1983). 
Nevertheless, fetal uptake of glucose from the 
placenta is directly related to maternal glycemia 
over the range of values induced by maternal 
insulin infusion (Anand et al., 1980), starvation 
(Crandell et al., 1983; Hay et al., 1984b), 
prolonged undernutrition (B. J. Leury and 
A. W. Bell, unpublished data) and glucose infu­
sion, at least up to concentrations of 6 to 8 mM 
in sheep (Crandell et al., 1983). On the other 
hand, it is increased during insulin-induced fetal 
hypoglycemia (Simmons et al., 1978) and de­
creased or even reversed by fetal hyperglycemia 
with no change in maternal blood glucose 
(Simmons et al., 1979). 
Evidence for a direct effect of insulin on 
placental glucose transport in sheep is arguable. 
Two reports from the same laboratory have 
claimed positive responses to uterine close 
arterial infusion of insulin that did not affect 
maternal systemic insulinemia or glycemia 
(Paxson et al., 1978; Crandell et al., 1982). 
However, these responses were either transient 
(Paxson et al., 1978) or statistically uncon­
vincing (Crandell et al., 1982). In contrast, Hay 
et al. (1984a) were unable to influence uterine 
or umbilical glucose uptake in hyperinsulinemic 
but normoglycemic ewes. Fetal hyperinsuline-
mia with fetal and materal normoglycemia 
appears to have a similar lack of effect on 
placental glucose transfer (Hay et al., 1985; 
Jodarski et al., 1985). 
Unlike glucose, most amino acids are present 
in higher concentrations in fetal than in mater­
nal blood in sheep and other species (Battaglia 
and Meschia, 1978). The processes that enable 
net transport of amino acids from mother to 
fetus are complex and appear to vary between 
different groups of amino acids. However, it is 
clear that most amino acids must be actively 
transported, at least from the maternal circula­
tion into placental tissue. This, together with a 
considerable fetal capacity for synthesis of non­
essential amino acids, probably explains why, in 
contrast to glucose, fetal concentrations and 
net umbilical uptake of most amino acids are 
relatively insensitive to changes in maternal 
concentrations (Lemons and Schreiner, 1983). 
On the basis of their work on the guinea pig 
placenta perfused in situ, Carroll and Young 
(1983) have proposed that placental amino acid 
transfer is linked to protein synthesis and turn­
over. This hypothesis would predict that the

Fetal Metabolism. The concept of fetal 
autonomy in the endocrine regulation of 
metabolism is now well established and, in 
particular, the role of insulin in the mainte­
nance of fetal glucose homeostasis has been 
studied in some detail. In general, the factors 
controlling pancreatic secretion of insulin in the 
fetal lamb appear to be similar to those in post­
natal nonruminants, despite reduced pancreatic 
responsiveness to major secretogogues such as 
glucose and amino acids (Bassett and Fletcher, 
1982). It is unlikely that fetal insulin directly 
influences the rate at which glucose is obtained 
from the placenta (Hay et al., 1985; Jodarski 
et al., 1985), but there is little doubt about its 
influence on fetal glucose utilization. This was 
indicated by studies in which insulin infusion 
caused fetal hypoglycemia (Simmons et al., 
1978) and extended by observations of hyper­
glycemia and reduced glucose uptake and utili­
zation in streptozotocin-treated or pancreatec-
tomized fetal lambs (Brinsmead and Thorburn, 
1982; Fowden et al., 1986). Recently, the 
glucose clamp technique was used to demon­
strate that in normoglycemic fetal sheep, 
supraphysiologic levels of insulin cause an 
approximate doubling of glucose utilization 
(table l;Hay et al., 1985). 
Insulin administration to fetal sheep also 
causes decreased plasma and tissue free amino 
acid concentrations and whole-body flux rate 
of lysine (Horn et al., 1983), suggesting a nega­
tive effect on tissue protein degradation. Insulin 
or amino acids alone had little effect on the 
fractional synthesis rate of mixed proteins in 
individual fetal tissues. However, when infused 
with a mixture of amino acids, insulin acutely 
caused a threefold increase in protein synthesis 
rate in skeletal muscle (table 1; Horn et al., 
1983). As with glucose, it appears important to 
distinguish between the separate influences of 
insulin and substrate concentrations on amino 
acid uptake and incorporation into fetal tissue 
proteins.

Fetal secretion of catecholamines, by the 
adrenal medulla and at sympathetic nerve ends, 
is responsive to a variety of stressful stimuli 
during late gestation, particularly hypoxia 
(Comline and Silver, 1966). Metabolic re­
sponses to epinephrine infusion include hyper­
glycemia and elevated plasma levels of lactate, 
nonesterified fatty acids (NEFA) and a-amino 
nitrogen (Jones and Ritchie, 1978). The hyper­
glycemia appears to be due to both increased 
endogenous glucose production and decreased 
glucose utilization (Jones et al., 1983), the in­
creased production being apparently due to 
hepatic glycogenolysis (Bristow et al., 1983). 
Depressed glucose utilization in hypoxic fetuses 
may be a consequence of the inhibitory effect 
of catecholamines on pancreatic insulin release, 
mediated via pancreatic a-adrenergic receptors 
(Sperling et al., 1980). 
Chronic Regulation 
of Fetal Metabolism and Growth 
Thus far, only the short-term homeostatic 
regulation of fetal nutrient supply and utiliza­
tion has been discussed, with particular empha­
sis on the effects of insulin on glucose and 
amino acid metabolism. The orderly, allometric 
development of fetal organ systems, together 
with the general absence of developmental 
anomalies that would be expected if ontogeny 
merely followed opportunities provided by 
competition between different tissues and 
organs, strongly suggests long-term, coor­
dinated regulation of fetal metabolism and 
growth. This is consistent with the notion of 
homeorhetic control, as previously proposed 
for postnatal growth (Bauman et al., 1982; 
see later). 
Potential regulators of long-term changes in 
fetal metabolism and growth include insulin, 
the somatomedins, somatotropin, prolactin, pla­
cental lactogen (PL), estrogens and thyroid hor­
mones (Etherton and Kensinger, 1984). How­
ever, in terms of demonstrated effects on fetal 
metabolism, none of these hormones or growth 
factors fully satisfies the criteria for homeorhetic 
regulation, as defined in earlier reviews (Bauman 
and Currie, 1980; Bauman et al., 1982), i.e., 
long-term coordination or orchestration of  dif­
ferent aspects of metabolism at multiple sites 
in the body, to support a dominant develop­
mental or physiological process (in this case, 
fetal growth). 
Somatotropin, which we believe to be a 
homeorhetic regulator of postnatal growth, 
does not appear to be required for nearly nor­
mal musculo-skeletal growth in the fetus, as 
demonstrated in hypophysectomized fetal 
lambs (Parkes and Hill, 1985) and decapitated 
fetal pigs (Campion et al., 1980). This is con­
sistent with the apparent lack of influence of 
somatotropin on fetal plasma somatomedin-
like activity (Falconer et al., 1979; Parkes and 
Hill, 1985). In the fetal lamb, circulating soma­
totropin concentrations steadily increase during 
the last few weeks of gestation (Bassett et al., 
1970), coinciding with a major decline in lipo­
genic activity of adipose tissue (Vernon et al., 
1981). Fetal lambs infused with somatotropin 
for several days develop insulin resistance

Postnatal Growth 
There is a vast literature on the morphologi­
cal characteristics and regulation of postnatal 
growth in cattle, sheep, pigs and other species, 
and on regulation of cellular metabolism in 
vitro in a variety of tissues. In the following dis­
cussion our focus will be on the much sparser 
body of information on patterns and regulation 
of the metabolism of key nutrients in specific 
organs and tissues in vivo, particularly in grow­
ing meat animals. Special consideration will be 
given to the physiological basis for nutrient 
partitioning between lean and fat tissue growth 
in terms of how this relates to nutrient supply 
and utilization during different phases of post­
natal growth. 
Nutrient Supply 
and Utilization before Weaning 
The slowing of fetal growth in late gestation, 
presumably due to placental limitation of fetal 
nutrient supply, contrasts with the extremely 
rapid, early postnatal growth rates that can be 
achieved by well-fed neonates. As an extreme 
example, the baby pig normally doubles its 
birth-weight within 1 wk of parturition. Pre-
and postnatal growth rates of lambs were 
directly compared in the same animals by 
Mellor and Murray (1982), who reported a 
three- to fourfold increase in growth rate be­
tween late gestation and the first 10 d of post­
natal life. However, the postnatal growth rates 
attained in their study were probably close to 
optimal, and it must be recognized that, just as 
in late gestation, neonatal growth rates can be 
limited by maternal nutrition and other en­
vironmental factors, via their influence on milk 
supply. 
In the transition from fetal to early post­
natal life in ruminants and pigs, the most strik­
ing change in the pattern of nutrient supply is 
the marked increase in dietary lipids. For 
example, in ewe's or sow's milk containing 8% 
fat, 5% lactose and 5.5% protein, fat accounts 
for about 60% of total energy. This has im­
portant consequences for neonatal metabolic 
adaptation, as well as for rate and composition 
of growth. One such adaptation is a high rate 
of gluconeogenesis, which is apparent soon 
after birth in lambs (Warnes et al., 1977b) but 
takes several days to develop in newborn pigs 
(Pegorier et al., 1982). This appears to be neces­
sary despite a supply of milk lactose which, on 
a body weight basis, is at least equivalent to the 
placental supply of glucose in well nourished 
fetuses. However, metabolic rates are increased 
soon after birth, particularly if neonates are 
exposed to suboptimal environmental tempera­
tures and the rapid rates of irreversible loss 
(White and Leng, 1980) and oxidation (Lindsay 
and Oddy, 1986) of glucose in young lambs 
indicate a high specific requirement for glucose 
at least until weaning. Part of this requirement 
may be for growth, as recently discussed by 
Lindsay and Oddy (1986), who quoted their 
own unpublished observations that muscle glu­
cose uptake was directly related to growth rate 
in 1-wk-old  lambs, although not in milk-fed 
lambs at 4 to 5 wk. 
The regulation of hepatic gluconeogenesis in 
the early postnatal period has been studied in 
some detail in the baby pig because of the pre­
disposition of this species to neonatal hypo­
glycemia. Particular attention has been paid to 
the likely importance of adequate rates of hepa­
tic fatty acid oxidation and of secretion of glu-
coregulatory hormones, especially glucagon. In 
the newborn pig, meager lipid stores and a rela­
tively poor ability to mobilize them as NEFA 
are associated with low rates of hepatic fatty 
acid oxidation (Mersmann, 1974) and glucose 
synthesis (Pegorier et al., 1982). Exogenous 
administration of oleic acid to fasting newborn 
pigs failed to stimulate hepatic gluconeogenesis 
(Boyd et al., 1985), suggesting that if glucose 
synthesis is limited by rate of fatty acid oxida­
tion, the problem is related to intracellular 
transport or mitochondrial oxidative capacity, 
rather than to extracellular supply of fatty 
acids. The latter study also failed to demon­
strate a primary effect of glucagon on gluco­
neogenesis in fasted newborn pigs, in contrast 
to the sensitivity observed in 5-d-old pigs 
(Helmrath and Bieber, 1975). By comparison, 
the surge in glucagon secretion in newborn 
lambs appears to play a significant role in early 
postnatal glucose homeostasis (Sperling et al., 
1977), which, as in older animals, also is modu­
lated by exogenous glucose supply and insulin 
secretion (Susa et al., 1979a). 
Rapid rates of fat accretion in the unweaned 
animal are clearly related to dietary supply of 
preformed, long-chain fatty acids. Thus, the 
baby pig increases its body fat content 10-fold 
during its first week of postnatal life. The very

Nutrient Partitioning 
and Tissue Metabolism after Weaning 
The composition of growth in weaned rumi­
nants and pigs displays a biphasic pattern that is 
characterized by a marked increase in the rate 
of gain of fat relative to lean tissues when the 
animal attains a certain proportion of its 
mature lean body weight (Searle et al., 1972). 
The approximate body weight at which this 
quite distinct change in rate of fattening occurs 
varies between sex, breed and of course, 
species. It is usually close to that at which 
sexual maturity is reached (Searle et al., 1972; 
Lister et al., 1983), which, in turn, may be 
related to the time at which growth of the 
skeleton slows markedly. However, the influ­
ence of factors other than gonadal secretion of 
sex hormones is indicated by the fact that the 
pattern of growth of steers is quantitatively 
intermediate, but qualitatively similar to that of 
bulls and heifers (Berg et al., 1979). Also, entire 
male and female lambs maintained on a milk 
diet for up to 18 wk after birth entered the 
rapid fattening phase at body weights less than 
those associated with puberty (Searle and 
Griffiths, 1976). 
The weight at which rapid fattening begins 
has considerable practical significance because 
it is at about this point that the energy cost of 
producing lean meat is minimal (Lister et al., 
1983), yet most meat animals are slaughtered at 
heavier weights than this. As a consequence of 
the close relation between body composition 
and weight, attempts to increase lean versus fat 
growth and efficiency of gain by traditional 
nutritional or genetic means have almost in­
variably resulted in animals with greater mature 
body size. Some alternative means of manipu­
lating body composition independently of body 
weight will be considered at the end of this 
review, after discussion of principles relating to 
the regulation of whole-body supply of key 
nutrients and their partitioning between indi­
vidual tissues. 
Feed Intake and Whole-body Nutrient 
Supply, The rates of entry of key nutrients into 
the posthepatic circulation are determined by a 
number of interdependent factors, including 
quantity and quality of feed intake, efficiency 
of digestion and absorption, and splanchnic 
and extrasplanchnic metabolism of absorbed 
and endogenously synthesized nutrients. Princi­
pal among these is digestible nutrient intake, 
and it is tempting to assign to intake regulation 
a primary, deterministic role in the regulation 
of nutrient supply to the whole body, and 
hence growth rate. However, in the long term, 
intake must be controlled by the animal's 
metabolic status, rather than vice versa, and this 
has been recognized in the construction of inte­
grative models of regulation of feed intake and 
energy balance (Forbes, 1980; Weston, 1982), 
although specific mechanistic details are lack­
ing. Thus it seems likely that the demands of 
growing tissues somehow influence intake, 
which in turn determines rates of entry of 
nutrients into the portal and peripheral circula­
tions. This would explain the excellent correla­
tions between glucose entry rate and growth

Splanchnic Metabolism in Ruminants. The 
splanchnic tissues that comprise the liver and 
portal-drained visceral organs (PDV) have a 
major influence on the energy metabolism of 
the whole animal, both in terms of energy 
expenditure and nutrient consumption, and in 
terms of the quantity and quality of nutrients 
entering the posthepatic circulation (Bergman, 
1975, 1986). Data are too few to allow analysis 
of relations between splanchnic metabolism and 
growth performance, but if the general relation 
between level of feed intake and growth is ac­
cepted, a number of principles are now evident. 
Metabolic rate of the PDV increases with 
plane of nutrition in adult sheep (Webster et al., 
1975) and growing steers (Huntington et al., 
1986), as, presumably, does protein synthesis, 
implying proportional increases in oxidation 
of absorbed VFA and other nutrients, and in 
net utilization of absorbed amino acids. Al­
though intake did not affect weight-specific 
metabolic rates of rumen epithelium (Fell and 
Weekes, 1975) or duodenal mucosa in vitro 
(McBride and Milligan, 1985), or fractional pro­
tein synthetic rate of rumen tissues in vivo 
(Davis et al., 1981), these observations are not 
incompatible because of the stimulatory effect 
of intake on growth of gut tissues (Fell and 
Weekes, 1975). The effect of plane of nutrition 
on hepatic oxygen consumption in ruminants has 
not been fully reported, but the marked increase 
associated with feeding in sheep (Thompson et 
al., 1978) implies that hepatic energy expendi­
ture is also closely related to level of feeding in 
the longer term. Together, hepatic and PDV 
metabolism account for about 40% of whole-
body oxygen consumption in maintenance-fed, 
adult sheep (Thompson et al., 1978). This value 
may be even higher in rapidly growing animals, 
because variations in fasting metabolic rates of 
pigs and lambs growing at different rates were 
closely related to variations in the relative 
weights of liver and gastrointestinal organs 
(Koongetal., 1985). 
The net metabolism by gut tissues of ab­
sorbed nutrients has a major effect on the rates 
of appearance of these substrates in portal 
blood. Bergman (1975) estimated that 30, 50 
and 90% of absorbed acetate, propionate and 
butyrate, respectively, are metabolized by rumen 
epithelium in mature, maintenance-fed animals. 
Most of the acetate is oxidized (Pethick et al., 
1981) as, presumably, is propionate, although a 
small fraction of propionate is converted to lac­
tate (Weigand et al., 1972a). Much of the butyr­
ate is hydroxylated to form the ketone body, 3-
hydroxybutyrate, which is released into the 
portal bloodstream (Weigand et al., 1972b). 
The net metabolism of absorbed amino acids in 
the small intestine varies considerably between 
individual acids, but most are used in substan­
tial quantities (15 to 55% of intestinal absorp­
tion) (Bergman, 1986), presumably to support 
the high rate of intestinal cell turnover. 
Rates of portal appearance of absorbed 
nutrients and their metabolites are generally 
related to level of feed intake (Huntington et 
al., 1986). This, in turn, has a potent effect on 
the quantity and pattern of nutrients taken up 
and metabolized by the liver. Important excep­
tions are acetate and 3-hydroxybutyrate, for 
which the liver has only a low metabolic capa­
city. Thus, rates of entry of these nutrients into 
the posthepatic bloodstream and their availa­
bility for peripheral metabolism tend to reflect 
rates of feed intake and rumen fermentation 
(Annison et al., 1967). In contrast, propionate 
and a number of the amino acids are almost 
quantitatively extracted by the liver, the former 
being used largely for glucose synthesis (Brock-
man and Greer, 1980) and the latter suffering a 
variety of metabolic fates, including protein 
synthesis, oxidative deamination, transamina­
tion and gluconeogenesis (Bergman, 1986). 
Although intestinal absorption and portal 
appearance of glucose can be appreciable in 
ruminants fed certain types of high-concentrate 
diets (e.g., ground corn), hepatic gluconeogene­
sis is normally by far the most important source

make extensive use of other metabolites taken 
up from the circulation. For example, the PDV

but also spleen, pancreas and mesenteric and

amounts of arterial glucose, much of which ap­

complex lipids (especially phospholipids) or 
oxidized (Bell, 1980; Pethick et al., 1984), but 
the quantitative significance of these pathways 
is poorly defined in ruminants. 
Muscle Metabolism. In contrast to the liver, 
gut and other metabolically active tissues, 
resting skeletal muscle has a low weight-specific 
metabolic rate and capacity for nutrient utiliza­
tion. Nevertheless, its impact on whole-body 
metabolism is significant because of its substan­
tial mass. The contractile properties of muscle 
that enable it to perform its special biological 
roles, maintenance of skeletal integrity, posture 
and locomotion, are often ignored by those 
concerned with muscle metabolism and growth 
in relation to meat production. However, even 
moderate exercise increases energy expenditure 
of muscle several fold and substantially alters 
its pattern of nutrient utilization in sheep (Bird 
et al., 1981). Also, systematic long-term usage 
of muscles (training) influences their growth 
and composition (Lawrie, 1979). 
Skeletal muscle is not an easy tissue to study 
in vivo in the conscious animal because of its 
diffuse distribution and intimate association 
with other tissues such as skin, bone and 
adipose tissue. Nevertheless, useful quantitative 
information on muscle nutrient metabolism has 
been obtained by measurement of blood flow 
and arteriovenous concentration differences 
across the predominantly muscular hindlimb in 
ruminants (Bell, 1980; Pethick et al., 1984) and 
pigs (Reeds et al., 1986). Despite variations in 
catheter location, sampling techniques and 
blood flow methods, a reasonably consistent 
picture has emerged of the pattern of nutrient 
utilization in the hindlimb tissues of ruminants, 
when factors such as feed intake and quality, 
physiological state and level of muscular 
activity are taken into account. 
In the mature, maintenance-fed sheep, the 
metabolic requirements of resting hindlimb 
muscle appear to be met mainly by the net up­
take of acetate, glucose (corrected for lactate 
metabolism) and, to a lesser extent, 3-hydroxy-
butyrate and NEFA from arterial blood 
(Pethick et al., 1984). Amino acid catabolism 
is not extensive in ruminant muscle, but the 
oxidation of branched-chain amino acids makes 
a further small contribution to energy expen­
diture (Lindsay, 1982). Most of the acetate and 
3-hydroxybutyrate taken up by the hindlimb 
tissues is promptly oxidized (Pethick et al., 
1981; Pethick and Lindsay, 1982). In contrast, 
the oxidation of glucose and NEFA is less com­
plete, at least in the short term, presumably 
because of significant incorporation of these 
nutrients into glycogen and triglycerides, 
respectively (Pethick et al., 1983; Oddy et al., 
1985). The net uptakes of acetate, ketones and 
glucose are probably a reasonable indication of 
their rates of utilization in muscle and asso­
ciated tissues. However, net exchanges of some 
other nutrients simply reflect the difference 
between bidirectional fluxes, which may be 
substantial but, in some cases, similar in magni­
tude. These include NEFA (Pethick et al., 
1983), lactate (Faichney et al., 1981) and prob­
ably most amino acids, for example alanine 
(Lindsay et al., 1977). The "true" uptakes of 
these substrates may contribute significantly to 
oxidative or synthetic metabolism in hindlimb 
tissue, but their flux in the opposite direction 
must be included in any analysis of net tissue 
energy or nitrogen requirements.

Smith, 1982) (table 3). Relations between mus­
cle protein synthesis, degradation and growth 
have recently been studied in some detail in 
young pigs (Mulvaney et al., 1985). A general 
decline in fractional rates of synthesis and 
degradation was observed during growth from 
22 kg to 45 kg live weight. However, fractional 
degradation decreased more than fractional 
synthesis in rapidly growing longissimus muscle 
(figure 3), leading the authors to suggest that 
muscle growth rate is regulated by develop­
mental changes in fractional rate of protein 
degradation. This study also highlighted differ­
ences in the protein turnover of early-maturing 
vs late-maturing muscles (figure 3), a considera­
tion that is particularly important in the choice 
of muscles as indicators of whole-body or car­
cass muscle growth. 
Adipose Tissue Metabolism. The metabolic 
characteristics of adipose tissue that lead to net 
deposition or mobilization of body fat in rumi­
nants and nonruminants have been recently re­
viewed (Vernon, 1980; Thornton and Tume, 
1984; Etherton and Walton, 1986). Most of this 
very valuable information has come from 
experiments in vitro and will not be considered 
here. Instead, this section will be confined to 
quantitative aspects of adipose tissue metabo­
lism that will ultimately have to be studied in 
vivo, and some possible experimental ap­
proaches to this end. 
The relative importance of fatty acids syn­
thesized de novo and those of exogenous 
(dietary) origin to fat deposition in adipose  tis­
sue has been difficult to establish in vitro. Both 
Vernon (1980) and Thornton and Tume (1984) 
concluded that endogenous synthesis probably 
accounts for considerably less than 50% of lipid 
accretion in fattening ruminants and tentatively 
suggested that the balance comes from pre­
formed long-chain fatty acids of dietary origin. 
This suggestion is consistent with observations 
that lipoprotein lipase activity is high in adipose 
tissue from various sites in fattening sheep 
(Tume et al., 1983). However, it remains pos­
sible that the lipogenic capacity of adipose 
tissue in vivo is underestimated by in vitro 
assays (Hood and Thornton, 1980). Also, the 
extrapolation of in vitro data to the whole ani­
mal is complicated by metabolic differences 
between adipose depots (Vernon, 1980). There 
is therefore a clear need for techniques that will 
allow measurement of uptake and metabolism 
of long-chain fatty acids and lipogenic pre­
cursor substrates by adipose tissue in vivo, in 
relation to rates of supply of these nutrients in 
the whole animal.

measurements of whole-body NEFA and gly­

1984). Its potential significance lies not only in 
its likely major effect on glucose requirement

changes in glycemia and attendant counter-

hormones. Nevertheless, it is evident that 
exogenous insulin promotes glucose uptake by 
the hindquarters of adult sheep (Hay et al., 
1984a) and growing steers (Prior et al., 1984) 
and stimulates the net extraction of some 
amino acids in steers (Ahmed et al., 1983). It 
also restores the impaired hindlimb uptake of 
acetate in alloxan-diabetic sheep (Jarrett et al., 
1974; Pethick et al., 1981). These hindlimb 
responses have been attributed to muscle, 
which is probably correct, but insulin also 
stimulates glucose uptake and decreases NEFA 
output by the fat tail of Syrian sheep, which is 
predominently adipose tissue (Khachadurian et 
al., 1966). On the other hand, the induction of 
moderate physiological hyperinsulinemia in 
sheep fasted for 24 to 36 h, with maintained 
euglycemia, causes major decreases in the hepa­
tic output of glucose (Brockman, 1983) and 
uptake of glucogenic precursors other than 
propionate (Brockman, 1985). Conversely, 
glucagon stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis and 
gluconeogenesis, the latter by increasing hepatic 
uptake of lactate and glucogenic amino acids 
(Brockman et al., 1975) but not propionate 
(Brockman and Greer, 1980). Any peripheral 
effects of glucagon appear to be indirect and 
related to specific effects on hepatic metabo­
lism (Brockman, 1986). 
Taken together, these in vivo responses to 
insulin are consistent with its proposed role as 
a short-term promoter of nutrient uptake and 
storage, particularly in adipose tissue and mus­
cle. Despite claims to the contrary, there is no 
convincing evidence that variations in blood 
insulin concentrations within the normal range 
have a direct, long-term effect on protein syn­
thesis and growth of muscle and other skeletal 
tissues. Growth responses attributed directly to 
insulin are more likely due to modulation of 
tissue IGF release, or, during prolonged hyper­
insulinemia, to non-specific binding of insulin 
to the type I IGF receptor (Hill and Milner, 
1985). 
A number of other natural hormones and 
synthetic hormone-like substances are thought 
to influence the rate and composition of 
growth through longer-term effects on nutrient 
metabolism. Metabolic responses to some of 
these, such as the thyroid hormones and cor­
ticosteroids, have been intensively studied in 
isolated cells or tissues, but not in terms of 
whole-body nutrient fluxes or tissue metabo­
lism in vivo, particularly in growing meat 
animals. Others, notably the androgenic and 
estrogenic sex steroids and some of their 
synthetic analogues, such as diethylstilbestrol, 
trenbolone and zeranol, are known to stimulate 
muscle protein accretion and depress fat deposi­
tion in growing ruminants (Galbraith and 
Topps, 1981). The metabolic bases for these 
responses, particularly some specific effects on 
muscle protein turnover, have been recently 
reviewed (Buttery, 1983). These will not be 
further detailed here, but it is interesting to 
note that the long-term nature of these re­
sponses and their location at multiple sites in 
the body fulfill at least some of the criteria of 
homeorhetic regulation as previously defined 
(Bauman and Currie, 1980; Bauman et al., 
1982). In this context it can be argued that the 
dominant physiological drive served by the sex 
steroids is the acquisition of secondary sexual 
characteristics, which include specific effects on 
rate and composition of growth. 
The principles of homeorhetic regulation are 
best illustrated by the metabolic actions of 
somatotropin. In pigs, sheep and cattle, long-
term administration of somatotropin increases 
rate of gain and alters carcass composition via 
increased lean growth and reduced fat accre­
tion (Bauman and McCutcheon, 1986). Soma­
totropin effects primarily center on nutrient 
partitioning because digestibility of the diet, 
maintenance requirement and the energy cost 
of tissue protein deposition are not altered 
(Eisemann et al., 1986a; Bauman and Mc­
Cutcheon, 1986). In contrast to homeostatic 
signals such as insulin and glucagon, administra­
tion of somatotropin causes no acute changes in 
circulating concentrations of nutrients and sever­
al days of treatment are required before altera­
tions in performance are observed (Bauman and 
McCutcheon, 1986; Boyd and Bauman, 1988). 
The response to somatotropin involves the 
orchestration of metabolism in an extensive 
array of tissues so that a greater portion of 
nutrients are partitioned for muscle and bone 
growth or, in the case of a lactating animal, for 
milk production (Bauman and McCutcheon,

Current evidence suggests that these coordi­

tissues (for example adipose tissue) and indirect 
effects mediated by somatotropin-dependent

The primary growth response to somato­
tropin is increased nitrogen retention in muscle 
(table 4). This stimulation of lean growth is 
independent from any effects on feed intake. 
This was first shown in swine (Machlin, 1972) 
and has been confirmed by observations of in­
creased nitrogen retention in somatotropin-
treated beef heifers fed a near-maintenance 
ration (Eisemann et al., 1986c). In the same 
heifers, whole-body protein synthesis was 
increased, whereas whole-body rate of leucine 
oxidation decreased (Eisemann et al., 1986a). 
At the tissue level, preliminary indications are 
that somatotropin enhances net amino acid 
uptake by the hindquarters (Jois et al., 1985) 
and increases fractional rates of muscle protein 
synthesis in cattle (Eisemann et al., 1986b) and 
sheep (Pell and Bates, 1987). 
A second major effect of somatotropin is to 
depress fat accretion in adipose tissue (table 4), 
thereby making nutrients used for lipogenesis 
and esterification available for use by other 
tissues. This has recently been dramatically 
demonstrated in growing swine, in which 
almost linear negative responses in fat accre­
tion to increasing doses of exogenous somato­
tropin were observed (Boyd et al., 1986; Ether­
ton et al., 1986). Fat accretion is dependent on 
relative rates of lipid synthesis and mobiliza­
tion. In lactating cows, which have been exten­
sively studied, the reduced fat accretion with 
somatotropin treatment involves alterations in 
both processes with the relative importance of 
effects on lipid synthesis versus mobilization 
varying in a predictable manner according to 
energy status of the animal (Bauman and Mc-
Cutcheon, 1986). A similar situation appears to 
exist in growing animals. When nutrients are in 
short supply lipids are mobilized during

Animals receiving somatotropin must still be 
capable of maintaining metabolic homeostasis. 
What are the mechanisms whereby somato­
tropin treatment results in a chronic shift in 
nutrient partitioning while accommodating the 
ability of homeostatic controls to maintain 
steady state conditions in nutrient availability? 
In crystalizing the concept of homeorhesis, we 
proposed that homeorhetic controls would 
function at least partly by altering the sensi­
tivity and magnitude of response to homeo­
static controls (Bauman et al., 1982; Bauman, 
1984). Although studied to only a limited 
extent, results to date for somatotropin are 
consistent with this proposal. Reduced rates of 
glucose clearance in response to insulin have 
been observed in goats, pigs and cattle treated 
with somatotropin (Hart, 1983; Boyd and Bau­
man, 1988). Incubation of ovine, bovine and 
porcine adipose tissue with somatotropin for 
a few days antagonizes the lipogenic effects of 
insulin (Vernon, 1982b; Etherton et al., 1987; 
Walton and Etherton, 1986). Similarly, in pigs 
and cattle treated with somatotropin, the in vivo 
lipolytic response to epinephrine challenge is 
dramatically increased, as is the antilipolytic 
response to an insulin challenge (Bauman and 
McCutcheon, 1986; Peters, 1986; Boyd and Bau­
man, 1988). In the context of the growing animal 
this series of changes in adipose tissue would 
result in chronic redirection of nutrients so that 
less are partitioned to adipose and more are par­
titioned for lean growth, while at the same time 
metabolic homeostasis is maintained. In similar 
fashion, the increased calcium retention and 
bone growth in somatotropin-treated sheep 
(table 4) is supported by increased calcium 
absorption (Braithwaite, 1975). Although not 
yet investigated in meat animal species, studies 
on rats have demonstrated that the enhanced 
calcium absorption involves altering the set 
points for regulators of calcium homeostasis, 
including renal hydroxylation of vitamin D3 
and intestinal production of calcium-binding 
protein (Boyd and Bauman, 1988). 
The roles of somatotropin and other likely 
chronic regulators of nutrient partitioning 
in growing meat animals must be investigated 
in long-term studies in which in vivo measure­
ments of splanchnic, muscle, and if possible, 
adipose tissue metabolism of key nutrients are 
combined with measurements of whole-body 
flux rates. 
This general approach should include assess­
ment of the mechanisms of action of an excit­
ing new group of synthetic substances with 
many of the ^-adrenergic properties of the 
natural catecholamines. These compounds, 
notably clenbuterol and cimaterol, are potent 
stimulators of muscle protein deposition and 
growth and are depressors of fat deposition in 
adipose tissue in lambs (Baker et al., 1984; 
Beermann et al., 1986), steers (Ricks et al., 
1984) and swine (Jones et al., 1985). Early 
indications are that the dramatic increases in 
muscle protein accretion in growing lambs may 
be achieved more by decreased protein degra­
dation than by enhanced protein synthesis 
(MacRae et al., 1986), in line with the effects 
of clenbuterol on muscle protein turnover in 
rats (Reeds et al., 1986). Effects of the new 
synthetic compounds on adipose tissue metabo­
lism have not been studied in vivo, but clen­
buterol causes marked decreases in lipogenesis 
and fatty acid esterification and an even greater 
increase in lipolysis in isolated ovine adipocytes 
(Thornton et al., 1985). At least some of the 
effects of chronic administration of (3-adrener-
gic agonists on nutrient partitioning may be 
mediated by differential effects on insulin 
sensitivity in muscle and adipose tissue. Pro­
longed treatment of rats with the ^-adrenergic 
agonist BRL 26830 dramatically increased 
insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle (Challis 
et al., 1985), whereas similar treatment of 
rat adipose tissue with isoprenaline leads to 
insulin resistance in this tissue (Haring et al, 
1986). 
Conclusion 
It is possible that much of the large variation 
in rate and, more importantly, efficiency of 
postnatal growth between breeds and individual 
animals can be explained in terms of our hy­
pothesis of homeorhetic regulation of nutrient 
partitioning. However, definitive evidence is 
lacking, particularly at the level of integrated 
organ and tissue metabolism in vivo, in animals 
grown under production conditions. To illu-

of growth regulation. Future studies must inves­
tigate the interactions between these at all 
levels of biological organization. Ultimately, 
however, the complexity of mammalian growth 
biology demands that regulation of nutrient 
partitioning is assessed in the organism of inter­
est, the intact growing animal. The challenge to 
develop concepts and the appropriate metho­
dologies for testing them is clear.
