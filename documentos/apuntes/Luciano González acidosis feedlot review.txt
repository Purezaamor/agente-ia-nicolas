1. Introduction
Ruminal acidosis is a nutritional disorder of cattle which receives a lot of attention by the feedlot industry even though it
occurs with relatively low frequency. Monitoring services of animal health in North American feedlots report that only 1.9%
of animals housed in the feedlots develop digestive diseases (USDA, 2000) and the average cost of each treatment is $6.20
US (USDA, 2001). Monthly mortality rates in North American feedlots range between 0.17% and 0.42% of the animals and
digestive disorders are responsible for 30–42% of these deaths (Smith, 1998; Galyean and Rivera, 2003). In addition, ruminal
acidosis and feeding of acidogenic diets have been associated with a reduction in feed intake (Britton and Stock, 1989; Stock
et al., 1995), damage of the digestive tract tissue (Aslan et al., 1995; Nocek, 1997), liver abscesses (Nagaraja and Chengappa,
1998; Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007), laminitis (Nocek, 1997), and inﬂammatory responses (Plaizier et al., 2009). All these
effects may not only reduce animal production and proﬁtability but also welfare.
A lot of research has been conducted to study the effects of diet, management, cattle genetics, and the environment
on rumen function and metabolic processes. Previous reviews on ruminal acidosis have focused on rumen microbiology
(Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007), nutrition and diet formulation (Krause and Oetzel, 2006; Plaizier et al., 2009), bunk man-
agement (Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al., 2003), and metabolic and histological changes (Penner et al., 2011). However, the
effects of feeding and social behaviors on gastrointestinal function in cattle are less understood and research in this area is
scarce (Galyean and Rivera, 2003). Daily feed intake determines acid production whereas chewing determines saliva (and
thus buffer) production (Beauchemin et al., 1994). Therefore, these two components of feeding behavior (i.e. intake and
chewing) throughout the day are closely related to the course of ruminal ﬂuid pH or acid–base balances. The present review
focuses on the inﬂuence of feeding behavior on ruminal ﬂuid acid–base balance and expands on recent ﬁndings on the
relationships between feeding behavior and ruminal acidosis. Short-term feed intake behavior can be summarized by meal
size and frequency, eating rate and distribution of intake throughout the day (Nielsen, 1999; Tolkamp et al., 2000). It is
important to note, that the method used to calculate these short-term feeding behavior measurements (e.g. deﬁnition of a
meal) may have a great inﬂuence in the outcomes (Tolkamp et al., 2000). Therefore, caution should be taken when comparing
results among studies as discrepancies maybe due to this issue. Short-term chewing behavior can also be assessed through
the frequency and distribution of chewing bouts throughout the day. The interrelationships between feed intake behavior,
chewing behavior and rumen function can be studied over long (i.e. from day to day) or short (within a day) time spans. In
addition, such interrelationships also depend on diet characteristics and management.
The objective of this review is to expose the current knowledge about the interplay between rumen function, short-term
feeding and chewing behaviors, feed ingredients, and management.
2. Ruminal acidosis and the relationship with feed intake
Acidosis is often categorized in several forms, including acute and sub-acute types often called clinical and sub-clinical,
respectively. Animals exhibit acute acidosis as an overt illness following consumption of readily fermentable carbohydrates
in sufﬁcient amounts to threaten life (Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007). With sub-acute acidosis, feed intake and performance
might be reduced but animals do not appear sick (Owens et al., 1998). However, the economic impact caused by the effects
of sub-clinical acidosis on animal performance could be greater than those of acute acidosis (Britton and Stock, 1989).
Ruminal ﬂuid pH of 5.6 or lower is considered the benchmark for sub-acute ruminal acidosis whereas a pH below 5.0 is
considered the benchmark for acute acidosis (Owens et al., 1998; Krause and Oetzel, 2006; Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007).
In acute acidosis, ruminal ﬂuid pH reaches such low levels because of the accumulation of lactic acid as a result of increased
production while its utilization by ruminal microbes is reduced because lactic acid is not fermented any more (Nagaraja and
Titgemeyer, 2007). Subsequent absorption of organic acids into the bloodstream might overwhelm the bicarbonate buffering
system, the excretion rate of acids, and the capacity of tissues and organs to metabolize acids resulting in systemic acidosis
(Brown et al., 2000). In sub-clinical acidosis, the reason for pH dropping below 5.6 is the accumulation of volatile fatty acids.
Although lactic acid is produced during sub-clinical acidosis, it does not accumulate because lactate-fermenting bacteria
remain active (Goad et al., 1998) and rapidly metabolize it to volatile fatty acids. Ruminal ﬂuid pH does not always explain
the typical signs and symptoms of ruminal acidosis although it is the most commonly used indicator (Huber, 1976). Both
a reduction in feed intake and variation in feed intake among days have been used as indexes of subclinical acidosis based
on the concept that an increased variability from day to day is associated with feeding acidogenic diets (Britton and Stock,
1989; Stock et al., 1995; Bevans et al., 2005). However, the reduction in feed intake in acidotic cattle is inconsistent and
may depend on the extent and convergence of multiple factors associated with low ruminal pH and the control mechanisms
of feed intake. Many theories for the reduction of dry matter intake during sub-clinical acidosis have been hypothesized:
low ruminal ﬂuid pH, high concentration of fermentation products (volatile fatty acid, VFA), high osmolality, inﬂammatory

(acute-phase) responses, reduced rumen motility, and reduced ﬁber digestion. These changes might be detected by sensors
in the rumen wall, or within the body after the changes become systemic through the blood and sensed by the brain or
different organs such as the liver (Forbes and Barrio, 1992; Allen et al., 2005). Also, low pH of the rumen ﬂuid may reduce
ﬁber digestion which reduces rate of passage and may eventually reduce feed intake (Plaizier et al., 2009). However, this
theory is less likely in feedlot cattle because of the low ﬁber content of their rations.
In the ﬁrst theory, the accumulation of organic acids in the rumen results in low ﬂuid pH and chemical receptors in the
epithelium send a feedback signal to the brain to reduce intake (Forbes and Barrio, 1992). Alternately, low ﬂuid pH reduces
rumen motility and consequently the rate of passage of feed, which leads to a reduction of feed intake (Dougherty et al.,
1975; Slyter, 1976; Forbes and Barrio, 1992). Fulton et al. (1979b) were able to increase feed intake in steers experiencing
acidosis by maintaining ruminal pH above 5.75, with ruminal infusions of NaOH, compared with control steers with ruminal
pH below 5.35. Nevertheless, recent research in dairy cows (Khaﬁpour et al., 2009a,b) and beef cattle (Faleiro et al., 2011)
indicate that ruminal ﬂuid pH per se is not responsible for the reduction in feed intake. In a second theory, Owens et al.
(1998) suggested that the accumulation of organic acids and glucose increases the osmolality of the ruminal ﬂuid which
is responsible for the reduction of feed intake in acidotic cattle. Ruminal osmolality between 300 and 700 mOsm/kg were
reported in acidotic animals and seem to be related to the accumulation of organic acids and low pH in the ruminal ﬂuid
(Andersen et al., 1994; Khaﬁpour et al., 2009a,b). Absorption of organic acids into the bloodstream and water ﬂux from
the bloodstream across the rumen wall increases blood osmolality. Both high ruminal ﬂuid and blood osmolality have also
been linked to the control of feed intake in ruminants (Carter and Grovum, 1990; Forbes and Barrio, 1992; Langhans et al.,
1995). However, these hypotheses are difﬁcult to test in in vivo experiments because animals cease eating to avoid a further
reduction in pH or an increase in osmolality and differences are therefore not observed.
In the third theory, high production rate of fermentation products, particularly propionate, leads to an accumulation in
the rumen ﬂuid and subsequently in the blood. Fermentation products are then transported to tissues or organs, e.g. the
liver, where oxidation or metabolization into larger molecules takes place. Eventually, saturation of the rate of oxidation
or gluconeogenesis of organic acids in the liver sends a feedback signals to the central nervous system which triggers the
behavioral mechanisms to stop feed intake even within the course of a meal (Britton and Stock, 1989; Allen et al., 2005). In
another theory, certain factors arising during low pH phases within the rumen, such as bacterial endotoxins and histamine,
may also reduce the frequency and amplitude of ruminal contractions or have a systemic effect potentially linked with the
reduction in feed intake (Dougherty et al., 1975; Andersen, 2003; Plaizier et al., 2009). Chronic inﬂammation caused either
by bacterial endotoxins entering the bloodstream or by damage of the rumen epithelium under sub-clinical acidosis may
antagonize intake and growth by releasing cytokines and triggering an immune response (Kleen et al., 2003). This hypothesis
is supported by recent ﬁndings in which acidosis challenges induced by both grain and alfalfa pellet were equally successful
in reducing ruminal ﬂuid pH (Khaﬁpour et al., 2009a,b). However, the grain-induced acidosis resulted in lower daily feed
intake whereas the alfalfa pellet-induced challenge resulted in greater feed intake. Interestingly, blood lipopolysaccharides
(LPS) and haptoglobin increased in the grain-induced but not in the alfalfa pellet-induced challenge. The reduction in DMI of
acidotic cattle may therefore be linked to the acute phase response. Similar observations were made by our research group
as a reduction in concentrate DMI and ruminal ﬂuid pH was accompanied by an increase in blood haptoglobin (González
et al., 2008c). However, elimination of forage (straw) from the diet led to a large reduction in ruminal ﬂuid pH but no effects
were observed in concentrate intake or blood haptoglobin (Faleiro et al., 2011).
3. Acid–base balance in the rumen
The process of eating and the fate of ingested feed in ruminants have a great inﬂuence on the acid–base balance of rumen
ﬂuid. After being chewed in the mouth, the ingested feed goes to the reticulorumen where fermentation by microbes results
in the production of organic acids (Fig. 1). The amount of organic acids produced at a given point in time after ingestion
depends on the amount of feed ingested (meal size), and the rate and extent of degradation of feed by the rumen microbes.
After dissociation, these organic acids liberate protons which may decrease ruminal ﬂuid pH (Fig. 1). However, this reduction
in pH depends on the amount of saliva added to the feed while eating (which neutralizes the acids) and the existing buffering
capacity of the ruminal ﬂuid (resistance to pH change). Feed is chewed in the mouth to reduce particle size, moisten it and
form the bolus for easy swallowing. The length of time spent chewing per unit of feed depends on the type of feed, eating rate
and determines feed ensalivation (mL of saliva added per gram of feed). The greater the eating rate, the lower the amount
of saliva per unit of feed is added (Fig. 2). Eating rate is inﬂuenced by the proportion of forage, particle length and initial
moisture content of feedstuffs (Bailey, 1961; Beauchemin et al., 2008) but also by other factors such as hunger, feeding
management (Carter et al., 1990), competition for feed or social pressure from pen-mates (Olofsson, 1999) and even the
presence of predators (Illius and Fitzgibbon, 1994). Ruminants also regurgitate the feed from the rumen back to the mouth to
chew it again and further reduce particle size until particles are small enough to pass from the rumen to the lower digestive
tract (Allen, 1997). The rate of saliva secretion during rumination (25 mL/min) is similar to that during eating (20 mL/min)
and about 2 times greater than during resting in cattle (10 mL/min in steers; Bailey and Balch, 1961). Saliva production is
very important for rumen function because it has high buffering capacity as a result of high concentration of bicarbonate
(125 mEq/mL) and phosphate (20 mEq/mL of phosphate; Bailey and Balch, 1961). Saliva production during eating, ruminating
and resting contributes to neutralizing around 0.30 of the acids produced daily in dairy cows (Erdman, 1988; Allen, 1997;
Russell, 2002; Fig. 1). However, this ﬁgure should be lower in feedlot cattle because of shorter chewing time and consequently,

of protons absorbed from the rumen.
4. Factors affecting feeding behavior and ruminal ﬂuid pH
This section describes several factors that may affect feeding behavior in feedlot cattle and therefore cause or help
to prevent ruminal acidosis. Understanding such factors is useful in developing preventive strategies and to explain the
underlying mechanisms affecting rumen ﬂuid pH where multiple factors converge. Factors affecting feeding behavior, and
consequently ruminal ﬂuid pH, can be divided into those related to feed characteristics, feeding or animal management, and
the environment.

4.1. Feed related factors affecting feeding behavior and rumen function
From a practical point of view, only two strategies related to feed formulation can be useful to prevent ruminal acidosis.
The ﬁrst is related to diet formulation including the choice of grain and forage in terms of type and proportion in the diet. The
second involves the use of feed additives such as buffers, natural plant extracts, ionophores, yeasts, direct-fed microbials,
organic acids or polyclonal antibodies against Streptococcus bovis.
4.1.1. Amount and type of grain
Feedlot
 diets are commonly based on high-concentrate diets where cereal grains are the main ingredient and the content
of non-structural carbohydrates is high. Structural carbohydrates have slow rumen degradation rates as opposed to the
fast rate of starch, sugars and pectins. Acidosis is most prevalent following ingestion of large amounts of starch present
in cereal grains. The contribution of starch to the acidogenic power of a diet depends on the starch content and its rate
of degradation in the rumen. According to starch content, grains are ranked in decreasing order as follows: wheat (0.78),
corn and sorghum (0.72), and barley and oats (0.58), whereas NDF content follows the opposite pattern (Huntington et al.,
2006). There is a strong negative correlation (r = −0.97) between ruminal ﬂuid pH and digestible starch content of a diet
(Sauvant et al., 1999). The rate of cleavage of starch to glucose is related to the ruminal degradation rate and varies with
grain source, grain processing, and starch type (Owens et al., 1997; Huntington et al., 2006). In general, metabolizable energy
from grains increases with both ruminal degradation and total tract digestion. Ruminal degradation rate and extent depends
on the chemical and physical structure of starch granules such as size, shape and embedment within the protein matrix.
Both concentration of the soluble fraction and the fractional degradation rate of starch have a great inﬂuence on ruminal
degradability, both being greater for rapidly degrading starch feedstuffs (Offner et al., 2003). Several processing methods are
known to increase ruminal degradability of starch such as reduction of particle size, thermal treatment (e.g. steam ﬂaking)
and shear forces (e.g. extrusion). Based on ruminal starch degradation rate, grains are ranked from fastest to slowest as
follows: oats, wheat, barley, high moisture corn, steam ﬂaked corn, dry rolled corn, dry whole corn, and dry whole milo
(Herrera-Saldana et al., 1990; Huntington, 1997; Offner et al., 2003; Huntington et al., 2006). The choice of grain type and
processing is therefore one of the most important considerations to prevent ruminal acidosis. It is desirable to increase the
extent of grain digestion without increasing the risk for ruminal acidosis and causing a reduction of DMI (Owens et al., 1997).
New grain varieties or hybrids are therefore becoming available in the market with characteristics such as harder or more
vitreous endosperm (e.g. ﬂint vs. dent or ﬂoury corn) resulting in a slower rate and extent of ruminal digestion and therefore,
higher ruminal ﬂuid pH (Taylor and Allen, 2005a; Jaeger et al., 2006). However, processing such as steam ﬂaking or high
moisture corn might eliminate such differences in ruminal degradability of starch among hybrids (Harrelson et al., 2009).
The acidogenic effect of a diet increases with the proportion of concentrate and rate and extent of ruminal degradability
of the ingredients (Fulton et al., 1979a; Murphy et al., 1994). However, other factors not related to the rate of acid production
in the rumen also play an important role in the acid–base balance. First, diets with lower proportions of forages and smaller
particle sizes have greater density allowing faster eating rate and therefore lower feed ensalivation (Beauchemin et al., 2008),
and result in larger meals of both total and ruminally degradable DM (Dado and Allen, 1995; Tolkamp et al., 2002). Similarly,
eating rate is faster, time spent eating is shorter and feed ensalivation lower for whole wheat compared to whole corn
(Beauchemin et al., 1994), and time spent eating is shorter for vitreous compared with ﬂoury corn (Taylor and Allen, 2005b).
Second, time spent ruminating per day and per unit of intake is also reduced more in diets with higher concentrate propor-
tion (Faleiro et al., 2011). Presentation of the diet is also an important factor affecting feeding behavior and ruminal ﬂuid pH.
For instance, feeding mixed rations, as opposed to ingredients fed in separate containers, promotes better synchronization
in time between acid production and neutralization and elimination. Mixed rations avoid the ingestion of large amounts
of concentrates shortly after feed delivery which is typical of feeding separate ingredients, and reduces daily eating rate
(DeVries and von Keyserlingk, 2009a) which is expected to increase feed ensalivation. However, feedlot cattle fed a mixed
ration with 100 g/kg of corn silage DM showed similar ruminal pH to those fed free choice of the ingredients allowed for ad
libitum consumption (Moya et al., 2011). However, animals fed free choice of ingredients consumed a greater proportion of
corn silage (200 g/kg of DM) and had longer and fewer meals per day with more frequent visits per meal compared those fed
the diet containing 100 g/kg of corn silage DM. Third, adaptations to acidogenic diets have to occur at several levels of the
animal: rumen microbial populations, epithelium of the digestive tract, metabolism, and behavior. Adaptations in the rumen
epithelium help to improve the absorption and metabolism of organic acids whereas other metabolic or physiologic adap-
tations of the animal help to eliminate acids by oxidation (e.g. in the liver), synthesis of larger molecules, and neutralization
and excretion of protons (Aschenbach et al., 2011; Penner et al., 2011). However, excessive acid production may also lead to
parakeratosis and hyperkeratosis which may consequently reduce the absorption of acids (Dirksen et al., 1985). Adaptation
of the rumen microbial populations includes maintaining active lactate-fermenting and reducing lactate-producing bacte-
ria (Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007). Behavioral adaptations to cope with acidotic diets are also very important although
research in this area is scarce. For instance, animals consuming a high-forage diet may be adapted to eat a large meal after
feed delivery in the morning as they can deal with the low acid production. However, the acidogenic effect of such meals
increases with the proportion of concentrate and ruminal degradability. Therefore, animals need to change their feeding
behavior toward smaller, more frequent meals to avoid the ‘harmful’ effect of large meals from more fermentable diets. The
animal needs ﬁrst to understand the post-ingestive consequences of diets that are more fermentable (such as unpleasant

The behavioral adaptations to more acidogenic diets are evident from the pioneering work done by Fulton et al. (1979a).
These authors fed during 5 d each of 4 step-up diets containing increasing amounts of grains with 350, 550, 750, and 900 g/kg
of DM of either corn or wheat. Animals fed the corn diet increased feed intake whereas those fed the wheat diet decreased feed
intake as the proportion of concentrate increased. Animals adapted to rations containing greater proportion of concentrates
by reducing the amount of feed consumed during the ﬁrst 2 h after feeding and increasing the amount consumed later
in the day. This reduction was greater in the more acidogenic wheat (from 2.40 to 0.35 kg/h) than in the corn diet (from
2.45 to 1.25 kg/h as the proportion of corn in the diet increased from 350 to 900 g/kg of DM, respectively). The daily intake
pattern disappeared completely when the proportion of wheat reached 900 g/kg of the diet DM (daily intake was equally
spread throughout the day). Interestingly, calculations from the NRC (2000) model indicate that the amount of the A plus
B1 fractions ingested within the ﬁrst 2 h after feeding increased from 0.61 to 0.83 kg/h as the proportion of corn increased
from 350 to 900 g/kg of DM. However, that amount of feed ingested decreased from 0.61 to 0.24 kg/h as the proportion of
wheat increased from 350 to 900 g/kg of diet DM. The A and B1 fractions of the NRC (2000) model are the most rapidly
degradable fractions of the diet and the most likely to contribute to the short-term drop in ruminal ﬂuid pH. Despite these
striking behavioral adaptations and lower daily feed intake, daily mean ruminal ﬂuid pH was lower and the post-prandial
drop greater in the wheat compared with the corn diet. Therefore, the modiﬁcation in feeding behavior helped to maintain
ruminal ﬂuid pH while adapting to greater proportions of corn in the diet but it was not sufﬁcient in the wheat diet. These
data indicate that daily feed intake is reduced when adaptations in feeding behavior and metabolism are not enough to avoid
a build-up of organic acids.
The previously described behavioral adaptations during the step-up period attempt to avoid an overload of organic acids
that could potentially overwhelm the capacity of the animal. However, these behavioral adaptations may disappear after
that other physiological or microbiological adaptations have taken place and the animal is able to deal with greater acid
loads. This hypothesis is supported by recent research in dairy cows which consisted of a 1-d acidosis challenge (change to
diets with more concentrates) repeated for 3 consecutive times at 14-d intervals. The acidosis challenge reduced the pH of
the ruminal ﬂuid and this reduction was greater as the challenge was repeated (Dohme et al., 2008; Table 1). This suggests
that there are physiological adaptations over time that allows animals to deal with lower ruminal ﬂuid pH such as epithelial
cell morphology and function for improved absorption and metabolization of acids (Penner et al., 2011). During the day of
the acidosis challenge, animals responded to low ruminal ﬂuid pH with an increase in meal frequency and a reduction in
meal size (DeVries et al., 2009). However, these behavioral adaptations (meal size and frequency) tended to disappear as the
animals’ physiological adaptation was able to cope with lower ruminal ﬂuid pH in the second and third challenges (Table 1).
4.1.2. Feed additives
Feed additives may have direct or indirect effects on ruminal ﬂuid pH. Direct effects of feed additives on ruminal ﬂuid
pH may be the result of changes in microbial populations or in the physicochemical conditions of the rumen (e.g. buffering
capacity and rate of passage). Polyclonal antibody preparations against S. bovis is one example with a direct effect on ruminal
pH through a reduction in rumen lactate-producing microbial populations (DiLorenzo et al., 2006; Blanch et al., 2009). Mineral
salts used as feed additives may have a direct effect on ruminal pH through changes in the physical environment as they
increase water consumption and therefore the rate of dilution or passage in the rumen, which contributes to greater wash
out of small feed (starch) particles reducing organic acid production (Russell and Chow, 1993). Buffers, such as sodium
bicarbonate, have a direct effect on rumen ﬂuid pH through chemical changes in the rumen because they neutralize acidity
through H+ sequestration and increase buffering capacity of ruminal ﬂuid (Kohn and Dunlap, 1998). Indirect effects of feed
additives on rumen ﬂuid pH include those derived from changes in feeding behavior such as meal size or frequency. These
effects of feed additives on feeding behavior may be a result of changes in ﬂavor, or in the concentration of nutrients,
metabolites (VFA), pH or osmolality in body ﬂuids and organs. Such changes might act as feedback signals to determine the
onset and termination of meals through the central nervous system. Therefore, feed additives may modify feeding behavior
which may eventually affect ruminal ﬂuid pH patterns in addition to their direct effect on the rumen (microbial populations,

Monensin is an ionophore widely used as a feed additive in ruminants’ diets around the world although it is currently
banned in Europe. This antibiotic has a direct effect on the ruminal microbial populations leading to changes in the amount
and proﬁle of organic acid production, increasing propionate while reducing acetate, and improving feed efﬁciency (Bergen
and Bates, 1984). Propionate has hypophagic effects in cattle because it triggers early satiation mechanisms when oxidized in
the liver leading to smaller meals and lower daily feed intake (Allen et al., 2005). Feeding behavior of monensin-supplemented
steers was characterized by more frequent (7.1 vs. 6.2 ± 0.50 meals/d) and smaller meals (2.2 vs. 3.7 ± 0.4 kg/meal) that were
eaten at a slower rate than control counterparts (0.23 vs. 0.27 ± 0.019/h for monensin vs. control, respectively, assuming an
exponential curve; Erickson et al., 2003). This has led to more stable ruminal fermentation patterns throughout the day, lower
variance (0.14 vs. 0.21 ± 0.02) and lower post-prandial drop (1.3 vs. 1.5 ± 0.13 pH units) of ruminal ﬂuid pH in monensin-fed
compared with control-fed animals, respectively. Lower feed intake variation was not only observed within the day but also
among days with the use of monensin (Stock et al., 1995). Similarly, Bach et al. (2007) suggested that greater meal frequency
of yeast supplemented dairy cows was involved in greater ruminal ﬂuid pH compared with no yeast supplementation. A
deeper discussion on the effect of yeast supplementation on ruminal ﬂuid pH can be found in this special issue (Calsamiglia
et al., 2012). In conclusion, monensin changes microbial populations and increases propionate production which affects
hunger-satiety mechanisms reducing the size and increasing the frequency of meals. These changes in feeding behavior
contribute to more stable ruminal fermentation and pH throughout the day.
Bicarbonate is the dominant natural ruminal buffer secreted through saliva and traditionally added to diets to moderate
ruminal ﬂuid pH. Sodium bicarbonate was extensively studied for several decades as a feed additive, although responses
are contradictory and confusion in the interpretation of results exist (Russell and Chow, 1993). Despite the large amount
of research with this feed additive, feed intake and chewing behaviors have not received much attention until recently.
González et al. (2008a,b) fed diets containing bicarbonate at 0, 12.5, 25, and 50 g/kg DM of the concentrate with ad libitum
access to both concentrate and straw. Heifers ate the most concentrate when fed the 12.5 and the least when fed the 50 g/kg
DM bicarbonate diet (7.7 vs. 5.7 ± 0.7 kg DM/d for the 12.5 and 50 g/kg DM bicarbonate diets respectively; P<0.05), with no
differences among the rest of the treatments. Addition of bicarbonate at any level reduced the number of hours with ruminal
pH below 5.8 from 12 to 3 h/d. The addition of sodium bicarbonate at concentrations greater than 12.5 g/kg of DM had no
further beneﬁt on ruminal pH and may reduce feed intake. The best treatment (12.5 g/kg DM) achieved a balance between
high intake and good rumen function through a distribution of the daily intake in several small meals throughout the day
(Fig. 3) and the longest time spent ruminating (5.4 vs. 3.9 ± 0.60 min/d for the 12.5 vs. 50 g/kg DM bicarbonate). Sodium
bicarbonate at 10 g/kg of diet DM has also reduced rumination in dairy cows (Mooney and Allen, 2007). In addition, heifers
fed the 50 g/kg of DM bicarbonate ate larger meals shortly after feed delivery (1.2 vs. 1.9 ± 0.26 kg DM/meal in the 12.5 vs.
50 g/kg DM bicarbonate during the ﬁrst 2 h after feeding; González et al., 2008b). Such large meals (58% larger on average)
after feed delivery resulted in a large reduction in ruminal ﬂuid pH although the nadir was reached at 12 h after feeding.

4.2.
 Feeding management
Feeding management is an important consideration in animal production because it may affect production costs and
animal performance. Feeding behavior is particularly sensitive to feeding management and may therefore disrupt or help to
maintain the acid–base balance in the rumen. For example, feeding practices that reduce meal size and eating rate, increase
meal frequency, or result in more uniform feed intake throughout the day are desirable because the risk of ruminal acidosis is
reduced. Feeding regimes that act in harmony with feeding behavior and allow the maintenance of ruminal acid–base balance
are desirable whereas those that disrupt the feeding pattern should be avoided. The following section shows examples of
feeding management strategies leading to effects on ruminal pH that are positive, such as feeding frequency, or negative
such as clean bunk management or restricted feeding.
4.2.1.
 Feeding frequency
Increasing the frequency of feed delivery during the day might decrease acidosis problems by favoring more stable acid
production throughout the day and improving the synchronization with saliva production, and absorption and passage of
acids from the rumen. To achieve this, feeding management should promote greater meal frequency or more even distribution
of feed intake, reduce meal size particularly of large meals typically occurring after the delivery, and promote rumination
at times of the day with low ruminal ﬂuid pH. Several studies have reported that more frequent feeding leads to more
stable ruminal pH with fewer daily ﬂuctuations (e.g. Soto-Navarro et al., 2000). In theory, more frequent feed delivery is
more important for diets with medium to high rates of degradation, during adaptation to more concentrate diets, and poor
feeding management including large day-to-day variation in the proportion of forage of the diet or in the amount of feed
delivered (Grant and Albright, 1995). Cattle being adapted to higher concentrate diets exhibited greater feed intake when
the frequency of feeding increased from 1 to 4 times per day which may be attributed to a reduction of acidosis (Tremere
et al., 1968). Multiple feedings may also reduce the magnitude of feeding errors and abnormal feeding patterns, or promote
the self-regulation of short-term feed intake of cattle (Pritchard and Bruns, 2003). In addition, more frequent feeding may
reduce social competition for feed and aggression after delivery (DeVries et al., 2005).
A study carried out by Robles et al. (2007) increased the frequency of feeding from 1 to 4 times per day in beef cattle
fed concentrate and straw to allow ad libitum consumption. Increasing the frequency of feeding did not affect intake, meal
frequency or size, mean ruminal pH or fermentation proﬁle as daily averages. However, feed intake and chewing patterns
were drastically changed by treatments. Eating rate and the amount of feed consumed (−31%) during the 2 h after the ﬁrst
morning feeding decreased linearly with feeding frequency, which may indicate lower motivation for feeding. Twice daily
feeding showed two similar peaks of feed intake after each feed delivery and delayed the second peak of feeding typically
observed around sunset in heifers fed once daily (Fig. 4). This led to 2 equal peaks in ruminal ﬂuid pH before each feeding
with the second one being 0.85 pH units greater at 12 h post-feeding compared with the once daily feeding. The second peak
in ruminal pH was the result, in part, of a build up of buffering capacity in the rumen from saliva production as animals
spent more time ruminating during the 2 h previous to the second feed delivery while waiting for the feed (Fig. 4). Ruminal
ﬂuid pH of heifers fed four times per day fell steadily from the ﬁrst morning feeding until 12 h post-feeding (Robles et al.,
2007). In summary, frequency of feed delivery changes both feed intake and rumination patterns throughout the day, which

4.2.2.
 Feed bunk management
Feed can be delivered following different approaches in feedlots, which is usually done by varying the amount of feed
offered or the length of time that feed is available each day, or both. There are 3 main types of feed delivery methods or
feeding programs: ad libitum or traditional, restricted or limit feeding, and clean bunk management. Ad libitum or traditional
feed bunk management aims at achieving minimal amounts of feed remaining by the time of the next feed delivery to
avoid removal of remaining feed to maintain bunk hygiene while animals are allowed to consume feed without restrictions
(Erickson et al., 2003; Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al., 2003). Another less frequent ad libitum feeding method involves using
self-feeders where animals are allowed unrestricted access to feed at all times and little human intervention is required
because hoppers are ﬁlled with concentrate at time intervals frequently exceeding several days. Restricted or limit feeding
systems, often called programmed feeding, aims at achieving a proportion of 0.05–0.10 lower feed intake than expected
with ad libitum consumption (Pritchard and Bruns, 2003). This method is believed to promote more consistent day-to-
day dry matter intake, facilitates feed delivery schedules, reduces feed wastage and sorting behavior, and improves feed
conversion efﬁciency (Loerch and Fluharty, 1998; Pritchard and Bruns, 2003). Finally, clean bunk management programs
attempt achieving empty feed bunks at certain times of the day and feed is therefore not available for 8–12 h/d (Erickson
et al., 2003). This management allows cattle to achieve long-term average intake that will meet or exceed that of cattle
fed ad libitum but training of animals and keeping feeding records is critical (Pritchard and Bruns, 2003). The greatest
criticism of limit feeding and clean bunk management systems, compared with the traditional, is that animals may become
meal eaters, consuming a few large meals each day, which may result in greater within day variability in ruminal ﬂuid pH
(Schwartzkopf-Genswein et  al.,  2003).

This hypothesis is supported by results from Erickson et al. (2003) who fed steers under the traditional ad libitum and
the clean bunk management program. Feed intake did not differ among treatments but steers consumed fewer (5.5 vs.
8.3 ± 0.4 meals/d) and larger meals (3.7 vs. 1.6 ± 0.4 kg/meal) at faster rate (0.30 vs. 0.18 ± 0.02/h) under clean bunk compared
with the ad libitum program. These behavioral trends were associated with greater post-prandial changes in ruminal ﬂuid pH
(1.5 vs. 1.1 ± 0.13 pH units) and daily variance (0.21 vs. 0.05 ± 0.02) in cattle fed with clean bunk compared with the traditional
ad libitum system. Similar behavioral changes were reported in beef cattle fed high-concentrate diets by Soto-Navarro et al.
(2000) and Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al. (2002). These alterations in feeding behavior are likely due to hunger and anxiety
experienced by the animals as feed is not available for variable periods of time each day. Upon feed delivery, cattle have
greater motivation for feeding, which drives them to eat larger meals at a faster rate leading to greater post-prandial drop
and within-day variation in ruminal ﬂuid pH.
4.2.3. Consistency of feeding
In addition to the aforementioned ‘planned’ periods of time of feed deprivation each day, animals could accidentally
become hungry as a consequence of mistakes in the amount of feed delivered or poor bunk management such as delays in
feed delivery. Furthermore, it is known that subordinate animals within a group have limited access to feed, particularly after
feed delivery, because socially dominant animals have priority of access (DeVries et al., 2004). As a consequence, cattle may
ingest large quantities of feed during a short time period once feed becomes available. However, experimental simulations
of these situations do not support this hypothesis. González et al. (2009) delayed the time of feed delivery (in regard to
the routine time) to some experimental animals while others in adjacent stalls received the feed as per the normal routine.
Delaying feed delivery time was not detrimental to ruminal ﬂuid pH because a stress response was triggered (increased
cortisol), which led to reduced concentrate intake, eating rate, and size of ﬁrst meal, and increased straw intake. Withholding
feed or delaying feed delivery with respect to the routine time has also been used as a model to induce ruminal acidosis
(Nagaraja and Titgemeyer, 2007). However, evidence suggests that ruminal acidosis might not be induced by short periods
of delays in feed delivery time of individually housed animals. Erickson et al. (2003) used a model of acidosis challenge
consisting of feeding 1.25 of the previous day’s intake at 4 h later than the routine feeding time. The authors did not succeed
in inducing ruminal acidosis and concluded that their intake challenge may not have been severe enough to cause acidosis
symptoms or effects on the animal. Interestingly, meal size (2.8 vs. 3.5 ± 0.5 kg/meal) and eating rate (0.17 vs. 0.32 ± 0.17/h)
were apparently reduced during the challenge compared with the pre-challenge phase, respectively, when animals were fed
under a clean bunk management program. These results are in agreement with those recently reported by González et al.
(2009). Day-to-day variations in the amount of feed offered have also been considered as a risk factor of ruminal acidosis
(Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al., 2003, 2004). However, some studies testing this hypothesis do not support this theory (Soto-
Navarro et al., 2000) or obtained contradictory results among trials (Cooper et al., 1999). It is likely that some discrepancies
are due to changes induced in feeding behavior and consequently from feeding management and feed intake.
4.3. Environmental factors affecting feeding behavior
The external environment surrounding feedlot cattle includes the physical environment, such as space or weather, and
the social environment such as group or herd mates. It has been hypothesized that changes in weather conditions are a
risk factor for ruminal acidosis because animals reduce feed intake under abnormal weather conditions (e.g. excessive heat
or storms) and the high motivation for feeding upon improvement of weather conditions may lead to aberrant feeding
behavior (Huber, 1976; Elam, 1976). High ambient temperature, low relative humidity and low wind speed were associated
with shorter time at the feed bunk but more research in this area is needed (Schwartzkopf-Genswein et al., 2003b). Data
regarding the effect of weather changes on feeding behavior concurrent with ruminal ﬂuid pH data is lacking. Therefore, this
section deals with the social environment, feeding behavior, and rumen function because a larger amount of information
has been reported in the literature.
4.3.1. Social environment: feeding space
Cattle are social animals that live in groups under natural or feral conditions and organized in social hierarchies (Reinhardt
et al., 1986). Hierarchies are established through social interactions or dominant-subordinate relationships such as physical
aggression, threats, fear and avoidance (Miller and Wood-Gush, 1991). The rank in the social hierarchy determines the priority
of access to resources, such as feed or receptive females, for which competition among group mates occurs (González et al.,
2008c). In intensive housing of commercial feedlots, feed (or feeding space) is commonly the resource leading to the greatest
competition among animals. Therefore, a great amount of aggression and exhibition of social dominance behavior is observed
in the feeding area. Competition increases as the amount of feed available becomes more limiting in which case aggression
is observed shortly after feed delivery. In addition, competition increases as feeding space becomes limiting, even with ad
libitum intake, in which case aggressions are greatest at peak feeding times such as sunset and sunrise. Competition might
be exacerbated in situations where both restricted feeding programs and limited feed bunk space converge. Competition for
feed results in shorter time spent feeding per day and increases eating rate (Harb et al., 1985; Olofsson, 1999). DeVries and
von Keyserlingk (2009b) reported that increasing competition from 1 to 2 heifers/feeding station did not affect feed intake
but tended to increase eating rate, and led to fewer and larger meals. Switching social pressure from 7.5 to 15 heifers/feeding
station, by feeding groups of 15 growing heifers in 2 or 1 feeders per pen (n = 120), resulted in a reduction of time spent
76 L.A. González et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 172 (2012) 66– 79
feeding per day (94 vs. 59 ± 2.2 min/d), while feed intake increased (10.7 vs. 11.2 ± 0.24 kg DM/d). Heifers compensated
shorter daily feeding times by eating at a 75% faster rate of a 43:57 barley grain to barley silage diet offered to allow ad
libitum consumption (120 vs. 211 ± 4.6 min/d; González and Schwartzkopf-Genswein, unpublished data). Such an increase
in eating rate would result in a reduction of feed ensalivation from around 3 to 1 mL/g DM ingested (Fig. 2) and 22 L/d
reduction in daily saliva production. In addition, competition increases the variability in feeding behavior among individuals
within a group because individual animals are affected to a greater extent and use different coping strategies (González
et al., 2008c; DeVries and von Keyserlingk, 2009b). It was also hypothesized that such ‘aberrant’ feeding patterns as a result
of competition may increase ruminal acidosis, and consequently lameness and liver abscesses (Cook et al., 2004; Nagaraja
and Chengappa, 1998).
González et al. (2008c) studied the effects of increasing social pressure with 2, 4 and 8 heifers per feeding place in the
concentrate feeder on behavior, performance and ruminal fermentation from samples obtained monthly by rumenocentesis
at 8 h after feeding (8 animals/pen; 9 pens; 7 months duration). As expected, social pressure increased competition for feed
as evidenced by over a 3-fold increase in the number of aggressive displacements from the concentrate feeders and large
modiﬁcations in feeding behavior. Competition increased the concentration of lactate in ruminal ﬂuid and the proportion
of heifers exhibiting sub-acute ruminal acidosis with ruminal ﬂuid pH below 5.6 (53, 62 and 74 ± 10% of heifers for 2, 4
and 8 heifers/place, respectively). However, pen average ruminal ﬂuid pH was lower only at the beginning of the growth
period (ﬁrst 4 months after arrival to the feedlot when animals weighed between 120 and 220 kg of body weight). A pH
lower than 5.0 in the ruminal ﬂuid, the threshold below which acute acidosis might occur, was observed in 1.8% of the 500
samples taken but there were no mortalities or veterinary treatments for acidosis-related problems. Subordinate animals
within a group usually experience the greatest alteration of feeding behavior as a result of competition such as greater eating
rate (Harb et al., 1985; Olofsson, 1999; DeVries and von Keyserlingk, 2009b). As a result, competition may have a greater
detrimental impact on digestive function of subordinate animals as they showed the greatest increase in blood haptoglobin
concentration (González et al., 2008c). However, both subordinate and dominant animals exhibited lower ruminal ﬂuid pH
regardless of social pressure. Dominant animals may show lower ruminal ﬂuid pH because their priority of access to feeders
allows them to eat the most desirable ingredients ﬁrst (concentrate in this case), and eat uninterrupted which results in
larger meals (González et al., 2008c).
Health status of the animals is another factor that has a great inﬂuence on feeding behavior and may consequently affect
rumen function (González et al., 2008d). For instance, lameness and other locomotory problems are painful and stressful
to cattle both while walking and standing. This condition leads to signiﬁcant changes in feeding behavior that may be
detrimental for rumen function. Lame animals cope with the pain caused by walking through a reduction in the number of
feeding visits per day and visits per meal (González et al., 2008d). Similarly, standing per se might also be a painful activity
in lame animals, who cope by reducing the time spent feeding per day by over 3-fold. Lame cattle compensate fewer meals
with larger meals, and shorter daily feeding time with faster eating rate (often over 3-fold faster) while daily feed intake
remains unaltered or is less affected (González et al., 2008d). Both of these changes in feeding behavior are known to be
detrimental for rumen ﬂuid pH. Therefore, feedlots or herds with high incidence of locomotory problems such as laminitis,
foot rot, and claw lesions might be more likely to experience ruminal acidosis.
5. Conclusions
Factors affecting feeding behavior should be considered in addition to diet chemical and physical composition when
attempting to reduce the problems associated with ruminal acidosis. Feed ingredients, feed additives, feeding management
(presentation and delivery), design of feeding facilities and the environment should work in synchrony to maintain rumen
function by promoting timely synchronization between acid production and neutralization/elimination. Such balance is
promoted with smaller meal size, slower eating rate, longer chewing time, and more homogeneously distribution of intake
and chewing throughout the day. Larger meals in short time periods result in greater acid production without a timely
neutralization through saliva and elimination through absorption and passage from the rumen. For example, highly fer-
mentable ingredients, which are ﬁnely ground, are eaten at a faster rate and thus are ensalivated at a lower extent and lead
to short chewing time result in lower daily saliva production. Therefore, such ingredients should be mixed with others which
result in slower eating rate and smaller meals of degradable fractions. The presentation of the ingredients could also help
achieving this by, for instance, avoiding separate ingredient feeding, excessive sorting by the animals, or feed quality to be
reduced throughout the day. Feed additives, such as monensin, could also be used to help regulate feed intake by improving
the distribution of intake throughout the day. Testing of existing and development of new additives to achieve desirable
feeding behavior should also be encouraged. Feeding programs should also be considered together with diet formulation
and those that restrict the amount or the time of feed availability should be avoided because animals become hungry. In
this sense, increasing feeding frequency and ad libitum feed delivery may help. Competition for feed and health problems
associated with locomotion (e.g. lameness) are also known to increase eating rate and reduce feeding time. Thus, sufﬁcient
feed bunk space special diets for lame animals may help reducing acidosis problems. Several factors play an important role
in the prevalence of ruminal acidosis which should be considered together in order to reduce their detrimental effects. These
include diet formulation, feeding management, design of facilities, and animal health status.
