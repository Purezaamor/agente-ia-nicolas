Summary 
Research during the last 25 yr has resulted in 
several major advancements in knowledge re- 
garding the nutritional energetics of ruminants. 
Respiration calorimetry became highly auto- 
mated. Comparative slaughter techniques were 
made less tedious and expensive by the use of 
large capacity grinders and the ability to predict 
body composition with reasonable accuracy 
from carcass density. The use of deuterium 
oxide to estimate body water and, subsequently, 
body fat and protein may become a reliable 
technique to estimate energy balance. These 
technical improvements have led to a clearer 
understanding of those factors influencing the 
efficiency of metabolizable energy (ME) utiliza- 
tion. In general terms, maintenance and lactation 
are energetically more efficient processes than 
growth. The net efficiency of ME use by ani- 
mals gaining fat and protein simultaneously is 
lower when the gained tissue contains a high 
proportion of protein. Net efficiency of protein 
deposition is lower than that of fat and lower 
than the efficiency of protein synthesis deter- 
mined from known biochemical pathways (due 
in part to the energy costs of protein turnover). 
Several factors influence the metabolizability of 
a diet and consequently the efficiency of ME 
utilization-level of intake is important and site 
of digestion may also play a role. Associative 
effects are not usual in relation to energy utili- 
zation of balanced diets but if observed may 
also be related to intake and site of digestion. 
Another major advance has been the develop- 
ment of feeding standards which more com- 
pletely consider differences in ME utilization. 
All newer feeding standards are based on net 
energy concepts although they differ in how 
the concepts are applied to practical production 
situations. Much remains to be accomplished. 
Maintenance energy requirements are large and

Introduction 
Armsby and Fries (1919) commented: "... 
not only is the utilization or 'Net' energy of a 
feeding stuff less than its fuel value, or metabo- 
lizable energy, but the two are not even approxi- 
mately proportional." Another quotation deals 
with a then-current belief (as in early publica- 
tions of Kellner) that ME values represented 
true values for maintenance. "Were such the 
case, it is evident that a feeding stuff would 
have two distinct energy values, or starch 
values. Of these, the larger, equal to its fuel 
value or metabolizable energy, would express 
its value for maintenance, while the smaller, or 
net energy value, would represent its value for 
production. Such a dualistic system would 
introduce most perplexing complications into 
all comparisons of rations ..."

During the period from the late 1920's to 
early 1950's, considerable efforts were expended 
to determine more clearly the extent and nature 
of those factors (animal and feed) that influence 
the efficiency of ME use, and to find an accurate 
means of coping with the "perplexing complica- 
tions" which had, in fact, been introduced. The 
contributions of Forbes, Brody, Kleiber, 
Marston, Mitchell, Ritzman, Benedict and 
others are not covered by this review (see 
review articles: Blaxter, 1950, 1956; Reid, 
1962 and books: Kleiber, 1961; Blaxter, 1962), 
but had considerable importance in the develop- 
ment of the modern concepts dealing with 
ruminant energy metabolism. Research by these 
investigators did not, however, have an immedi- 
ate influence on any of the existing feeding 
standards. 
It is not possible to select a single paper 
which signaled a new era of research in the field 
of energy metabolism. However, many indi- 
viduals active in this area of research would 
undoubtedly be comfortable with the suggestion 
that the First Symposium on Energy Metabolism 
sponsored by the European Association of 
Animal Production held in Copenhagen in 
September 1958 (Thorbek and Aersoe, 1958) 
was a major milestone. This symposium provided 
an opportunity for 34 participants from 12 
nations to discuss the principles and methods of 
measuring energy metabolism in farm animals. 
The ninth symposium on energy metabolism 
was held in September 1982. Proceedings of 
these international meetings are available and 
chronicle the research that has occurred over 
the last 25 yr. 
Techniques Used to 
Measure Energy Flux 
Investigational techniques employed in nu- 
tritional energetics of ruminants have classically 
been concerned with the partitioning of die- 
tary energy into fecal, urinary, methane, heat 
and recovered or product energy. These tech- 
niques remain of basic importance, but increased 
attention has been directed towards establishing 
the tissue and biochemical origin of the heat 
production. 
Proceedings of the first symposium on energy 
metabolism contained papers describing the ma- 
jor facilities and equipment which were being 
used at that time in farm animal energy metab- 
olism experiments. Most of the papers described 
respiration calorimetry equipment, largely of 
the open circuit type. These papers represent a 
primer of the principles and concerns of animal 
handling, energy balance, gas flow and gas 
composition measurements. 
Indirect respiration calorimetry continues to 
be an important energetics research tool. Major 
innovations over the last 25 yr have been 
development of automatic gas analyzers, 
principally infrared and paramagnetic, and 
improved data acquisition and processing 
systems. Such systems (Moe and Flatt, 1969; 
Thorbek, 1970; Vermorel et al., 1973; Itoh et 
al., 1974; DeKay et al., 1976; Haaland et al., 
1981) have alleviated much of the difficulty 
and tedium of measuring the heat production of 
animals. The cumbersome volumetric Haldane 
gas analysis apparatus (Van Es, 1958), infre- 
quently used for routine analysis in present day 
experiments, constitutes the basis of primary 
standardization of most of the electronic 
analyzers used for indirect calorimetry studies 
on farm animals in Europe and the US. 
Several alternatives to the dry gas meter for 
the accurate measurement of high volumes of 
air flow in indirect calorimetry systems for 
large animals have been described. These 
include the orifice differential pressure meter 
(Thorbek and Neergaard, 1970), the venturi 
flow meter (Vermorel et al., 1973) and the 
choked supersonic flow meter (Chasteau and 
Cronje, 1974). The first two of these devices 
along with acceptance of dry gas meters have 
largely replaced the inconvenient but extremely 
accurate mercury pumps (Schiemann, 1958) or 
large water pipettes (Klieber, 1958). A unique 
open-dosed respiration calorimetry system 
(Blaxter et al., 1972), which dispenses with the 
need to measure flow volume, has also been 
described. The closed chamber permits gases to 
accumulate and then periodically the entire 
chamber is automatically flushed with fresh air. 
Nonchamber methodology for individual 
animal calorimetry measurements has been 
reviewed by Brockway (1978) as part of a 
symposium on the application of human and 
animal calorimetry. Mask and hood techniques 
have and continue to be used extensively in 
special applications for shorter term measure- 
ments and tracheal cannulation techniques have

also been employed. The carbon dioxide entry

described by Corbett et al. (1971) and Engles et

cattle and sheep studies. The use of heart rate 
as an index of oxygen consumption and energy

A novel approach to determination of the 
origin and source of heat production by the 
gastrointestinal tract of ruminants has also been 
described (Webster and White, 1973). This 
technique utilizes blood flow, thermal and gas 
analyses to measure and separate heat generated 
from the intestines into anerobic and aerobic 
origins. Techniques to assess tissue energy 
metabolism from blood flow and substrate flux 
across the liver (Thompson and Bell, 1976) and 
the muscle of the hind limb (Thompson et al., 
1975) have been successfully employed. 
While heat production measurements have 
improved dramatically, the tedium and diffi- 
culty of measuring fecal and urinary losses has 
changed little during the history of large animal 
nutrition research. Direct and indirect approach- 
es to assessment of digestibility and the errors 
associated with these and other components of 
balance trials in ruminants have been described 
(Graham et al., 1958; Blaxter, 1967). Although 
research on development of indicator techniques 
continues (Thonney et al., 1979), the laborious 
conventional 7 to 10 d total collection remains 
standard. 
There has been considerable development 
and use of techniques that provide a more 
direct measure of body energy and protein 
stores in the growing animal than are provided 
by the calorimetry-balance trial approach. 
These have usually allowed experiments to be 
conducted at greater feed intakes, rates of 
performance and more normal ambiency 
compared to the livestock industry than is 
commonly obtained in the laboratory. Direct 
measures of body energy storage must be 
conducted over a significant portion of the 
growth phase of the animal to allow an accurate 
determination of changes that occur between 
the initial and final experimental groups. Tissue 
and energy composition and storage have been 
determined through physical separation and 
chemical analysis or by inference from carcass 
specific gravity or body water determined by 
isotope dilution. 
The accurate but expensive technique em- 
ploying whole body grinding and chemical 
analysis has been used extensively in energetics 
and development investigations with sheep and 
cattle, particularly by researchers at Cornell 
(Reid et al., 1955, 1968; Fortin et al., 1980). 
Others have used total body chemical analyses 
primarily as a calibration of other indices of 
body composition (Garrett and Hinman, 1969; 
Byers, 1979b). 
Measurements of carcass density or specific 
gravity have shown moderate to high statistical 
relationship to fat and energy composition 
of the empty body of animals (Garrett 1968, 
1971; Preston et al., 1974). Although there 
have been questions concerning air pockets in 
the carcass and general accuracy, particularly 
with animals of low fat content, this technique 
has and continues to be used widely in the US 
for determination of net energy requirements 
or feed values, especially in cattle experi- 
menu. 
The quest for an inexpensive nondestructive 
method of measuring the body composition 
and thus energy retentions of animals in vivo 
has continued for many yr (Ward and Johnson, 
1972). Although a number of techniques have 
been employed, most center on the determina- 
tion of empty body water by isotope dilution 
procedures. Extensive Australian data (Searle, 
1970) illustrate close relationships between 
body components or energy and tritiated water 
space. The use of D20 has also received con- 
siderable attention (Foot and Greenhalgh, 
1970; Robelin, 1977), particularly with the 
advent of increased sensitivity in analytical 
method (Byers, 1979a). Isotope dilution 
techniques have potential problems with 
variability in label exhange with other organic 
hydrogen (McManus et al., 1969), variability in 
gut water which can amount to over 30% of 
body water (ARC, 1980) and also questions 
concerning constancy of the fat free empty 
body. A method designed to separate the 
empty body water pool from that of the gut 
water contents has been proposed recently 
(Byers, 1979b) and is presently being extensively 
investigated.

Conventional measurements of dietary energy 
metabolizability are computed by subtracting 
apparent fecal, urinary and gaseous energy losses 
from the GE content. The remaining diet or feed 
energy is then expressed either as a fraction of 
the GE (frequently termed percentage ME and 
given the symbol q) or as concentration in the 
dry matter. The largest single component of 
gross energy loss in utilization of diets for 
maintenance and production by ruminants is 
fecal energy loss. Fecal losses from feedstuffs 
are most importantly related to amount and 
lignification of the fibrous portion of the feed 
as illustrated in the summative equation (Van 
Soest et al., 1966). In this relationship, fecal 
losses are predicted to be 13% (the metabolic 
fecal component) plus 2% of the nonstructural 
components plus from 10 to 90% of the struc- 
tural components depending on lignification or 
other factors. This general relationship is 
reasonable not only for a wide range of forages 
for which it was developed, but also for cereal 
grains and for many mixtures of grain and 
forage, particularly if they are fed at a low 
intake. The general relationship, for instance, 
would predict reasonably well the 60% fecal 
losses observed when feeding soybean straw 
(Gupta et al., 1978) as well as the 10 and 15% 
fecal losses observed by feeding all corn diets to 
sheep (Johnson, 1972) or to steers (Joanning et 
al., 1981). The relationship can give dissonant 
results, however, for forages under conditions 
when high rates of passage occur or when fiber 
utilization is depressed in high starch diets. 
Also, the implied 2% fecal losses of noncell wail 
components can result in a gross underestimation 
of fecal losses of minimally or unprocessed 
cereal grains in mixed grain-forage diets particu- 
larly when fed at high intakes. As much as 67% 
of the starch was voided in the feces, for 
example, when whole barley was incorporated 
into a mixed grain forage diet fed to cows 
(Morgan and Campling, 1978). 
The second major factor that impacts on fe- 
cal energy losses from feedstuffs involves an in- 
teraction of the animal to the dietary regimen- 
the rate of passage of the diet through the gas- 
trointestinal tract. Digestibility is influenced by 
rate of passage and rate of digestion. This was 
well illustrated by the experiment of Blaxter et 
al. (1956) in which decreased hay diet digesti- 
bilities were related to decreased particle size 
and increased rates of passage. Determination 
of rate of passage and rate of digestion has been 
the subject of a large number of investigations 
in the last 25 yr. Both aspects, however, are 
fraught with technical difficulties in the attempt 
to apply them to the producing animal and, to 
date, no comprehensive system applicable to 
commercial conditions exists. More progress has 
been made concerning these measurements in 
systems describing forage (Mertens and Ely, 
1982) and protein (Owens, 1982) utilization. 
Site of Digestion 
It is clear that the ruminant digestive process 
cannot be considered primarily as a ruminal 
fermentation process with minor digestion 
occurring in the gastrointestinal tract distal to 
the rumen. Information from literature surveys 
indicates that, with some mixed grain-forage 
diets fed at producing levels, 50% of the starch 
and 30% of the fiber digestion may occur distal 
to the rumen (Hoover, 1978; Johnson and 
Bergen, 1982). Considerable uncertainty resides 
in these estimates. Errors in marker ratio 
techniques can easily result in 30% over or 
under estimations in the amount of organic 
matter flowing to the abomasum (Faichney, 
1980). A second limitation is that most esti- 
mates have been made at low intakes and 
must be extrapolated up to that of the produc- 
ing animal. Estimates (table 1) of the fraction 
of organic matter digestion which occurs distal 
to the rumen in the producing animal fed 
mixed diets range from 8 to 10% for ground 
barley and flaked maize, to 15% for chopped 
hay to 35 to 45% for a coarsely ground or 
cracked corn grain. Involvement of the lower 
gut in the digestive process reflects a multitude 
of factors influencing rate of digestion and rate 
of passage. In addition to level of intake, kind 
of grain and processing of the forage or grain,

Intake Effects on Metabolizability 
In a recent synthesis (ARC, 1980) the 
metabolizability of dietary energy at any feed- 
ing level (qL) is related to the metabolizability 
of that diet fed at maintenance (qm) with a 
correction for feeding level (L is a multiple of 
maintenance) by the equation: 
qL = qm + (L -- 1) (.20 [qm -- .6231 ) 
This relationship is based on a large number of 
energy balance experiments with cattle and 
sheep in Scotland and implies that in mixed 
grain-forage feedlot or dairy cattle diets with 
ME concentrations greater than about 2.75 
kcal/g, there is little or no depression in q as 
feeding level increases. Major decreases in q 
would occur only with forage diets, particularly 
those of low quality. 
The above general synthesis of intake effects 
on q in ruminant diets is in sharp contrast to 
that projected by Tyrrell and Moe (1975) for 
dairy cattle diets and that of Reid et al. (1980) 
for ruminants in general. The latter syntheses 
predict little or no depression in digestibility 
of forage diets with increasing intake unless 
those forages are finely ground and(or) pelleted. 
Additionally, marked depressions in digestibil- 
ity and q are shown for mixed grain-forage 
diets, particularly with 60 to 75% grain, which 
are precisely the diets for which the ARC, 1980 
relationship predicts no depression to occur. 
This latter scenario is also based on a large 
number of observations with cattle and sheep 
and, together with the ARC review, indicate the 
wide range of variables that must be considered 
in an appropriate energetics scheme for the 
producing animal. 
The sharp contrast between these British and 
US intake depression syntheses cannot presently 
be resolved. The lack of depression, how- 
ever, in the mixed grain forage diets observed in 
the British data is likely a reflection of the 
observations being made largely with flaked 
maize and(or) ground barley. These constituents 
have relatively high rates of digestion (calculated 
from data of Jung and Kawashima, 1977; 
Berger et al., 1981) and are unlikely to result in 
a depression of starch digestion as reported for 
nonsteam processed corn (Joanning et al., 
1981). 
Associative Effects 
The existence of associative effects between 
feedstuffs within nutritionally balanced diets 
has been argued for much more than the 25 yr 
period of this review. The discussion continues, 
but with some hope of resolution. Many 
extensive sets of data have indicated little 
deviation from additivity between forages and 
grains. Blaxter (1974) wrote "as proportions of 
cereals to roughages change, q changes in direct 
proportion." Wainman et ai. (1976) in an 
extensive series of experiments with oats and 
barley fed in 20% increments or as single feeds 
along with either dry forage or grass silage, 
showed no associative effects in the digestibility 
or metabolizability of these feeds fed at 1.0 or 
1.5 times maintenance. Garrett (1979) did not 
find evidence for nonlinearity across a wide 
range of diets differing in metabolizable energy 
content. 
On the other hand, associative effects 
exhibited as depressed performance of animals 
fed mixtures of corn grain and corn silage have 
been reported (Peterson et al., 1973). Marked 
depressions in digestibility were noted for 
mixtures of whole corn and corn silage (Byers 
et al., 1976) and for mixtures of either immature 
or mature corn silage and cracked corn grain 
(Joanning et al., 1981). Negative associative 
effects between grains and forages, if proven to 
exist, seem most likely to occur with high 
intakes and when starch and fiber digestibilities 
are depressed. Kind and physical form of the 
grain may be involved. There is also the possi-

Feed Additive Effects on 
Digestibility and Metbanogenesis 
Considerable efforts have been expended in 
attempts to eliminate or reduce energy losses as 
methane. Extensive investigations with fatty 
acids or their analogs (Czerkawski and Brecken- 
ridge, 1975) and with halogenated methane 
analogs (Trei et al., 1971) have shown marked 
depressions in methanogenesis in vitro and in 
vivo. However, adaptations of the microbial 
populations to these inhibitors over time 
(Johnson, 1974) and for various other reasons 
this research has not led to a compound for 
use by the livestock industry. Curiously, two of 
the experiments with methane analog inhibitors 
have shown improved dry matter digestibility 
(Sawyer et al., 1971; Johnson, I974). 
Monesin addition to forage or grain substrate 
fermentations in vitro has reduced methano- 
genesis from 33 to 47% (Slyter, 1979; Fuller 
and Johnson, 1981). In vivo experiments with 
animals fed 50 to 70% concentrate diets have 
indicated that monesin decreases methanogene- 
sis from 15 to 31% below control observations 
(Joyner et al., 1979; Thornton and Owens, 
1981; Wedegaermer and Johnson, 1983). 
Forage diets, however, have resulted in con- 
siderably less (Benz, 1982) or no effects (Gar- 
rett, 1982) of monesin or methanogenesis. 
Monesin effects on diet digestibility by 
ruminants have been highly variable. Effects on 
mixed grain-forage diets have ranged from no or 
slightly negative effects (Utley et al., 1977; 
Muntifering et al., 1980; Garrett, 1982) to 
small, but statistically significant, increases in 
digestibility in the range of 2 to 3% (Joyner et 
al., 1979; Wedegaertner and Johnson, 1983). 
Effects on forage, although variable, have not 
indicated significant monesin effects on digesti- 
bility (Dinius et al., 1976; Benz, 1982; Garrett, 
1982).

To determine the partial efficiency of ME 
use or net energy values requires more than one 
level of feeding. Because the relationship be- 
tween energy intake and energy balance is 
curvilinear over the entire range of food intake, 
net energy values and partial efficiencies are not 
constants but are influenced by the intakes over 
which measurements are made. This helps 
explain some of the anomalies present when 
comparing the works of Kellner, Armsby and 
others. 
The convention that has been generally ac- 
cepted, at least for now, measures NE values for 
a productive function with both levels of 
feeding above the maintenance requirement of 
the animal. Net energy for maintenance or the 
partial efficiency of ME use for: maintenance is 
determined with the incremental change in 
feeding between fasting and maintenance. The 
acceptance of this concept is essentially the 
merging of the Armsby and Kellner procedures 
to determine net energy values. As shown in 
figure 1, the increment of feeding used by 
Armsby was below maintenance while that of 
Kellner was above.

ciencies for maintenance and productive 
functions did not become well established until 
after the second Energy Symposium, held in 
the Netherlands, in 1961. At this symposium a 
paper by Blaxter (1961) summarized work

latter paper, the abbreviations b m and bp were 
replaced by k m and kg. The curvilinear relation- 
ship between recovered energy (RE) and ME 
intake was thus approximated by two linear 
relationships-a unification, in a sense, of the 
general principles of Armsby and Kellner.

During this same period (late 1950's and 
early 1960's) research at the University of Cali- 
fornia, Davis, using a comparative slaughter pro- 
cedure with beef and sheep feeding trials (later 
described by Lofgreen, 1965), also investigated 
the net energy concept as a means to the 
establishment of more accurate feeding stand- 
ards. The publication of results by Garrett et 
al. (1959) was the first of a series (e.g., Lofgreen 
et al., 1962, 1963a; Garrett et al., 1964) which 
led to development of a practical net energy 
system for use by cattle feeders. The system 
was first introduced to the cattle industry in 
1963 (Lofgreen, 1963a,b), and was later revised 
and published (Lofgreen and Garrett, 1968). 
This system, which became known as the 
California net energy system, assigned two net 
energy values to each feed--NEm, for mainte- 
nance and NEg, for energy retention. Animal 
requirements were similarly stated. NE m and 
NEg are related to k m and kg; NEm = k m ME 
and NEg = kg ME. The California net energy 
system was the forerunner, but not the progeni- 
tor, of several new schemes (all based on net 
energy principles) for use in feeding standards.

Efficiency of ME use for 
Protein and the Fat Synthesis 
Interest in utilization of major end products 
of digestion by the ruminant has led to theo- 
retical estimates of the efficiency of ME use. 
These estimates are generally based on known 
biochemical pathways and the assumption that 
nutrients replace each other in proportion to 
their energy yield computed as phosphate bond 
energy of ATP. 
The efficiency of ME utilization for protein 
and fat synthesis according to the early theoret- 
ical estimates of Blaxter (1962) were about 90 
and 70%, respectively. Later estimates of the 
energy cost of protein and fat deposition by 
Kielanowski (1965), using comparative slaugh- 
ter and regression methods, as well as the 
theoretical estimates of synthesis by Baldwin 
(1968), appeared to confirm the possibility of a 
higher efficiency of energy deposition as 
protein than as fat. 
Synthesis of protein or fat, however, is not 
synonymous with deposition; deposition is the 
net result of synthesis and degradation. Evi- 
dence presented at the fourth Symposium on 
Energy Metabolism (Kotarbinska and Kielanow- 
ski, 1969; Thorbek, 1969) indicated that fat de- 
position may be more efficient than protein 
deposition. Subsequent work (mostly using 
regression techniques to partition heat produc- 
tion) with several species (Orskov and Mc- 
Donald, 1970; Thorbek, 1970; Rattray et al., 
1974a; Pullar and Webster, 1974, 1977; Kirch- 
gessner et al., 1976; Rattray and Jagusch, 1977; 
Graham, 1980) have confirmed that protein 
deposition in growing animals is energetically 
less efficient than fat deposition. In simple- 
stomached species the energy cost of protein 
and fat deposition is 12 to 13 kcal/g which 
corresponds to efficiencies of ME use of 45 to 
50% and 70 to 77% for protein and fat, respec- 
tively. In ruminant species the estimated cost of 
protein and fat synthesis have been more 
variable. Most estimates of the efficiency of ME 
use for protein and fat deposition in ruminants 
are within the ranges of 10 to 40% and 60 to 
80%, respectively. 
The theoretical cost of protein synthesis in 
the ruminant may be about 80% (Van Es, 
1980). The lower estimates associated with 
protein deposition obtained in whole animal 
trials are most likely the result of costs associ- 
ated with protein turnover (amino acid synthesis 
and resynthesis) as well as the transport of 
contributing nutrients and ions. The energetics 
of protein synthesis, turnover and deposition 
have recently received considerable attention 
(Cole et al., 1976; Tamminga, 1977; Buttery

and Lindsey, 1980; Byrant and Smith, 1982) 
and can be expected to improve greatly our 
understanding of whole animal energetics in the 
coming decade. It may be, however, that 
heat production now being ascribed to protein 
metabolism (by the usual regression techniques 
currently used to partition ME consumed 
above maintenance) is actually associated with 
some general increase in heat production 
(Millward et al., 1976). 
One likely source of increased heat produc- 
tion associated with energy retention or lactation 
is ion transport, particularly Na +, K+-ATPase 
activity (Milligan, 1971). Recently, Gregg and 
Milligan (1982a,b) have suggested that Na +, 
K+-ATPase accounted for a minimum of 40% 
of muscle 02 consumption while peptide bond 
synthesis was estimated to account for less than 
10% of muscle 02 consumption in cattle and 
sheep. In addition, it was found that Na +, 
K+-ATPase activity is responsive to physiolog- 
ical changes. Lactation, for example, increased 
ewe skeletal muscle 02 consumption by 32%. 
Two-thirds of this increase was to sup- 
port Na +, K+-ATPase activity. It remains 
to be determined whether these in vitro 
findings can be extrapolated to the ani- 
mal. 
The recognition by Van Es (1980) and 
Graham (1982) that energy feeding standards 
might be more effective if they contained a 
separate term (called "work of growth" by Van 
Es) may eventually be related, at least in part, 
to ion transport. This is an important area for 
increased research, which quite possibly has 
connecting links with the use of ionophores in 
cattle diets (Byers, 1980; Garrett, 1982) and 
with the continuing interest and exploration of 
the components of basal metabolism and fed 
animal heat production. 
Webster (1980) has made a significant 
contribution to delineating components of the 
heat increment of feeding. His work indicates 
the total energy costs of ingestion and digestion 
(all work done by the gut) in the ruminant are 
consistently 40 to 45% of the heat increment 
from forage feeds. Data are not yet available 
which reveal the extent to which fiberousness 
of feed increases heat increment, but Webster 
(1980) suggests muscular work expended by 
the gut in mixing and propelling indigestible 
material is trivial compared to energy costs of 
metabolic and secretory processes associated 
with digestion.

for Fetal Growth 
Energy deposition in the conceptus of sheep 
and beef cattle has been determined in several 
serial slaughter analysis experiments (sheep: 
Langlands and Sutherland, 1968; Lodge and 
Heany, 1973; Rattray et al., 1973, 1974b; 
McDonald et al., 1979; cattle: Ferrell et al., 
1976; Prior and Laster, 1979) and estimated 
from respiration calorimetry trials with sheep 
by Graham (1964b) and with dairy cows by 
Moe and Tyrell (1972). 
The efficiency of energy utilization for 
conceptus growth is generally defined as energy 
recovered in concept-us tissues divided by ME 
available or used for growth (ky) of those 
tissues. Using this definition the efficiency of 
ME use for conceptus development has been 
uniformly low with values generally ranging 
from .10 to .20. Some of the variation in these 
estimates is probably of dietary origin. Robinson 
et aI. (1980) has reviewed ky values obtained 
with sheep and suggest the evidence would 
tentatively justify the hypothesis that ME 
concentration has a relationship to ky similar to 
the accepted relationships to k m and kg. 
Robinson et al. (I980) also suggest that ME 
from diets with less than 2.4 kcal/g is used less 
efficiently than energy derived from material 
tissue loss. 
An average value for ky of .13 seems appro-

ments for pregnancy. This figure is low not be- 
cause of inefficient metabolic pathways associ- 
ated with conceptus development but because 
maintenance of the conceptus is included as an 
energy cost (Rattray et al., 1974b).

Development of Feeding Systems 
One of the goals of energy metabolism 
research with ruminants always has been the 
development of an accurate means for evaluating 
feedstuffs and stating animal requirements. 
From a practical view a feeding system must: 
1) enable the value of feeds to be appraised as 
substitutes for one antoher, 2) permit deter- 
mination of the quantity of a feed of given 
quality to support a particular management 
goal and 3) enable an estimate to be made of 
animal performance if feed intake and feed 
quality are known. 
Until the California Net Energy System 
(CNES) was proposed in 1963 (Lofgreen, 
1963a,b; Lofgreen and Garrett, 1968) feeding 
standards consisted of a single estimate of the 
energy value of feedstuffs and an expression of 
animal requirements in the same terms. The 
CNES broke with tradition and assigned two 
energy values to feedstuffs, net energy for 
maintenance (NE m) and net energy for gain 
(NEg) and listed animal requirements for 
energy in similar terms. The system is suffi- 
ciently simple to permit diet formulation and 
estimates of animal response to particular 
feeding regimens to be made by hand calcula- 
tions. It can also be adopted for manipulation 
or adjustments, usually by computer, to be 
useful in animal management models (Brokken, 
1971; Fox et al., 1982). It is basically an 
empirical system developed by using compara- 
tive slaughter feeding trials to determine the net 
energy requirements of growing ruminants in a 
manner which separates the energy needs for 
maintenance from those required for growth. 
This separation recognizes different efficiences 
of ME utilization for maintenance and for 
growth. Requirements for lactation can be 
listed in terms of NEro because the efficiency 
of ME use for lactation changes with diet in a 
manner similar, but not identical, to the effi- 
ciency of energy use for maintenance (ARC, 
1965, 1980). The requirements for fetal growth 
have also been given in NE m equivalents (NRC, 
1976) based on an efficiency of ME use for 
fetal growth and maintenance of about 13% 
(Rattray et al., 1974b; Ferrell et al., 1976). 
The Agricultural Research Council of the 
United Kingdom (ARC, 1965) described in 
detail a system for feeding standards based on 
the research of Blaxter and colleagues cited 
earlier. Although described by Moe (1981) as 
an ME system, the system is based on net 
energy principles and is very similar, concep- 
tually, the the CNES. Feed values and animal 
requirement s are listed in terms of ME, but ME 
feed values and animal requirements are adjusted 
by a series of equations to account for changes 
in ME content of feedstuffs associated with 
level of intake and for efficiency of ME use for 
various physiological functions. The proposals 
of the ARC (1965) were later modified for 
practical use (MAFF, 1975) because the 
original form required extensive calculations to 
prepare a diet or to estimate animal response 
from a known feed intake. The ARC (1980) has 
now made further modifications based on 
advances of knowledge and incorporating 
suggestions of Blaxter and Boyne (1978). The. 
ARC system has the advantage that it can be 
readily modified to accept new information 
without changing feed values because ME 
determined at the maintenance level of feed is 
the basis for the calculation of all requirements.

A system based on net energy fat (NEF) was 
introduced in Germany (DDR) for feed evalua- 
tion and for expressing nutrient needs of ani- 
mals (Nehring and Haenlein, 1973). The system 
predicts NEF of diets from the digestible 
proximate constituents plus, for ruminants, a 
correction factor based on digestibility of 
energy. NEF is equal to 1 kcal of net energy 
produced in adult animals under standard 
conditions. For practical use a "energy feed 
unit" (EF) equal to 2.5 NEF for cattle has been 
introduced. Animal requirements for various 
productive functions are stated in terms of EF. 
At least three other European countries have 
recently developed feeding systems for rumi- 
nants. General descriptions of these systems 
(Netherlands [Van Es, 1978], Switzerland 
[Bickel and Landis, 1978], and France [INRA, 
1978; Vermorel, 1978] ) are available. 
The Netherlands and Switzerland systems 
are almost identical, basically derived from 
suggestions of Van Es (1975). In Switzerland, 
energy values are stated in megajoules or 
kilojoules; in the Netherlands arbitrary feed 
units are used (VEVI, one feed-unit-growth, 
contains net energy for growth approximately 
equivalent to 1 g of barley, for example). These 
systems calculate ME from digestible proximate 
constituents and convert ME to net energy 
values and feed units using prediction equations 
which adjust for feeding level, efficiency of ME 
utilization and energy concentration of the 
diet. 
The system adopted in France is similar with 
some exceptions. ME is derived from gross 
energy, DE and a DE:ME ratio. Net energy 
values calculated from ME are expressed in feed 
units (FU) equivalent to 1 kg standard barley 
(UFL is feed unit for lactation, UFV is a feed 
unit for maintenance and meat production at 
an intake of 1.5 maintenance). Energy allowance 
for growth and fattening have been estimated 
from feeding trials conducted in France. 
Growing animals are broadly classified as slowly 
growing (breeding females, stockers) or rapidly 
growing (destined for slaughter). A net energy 
value for growth (combining NE m and NEg) is 
calculated based on an average feeding level 
(APL) for a classification. This procedure is 
similar in concept to the suggestion of Harkins 
et al. (1974). 
All feeding systems developed during the 
past 25 yr are based on net energy principles. 
Each system incorporates separate estimates for 
maintenance and productive functions. All use 
ME (or digestible proximate constituents) as 
the starting point for estimation of feed value. 
The California system lists NE m and NEg values 
for each feedstuff. Some net energy values have 
been determined but most are estimated from 
ME (Lofgreen and Garrett, 1968; Garrett, 
1980). All systems have some adjustment of 
feed values for plane of nutrition. The adjust- 
ments are by constants and equations in some 
systems and inherent in others. The California 
system, for example, measures NEg as the slope 
of the regression line between retained energy 
and dry matter intake with one level of intake 
at ad libitum. Some inherent correction for 
plane of nutrition is therefore present. All 
systems use a measured or estimated fasting 
heat production as the basis for estimating net 
energy requirements for maintenance and 
retained or recovered energy (RE) as net energy 
for a productive function. 
Systems differ in how basic net energy prin- 
ciples are applied to practical situations and in 
the methodology used to estimate RE. The 
California system is based exclusively on long 
term comparative slaughter trials. ARC and 
Continental European systems are based 
primarily on short-term respiration calorimetry 
experiments. ARC (1980) however, estimates 
RE of growing animals from a summary of 
relationships established from published and 
unpublished slaughter experiments in which 
body composition was determined by chemical 
analyses. The continental European systems 
also incorporate some feeding trial and slaughter 
analysis information. 
On the surface it may appear unfortunate 
that so many countries have adopted different 
feeding systems. Certainly basic animal biology 
and metabolic pathways are much the same in 
aU areas of the world. Dominant animal types, 
primary feed resources and consumer or eco- 
nomic pressures, however, differ considerably 
from one country to the other. Even if all 
scientists could agree on one "best" feeding 
system, local adjustments would still be required 
to translate experimentally determined energy 
units into practically useful weight units. In the 
long run it is probably beneficial that experience 
be gained by using different feeding systems in 
a variety of practical circumstances. This will 
provide a much broader base for changes and 
unifications which are certain to occur.

Dietary Energy Intake 
The ability to predict energy intake of the 
ruminant is of prime importance as attempts 
are made to maximize energetic efficiency. The 
25 yr period covered by this review followed a 
decade of research giving rise to various "static" 
theories regarding the regulation of energy 
intake, i.e., thermostatic, glucostatic and 
lipostatic. Much of this earlier work was with 
nonruminants but probably provided an impetus 
for research with ruminant species, because 
none of the "static" hypotheses were in com- 
plete harmony with what was known about 
food intake in ruminants. 
The review of Balch and Campling (1962) 
provides access to the earlier literature. Reviews 
by Jones (1972), Baile and Forbes (1974) and 
Baile and Della-Ferra (1981) consider various 
aspects of feed intake control in ruminants. 
Only a few findings which seem especially 
important with regard to whole animal ener- 
getics are summarized here. 
The relationship between dry matter digesti- 
bility and dry matter intake of lactating cows 
determined by Conrad et al. (1964) and gen- 
eralized by Montgomery and Baumgardt (1965) 
provided an important basis for increased 
understanding of food intake in ruminants. 
The general scheme suggests that physical 
factors (reticulo-rumen distension) are primary 
regulators of food intake up to some "critical 
level" of ME (or DE) density in the diet. As 
diet increases in ME density above a "critical 
level," food intake decreases but ME intake re- 
mains reasonably stable in accord with physio- 
logical demands for substrate(s). Therefore, the 
"critical level" is not constant but is adjusted 
after a time to higher or lower levels in response 
to an animal's changing requirements for 
energy. The primary regulators of short term 
food intake when the ME density of the diet is 
above the critical level may be acetate (lumen 
side of rumen) and propionate (rumen vein, 
also in luminal side of the rumen and possibly 
in the liver [Anti and Forbes, 1980; Baile and 
Forbes, 1974; Baile and Della-Ferra, 1981]). 
Glucose is not a key metabolite in the regulation 
of energy intake in ruminants (Balle and Mayer, 
1970). Less is known about long-term regulation 
of food intake in ruminants. Does an animal 
aspire to some genetically determined target 
weight, perhaps to some energy ballast, i.e., a 
particular body composition (a component of 
the lipostatic theory of Kennedy, 1953, 1961) 
or is the equilibrium condition obtained by an 
animal the result of a combination of current 
and past environmental and nutritional interac- 
tions which eventually result in energy balance? 
Much food for thought can he found in the 
paper of Louis-Sylvestre (1980) which sum- 
marizes the neuroendocrinology of hyperphagia

Future Researcb 
Several areas have been identified in which 
additional research is likely to improve our 
understanding of animal energetics. 
Supplying the energy required for mainte- 
nance is a large component of any animal 
production enterprise. 
The definition of maintenance as zero 
energy balance is straight forward, but many 
problems are associated with determining and 
partitioning the energy lost as heat among 
increment, basal metabolism and environmen- 
tally induced (broadest sense to include activity) 
heat production. The inability to assess accur- 
ately the various components of maintenance 
has been responsible for considerable ambiguity 
surrounding the maintenance concept. 
One example of this equivocal nature of 
maintenance requirements is the concept of 
"working maintenance." There is no doubt that 
increased food intake associated with produc- 
tion (growth or lactation) results in increased 
relative weights of vital organs (intestinal tract, 
liver, kidney, heart) which have heavy energy 
expenditures per unit mass. Fasting heat 
production (FHP) of animals adapted to 
different planes of nutrition has been signifi- 
cantly correlated to the weight of these meta- 
bolically active tissues (Koong et al., 1982). It 
may be a matter of accounting whether this 
increased FHP is assessed against the mainte- 
nance or the production function, but a clearer 
understanding of all components of maintenance 
is needed to adequately resolve this issue. 
Another component of maintenance require- 
ment that warrants further investigation is the 
influence of animal behavior. This can involve 
differences in actual physical activity under 
similar environmental circumstances or qualita- 
tive differences in "emotional state" of animals. 
The likely energy costs of these situations need 
to be assessed in respiration chambers and in 
more extensive lot or pasture situations. 
Many other factors have been shown to influ- 
ence maintenance metabolism (species, age, sex, 
environment, rate of maturing are examples) 
and additional research is necessary to determine 
the cause of variation and account for that 
variation in practical feeding situations. Research 
at the cellular and organ level (tissue and 
substrate turnover along with ion transportion) 
as well as whole animal studies are needed to 
increase our understanding of maintenance 
energy metabolism. It is quite likely that the 
artificial, but practically useful, separation of 
the maintenance and productive functions will 
no longer be the most satisfactory way of 
expressing animal requirements. 
The problem of scaling weight for compara- 
tive studies of energy metabolism of growth 
and efficiency also needs to be resolved. There 
may be some advantages to including mature 
size as a scaling characteristic (Taylor, 1980). 
Defining mature size, of course, may be as 
controversial as metabolic body size (wa). The 
power function 'a' could perhaps be adjusted

There is no doubt that source of ME is

of large amounts of acetate by ruminant tissues

A troublesome anomaly referred to under 
the Development of Feeding Systems section is 
the generally lower estimates for the efficiency 
of growth as measured by comparative slaughter 
compared to that estimated by respiration 
calorimetry. Research is needed to resolve 
differences between the two techniques. Geay 
(1983) has recently discussed this problem in 
some detail. 
Additional efforts should be expended to 
develop in vivo techniques to assess empty 
body composition accurately. Refinement of 
current techniques (estimation of body compo- 
sition from body water, for example) may 
prove adquate for some purposes, but cannot 
be expected to provide the information neces- 
sary for short term energy metabolism experi-

assisted data gathering and imagining techniques 
(like tomography) using X-rays, ultra-sound or

eventually permit daily assessment of body 
composition and energy balance in animals as 
they walk through or stand in an instrumented 
area. Innovations made possible by electronics 
are almost certain to appear in this and other 
areas of importance to the study of nutritional 
energetics. 
The influence of ionophores on energy 
metabolism needs further investigation. Is the 
response of ruminants to oral carboxylic 
ionophores the result of an antibiotic-like 
influence on the microbes of the gut, directly 
on the tissues of the gut and(or) at the level of 
the cell membrane of certain tissues where 
nutrient or ion transport might be influenced? 
Innovative techniques will need to be used by 
animal scientists along with usual calorimetry 
and comparative slaughter procedures to 
advance the science of animal energetics much 
beyond present limits. 
Success has been reported in the repartition-

fat in the dairy cow (Tyrrell et al., 1982)

response to exogenous injections of growth 
hormone. These findings plus those of increased 
growth rate and feed efficiency in response to 
changing photoperiod of sheep (Schanbacher 
and Crouse, 1980) and cattle (Peters and 
Tucker, 1978) all indicate controllable produc- 
tive functions. Hormonal control or hormonal 
mediated responses coupled with the rDNA

hormones or peptides hint at future possibilities 
for making dramatic changes in the composi-

particular need is an assessment of NE values at 
production intakes. This may not always be 
feasible in the general laboratory situation 
because of problems associated with high 
rates of consumption and production in the 
usual metabolism facility. Energy digestibilities 
of many mixed diets can be 12 to 15 percentage 
units lower at production levels of intake than 
they are at maintenance intakes. Sufficient 
metabolism trials should be conducted at two 
or more levels of intake to permit extrapolation 
to production levels. Also, reliable techniques 
should be developed for use in the production 
situation. Level of intake variation evokes great 
uncertainty in feed value assessment as well as 
in setting production requirements. Because 
most lactating dairy cattle and feedlot cattle in 
the US are fed high intakes it is very important 
to assess values under these circumstances. 
From a practical viewpoint the very next 
generation of feeding standards is likely to be 
similar to the present ones. However, require- 
ments for maintenance and production will be 
made more precise and additional variables will 
be incorporated to improve our ability to 
predict animal responses and feed values. 
Increased computer use for diet formulation 
and predictions of animal production will 
consider more variables and require more 
sophisticated estimates of all input and output 
relationships than presently available. Predicting
