ABSTRACT: Inﬂ ammation caused by bovine respira-
tory disease (BRD) continues to be one of the greatest 
challenges facing beef cattle producers and feedlot man-
agers. Inﬂ ammation decreases DMI, ADG, and G:F in 
feedlot calves, decreasing growth rate and increasing 
days on feed, which results in economic losses during 
the feeding period. During the past decade, marketing 
of feedlot animals has changed from selling cattle on a 
live basis to a grid-based marketing system. When cat-
tle are marketed on a live basis, the economic effects 
of BRD stop at increased health cost and decreased 
feedlot performance, carcass weight, and death loss. 
However, when cattle are marketed in a grid-based 
system, inﬂ ammation has the potential to also affect 
carcass cutability and quality. The effects of inﬂ amma-
tion on feedlot cattle in regards to performance are well 
understood; however, speciﬁ c effects on cattle growth 
and ultimately carcass merit are not as well described. 
Recent studies in feedlot cattle have indicated that the 
incidence of BRD decreases both HCW and marbling; 
however, mechanisms are not understood. Research in 
other species has demonstrated that during the acute 
phase response, pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines promote 
skeletal muscle catabolism to supply AA and energy 
substrates for immune tissues. Further, during this 
early immune response, the liver changes its metabol-
ic priorities to the production of acute phase proteins 
for use in host defense. Together these dramatic shifts 
in systemic metabolism may explain the detrimental 
effects on performance and carcass traits commonly 
associated with BRD in feedlot calves. Moreover, 
recent studies relative to human health have revealed 
complex multilevel interactions between the metabolic 
and immune systems, and highlighted inﬂ ammation as 
being a signiﬁ cant contributor to major metabolic dis-
eases. The objective of this paper is to review data to 
help explain the economical and physiological effects 
of inﬂ ammation on cattle growth and carcass merit.
Keywords: bovine respiratory disease, carcass merit, cattle growth, feedlot cattle

INTRODUCTION
Bovine respiratory disease ( BRD) is the most eco-
nomically important disease in the beef cattle industry, 
occurring primarily in high risk, recently weaned, com-
mingled cattle arriving at the feedlot (Duff and Galyean, 
2007). Bovine respiratory disease increases morbidity 
and mortality in stocker and feedlot calves resulting in 
decreased growth performance and carcass value. In 
addition, antimicrobial treatments are costly, increasing 
cost of BW gain and decreasing pro ﬁ tability. Bovine 
respiratory disease is a disease that has been prevalent 
for several decades, and data indicate that there has 
been no signi ﬁ cant decrease in BRD cases over the 
past 30 yr. From 1979 to 1994, morbidity ranged from

Available data indicate that the diagnosis of BRD, 
which is done subjectively by visual observation 
(Galyean et al., 1999), is often ineffective at identifying 
BRD cases. Supporting evidence has been developed 
by retrospectively correlating the presence of lung le-
sions in feedlot cattle at slaughter. Cattle that were never 
treated for clinical signs of BRD have been reported 
to have evidence of lung damage, and animals treated 
for BRD have been reported with no pulmonary lesions 
(Gardner et al., 1999; Thompson et al., 2006; Schneider 
et al., 2009). When de ﬁ ning BRD incidence based on

less internal fat, and decreased marbling scores than 
steers that remained healthy throughout the feeding pe-
riod (Schneider et al., 2009). Gardner et al. (1999) ob-

relation with lung lesions than treatment records. Steers 
with lung lesions and active bronchial lymph nodes at 
slaughter returned a net of $73.78 less than steers with 
no lung lesions; 21% of this loss was attributed to cost of 
treatment, and 79% was due to lighter HCW (8.4% less) 
and poorer quality grade (24.7% more USDA Standard 
carcasses). These changes in performance and carcass 
characteristics indicate that BRD may have much more 
serious economic repercussions than antimicrobial treat-
ment costs and death loss alone. The combination of 
treatment, death loss, and decreased G:F have been esti-
mated to cost the industry $800 to 900 million annually 
(Chirase and Greene, 2001).
BOVINE RESPIRATORY DISEASE
What is Bovine Respiratory Disease?
Bovine respiratory disease is a multifaceted disease 
that can involve both viral and bacterial pathogens after 
cattle are exposed to a variety of risk factors (Duff and 
Galyean, 2007). As the name implies, BRD is a disease 
that affects the respiratory tract of animals. The viral 
infectious agents believed to cause BRD include infec-
tious bovine rhinotracheitis, bovine viral diarrhea virus 
(BVDV), and bovine respiratory syncitial virus ( BRSV; 
Duff and Galyean, 2007). Bacterial pathogens include 
Mannheimia haemolytica  (formerly P asteurella haemo-
lytica), Pasteurella multocida , Histophilus somni , and 
Mycoplasma bovis. Mannheimia haemolytica is consid-
ered to be the most important bacteria responsible for 
clinical and pathological signs of BRD in the United 
States (Whitley et al., 1992). When combined with the 
stress of weaning and marketing, these pathogens can 
lead to a greater incidence of BRD (Blecha et al., 1984). 
Pathogen exposure often occurs at auction markets and 
during transport where cattle are commingled (Duff and 
Galyean, 2007). The pathogens involved in the BRD 
complex work in a synergistic fashion with detrimental 
results in the host animal. It is hypothesized that poor 
management or environmental stress factors and viral in-
fection alter the upper respiratory tract epithelium allow-
ing M. haemolytica to colonize.  Mannheimia haemolyt-
ica possesses several virulence factors, including endo-
toxin (lipopolysaccharide; LPS) and leukotoxin, which 
enable it to adhere to and colonize respiratory mucosal 
surfaces, evade host defense mechanisms, and disrupt 
pulmonary structure and function (La ﬂ eur et al., 2001; 
Jeyaseelan et al., 2002).
Recent research regarding viral pathogens involved 
in ﬁ eld cases of BRD has focused on BVDV (Loneragan 
et al., 2005; Elam, et al., 2008; Hessman, 2009). Of pri-
mary concern are calves persistently infected ( PI) with 
BVDV (PI-BVDV). When cows are exposed to BVDV 
during d 45 to 120 of pregnancy, the calves incorporate 
the BVDV into body tissues, and their immune systems 
do not recognize the virus as a pathogen. Calves that are 
PI-BVDV cannot be distinguished from calves not PI-
BVDV by visual or clinical observation, and PI-BVDV 
calves shed the virus their entire lives. Healthy, non-ex-
posed animals will develop signs of disease and build an 
immune response to the virus when exposed to PI-BVDV 
animals. The prevalence of PI cattle arriving in commer-
cial feedlots has been reported to be 0.3%, and the prev-
alence of PI-BVDV is increased in calves that became 
chronically ill (2.6%) or died (2.5%; Loneragan et al., 
2005). In addition, the presence of a PI-BVDV animal 
in a pen resulted in 43% greater risk of initial treatment 
for BRD (Loneragan et al., 2005). However, the effect 
of BVDV on ultimate health and performance of groups 
of feedlot cattle is con ﬂ icting. Hessman et al. (2009) 
reported losses of $5.26/animal due to fatalities and 
$88.26/animal due to lost performance in pens of cattle 
that had direct exposure to PI-BVDV animals compared

Effects of Bovine Respiratory Disease on Feedlot 
Cattle Performance
The effects of BRD on performance of feedlot cat-
tle were investigated by several groups, both during the 
time when the disease process is occurring and the ef-
fects of disease on subsequent performance and carcass 
characteristics. Feed intake by lightweight stressed 
calves averaged only 1.5% of BW during the ﬁ rst 2 wk 
after arrival (Hutcheson and Cole, 1986; Galyean and 
Hubbert, 1995). In a summary of 18 experiments in-
volving transit-stressed calves, only 83.4% of morbid 
calves and 94.6% of healthy calves had consumed any 
feed by d 7 (Hutcheson and Cole, 1986). In addition, 
measured DMI of morbid calves was 58, 68, and 88% 
of that DMI for healthy calves across d 1 to 7, 1 to 14, 
and 1 to 56, respectively.
Using a radiofrequency monitoring system, Sowell 
et al. (1999) recorded the frequency and timing of visits 
made to the feed bunk by newly received calves during 
receiving and growing periods. In Exp. 1, 94% of calves 
identiﬁ ed as healthy and 87% of morbid calves visited 
the feed bunk on the d of arrival, but 100% of healthy 
calves and only 91% of morbid calves had visited the 
bunk by d 3. However, in Exp. 2, only 13 and 10% of 
healthy and morbid calves, respectively, visited the feed 
bunk on d 1. Again, all healthy calves had visited the 
bunk by d 4, but only 76% of morbid calves were ob-
served at the feed bunk. In both experiments, healthy 
calves had more overall feeding bouts per day and spent 
more time at the bunk daily than morbid animals, both 
over the ﬁ rst 4 d and throughout the 32-d experiment. 
In Exp. 1, 52% of calves were identiﬁ ed as morbid, and 
82% were classiﬁ ed as morbid in Exp. 2. In both experi-
ments, a similar 80% of morbid calves were identi ﬁ ed 
within 10 d of arrival.
Using similar technology, Buhman et al. (2000) not-
ed no difference in the frequency or duration of visits 
to the feed bunk between healthy and morbid calves for 
d 1 to 3, 4 to 5, or 6 to 10 after arrival. From d 11 to 27, 
sick animals had approximately 3 fewer visits and spent 
nearly 20 fewer minutes at the bunk daily than healthy 
animals. However, from d 28 to 57 of the growing pe-
riod, animals that had been identi ﬁ ed as morbid visited 
the bunk nearly 1 additional time daily than did healthy 
animals. Notable are the days on feed when BRD was 
identiﬁ ed. Of the 170 calves enrolled in 2 studies, 43 
were treated. Twenty-seven calves were treated for BRD 
by d 11, 12 were treated between d 11 and 27, and 4 were 
treated between d 28 and 56. The decrease in number of 
feed bunk visits and time spent at the bunk occurred at 
the time when BRD cases were most prevalent.
Longer-term studies examining the effect that BRD 
has on feed intake after recovery are limited. Jim et 
al. (1993) sorted calves by elevated rectal temperature, 
classifying them as sick or healthy. Sick calves were re-
quired to have an increased rectal temperature (i.e., ≥ 
40.5°C) both 48 and 72 h after processing. Dry matter 
intake could not be measured during the ﬁ rst 48 h af-
ter processing because all animals were comingled from 
processing on d − 3 to d 0 when sick animals were de-
termined and pens were allocated. During that time, sick 
animals lost 0.5 kg/d, whereas healthy calves gained 
1.53 kg/d. However, during the next 27 d, sick animals 
gained 11% more than healthy animals while eating 0.3 
kg/d less DM. Overall, d 0 to slaughter, ADG and DMI 
were not different between sick and healthy cattle, but 
G:F was 3.15% greater for animals identi ﬁ ed as sick 
upon arrival. However, when DMI and G:F were ex-
trapolated from processing to d 0, no differences were 
observed. The improvement in efﬁ ciency likely resulted 
from shrink occurring in the sick animals between pro-
cessing and allocation when they were likely not con-
suming feed. The subsequent compensatory response in 
early BW gain and efﬁ ciency was likely due to regaining 
lost gut ﬁ ll. In their experiment an additional 6.6 and 
4.7% morbidity was observed in sick and healthy calves, 
respectively, after allocation. Therefore, the differences 
in ﬁ nishing DMI and G:F could be biased by the timing 
of subsequent morbidity.
McBeth et al. (2001) segregated heifers by the num-
ber of BRD treatments (i.e., 0 or 1) administered during 
a 42-d preconditioning period and observed subsequent 
ﬁ nishing performance. At the beginning of the ﬁ nishing 
phase, no difference in BW between healthy and morbid 
steers existed. However, ADG was increased 14.4 and 
5.8% for treated heifers during d 0 to 28 and d 0 to 112, 
respectively. While DMI was not different at any time 
during the 140-d ﬁ nishing period, the increase in ADG 
made treated heifers more ef ﬁ cient during the ﬁ rst 28

slightly decreased in challenged steers compared with

The majority of published reports regarding BRD 
and animal performance have been conducted by retro-
spectively correlating treatment records with BW and 
ADG. In general, incidence of BRD during ﬁ nishing 
results in decreased ADG and ﬁ nal BW (Gardner et al., 
1999; Thompson et al., 2006; Schneider et al., 2009). 
Generally, within studies, initial BW has not been cor-
related with subsequent BRD incidence (Gardner et al., 
1999; Waggoner et al., 2007) or the success of antimi-
crobial treatment (relapse rate; Bateman et al., 1990). 
However, Montgomery et al. (2009) reported a linear 
decrease in arrival BW for heifers treated 0 to 3 times. 
A portion of the lost ADG observed in experiments sur-
veying cattle in commercial feedlots may be due to man-
agement of the morbid animals. Gardner et al. (1999) 
noted that the sick animals in their experiment were 
removed to hospital pens during treatment where they 
were fed diets that contained more roughage, and were, 
therefore, less energy dense. Decreased performance by 
calves moved to hospital pens may be due to decreased 
energy intake while in hospital pens and the necessity to 
re-adapt to high-energy diets and competition for bunk 
space when returned to their home pen.
There is evidence that upon recovery, morbid ani-
mals experience compensatory BW gain compared with 
non-treated animals. This compensation may be due to 
recovering gastrointestinal ﬁ ll or reduced competition 
for nutrients when cattle are moved from preconditioning 
pens to pasture (Montgomery et al., 2009). Thompson 
et al. (2006) reported decreased ADG by morbid cattle 
from d 0 to 35 in South African feedlots. However, no 
difference in ADG was observed throughout the remain-
der of the feeding period. The ﬁ rst 35 d on feed was also 
the period in which 87% of all BRD treatments occurred. 
In a large survey conducted in a commercial feedlot 
over multiple classes of cattle, lighter cattle generally 
had greater overall incidence of BRD, and tended to be 
treated more times in the feedlot (Babcock et al., 2009). 
Morbidity rate was 17.3% and mortality rate was 0.80% 
across 37,078 cattle. Cattle in this dataset ranged in BW 
from 136 kg to greater than 409 kg. Approximately 49% 
of cattle received a ﬁ rst treatment within 4 wk of arrival 
date, and 74% of initial treatments occurred within the 
ﬁ rst 42 d. Moreover, the majority of the cattle received 
an initial treatment 28 to 24 wk from slaughter (Babcock 
et al., 2009). These data give a good indication that a 
signiﬁ cant portion of cattle become infected with BRD 
within the ﬁ rst 6 wk of the feeding period. However, 
lighter cattle with arrival BW between 227 and 272 kg 
had fewer effects from BRD on performance and carcass 
merit during subsequent ﬁ nishing than heavier animals 
(Babcock et al., 2009). This was likely because more 
days on feed between treatment and slaughter allowed 
calves with lighter initial BW more time to recover and 
compensate for lost BW gain that occurred during mor-
bid episodes.
Holland et al. (2010) examined the effects of BRD 
during a 63-d preconditioning period on subsequent ﬁ n-
ishing performance. For 337 heifers placed on the experi-
ment, 33.5% were never treated, 29.7% were treated once, 
10.1% were treated twice, 11.6% were treated three times, 
3.9% were deemed chronic, and 8.0% died. During the 
preconditioning period, there was a linear decrease in 
ADG as the number of antimicrobial treatments increased. 
However, across the ﬁ nishing phase, there was no differ-
ence in ADG across treatments. Therefore, from arrival to 
the end of the feeding period there was a linear decrease 
in ADG as number of times treated for BRD increased. 
During the ﬁ nishing phase, DMI decreased as number of 
times treated during the preconditioning period increased,
 
but this difference was not apparent when DMI was ex-
pressed as a percentage of BW. Ef ﬁ ciency of BW gain 
was increased across the entire feeding period as the num-
ber of treatments for BRD increased. Therefore, when 
fed to the same compositional endpoint, cattle treated for 
BRD were able to compensate for lost performance dur-
ing the preconditioning period, although BW of chronic 
calves did not return to the average BW of heifers from 
the other treatments.
From the studies reviewed, it is clear that there is 
a substantial decrease in performance by calves diag-
nosed with BRD. Dry matter intake, ADG, and G:F are 
decreased during the early feeding period when the ma-
jority of disease occurs. This decreased ADG often car-
ries through the entire feeding period when cattle are 
harvested at the same number of days on feed. However, 
similar ﬁ nal BW and ADG may occur if days on feed 
are increased with increasing number of antimicrobial 
treatments to reach the same compositional endpoint. 
Decreased performance due to BRD can also have a 
negative impact on carcass quality and retail yield.
Effects of Bovine Respiratory Disease on 
Carcass Characteristics
Parallel to the observed differences in feedlot cattle 
performance response, BRD in cattle is related to lighter 
and leaner carcasses (Larson, 2005). Garcia et al. (2010) 
evaluated the effects of BRD on carcass composition 
and meat quality traits on 2 separate herds at the US

Meat Animal Research Center, Clay Center, NE. Cattle 
showing signs of BRD experienced a 5 kg decrease in 
HCW, and a 10 to 15% decrease in 12th-rib fat thickness 
resulting in a 5 to 7% decrease in USDA Yield Grade. 
These authors also evaluated the effects of overall patho-
genic disease on carcass traits. If an animal experienced 
any disease event, 12 th-rib fat thickness was decreased 
12 to 14%, and USDA Yield Grade was decreased by 
0.2 units. Similarly, Schneider et al. (2009) reported a 
2.5% decrease in HCW, a slight decrease in LM area, a 
7.0% decrease in 12 th-rib fat thickness, and a 2.5% de-
crease in marbling score in treated vs. non-treated cattle. 
Gardner et al. (1999) examined the effects of the num-
ber of times treated on carcass characteristics. Hot car-
cass weight was decreased 4.1%, 12 th-rib fat thickness 
was decreased 26.5%, and KPH and USDA Yield Grade 
were also decreased in treated vs. non-treated calves. 
Similarly, HCW was decreased by 4.7%, 12 th-rib fat 
thickness by 43.4%, and KPH and USDA Yield Grade by 
21.1 and 18.2%, respectively, when cattle were treated 
more than once compared with cattle only treated once 
(Gardner et al., 1999). When morbidity was de ﬁ ned by 
the presence of lung lesions at slaughter, Gardner et al. 
(1999) observed morbid steers had HCW that were 94% 
of steers with no evidence of prior disease. Additionally, 
when active bronchial lymph nodes were present, indi-
cating an ongoing disease process, HCW were less than 
animals with no lesions. In general, evidence of prior 
morbidity was associated with decreased dressing per-
centage, internal fat, external fat, smaller LM area, and 
decreased marbling scores. Similar results were noticed 
when carcass characteristics were examined based on 
BRD treatment records. Cattle that received more than 
1 treatment for BRD had an additional 5% decrease in 
HCW compared with those that were treated only 1 time 
(Gardner et al., 1999).
Roeber et al. (2001) reported a 3% decline in HCW 
in steers treated more than 1 time for BRD compared 
with those treated 1 time. However, in their report, no 
differences in HCW were observed between cattle never 
treated and those treated 1 time, and no differences in 
HCW across number of BRD treatments were reported 
by Waggoner et al. (2007). Although fewer differences 
in carcass weights and no differences in LM area were 
reported by Roeber et al. (2001), measures of carcass 
fatness were affected by BRD incidence. While internal 
fat was not different among treatment groups, 12 th-rib 
fat thickness, calculated yield grade, and marbling score 
were all greatest for cattle never treated, intermediate for 
those treated once, and least after multiple treatments. 
As a result of differences in marbling, the distribution of 
USDA Quality Grades has been shown to be affected by 
BRD. Gardner et al. (1999) observed that treated steers, 
or steers with lung lesions, had a greater percentage 
of USDA Standard carcasses at the expense of Choice 
and Select compared with healthy animals. Similarly, 
McNeill et al. (1996), in an evaluation of over 7,000 cat-
tle, reported 39% of cattle never treated for BRD graded 
USDA Choice or better, but only 27% that were treated 
graded USDA Choice or better.
Differences observed in marbling scores and quality 
grades have generally not translated into differences in 
meat tenderness or palatability when measured. Gardner 
et al. (1999) measured decreased Warner-Bratzler shear 
force (WBSF) of LM from steers with lung lesions pres-
ent compared with those with no lung lesions after ag-
ing 7 d. However, when steaks were aged 14 or 21 d, 
no differences in WBSF were observed. Clinical treat-
ment records were not associated with any differences in 
WBSF and no differences in the distribution of tender or 
tough steaks were observed based on treatment records 
or lung lesions. Roeber et al. (2001) also observed no 
difference related to treatment records in WBSF, ten-
derness, or juiciness when steaks were cooked to two 
different degrees of doneness. Warner-Bratzler shear 
force and palatability measurements were conducted 
after 14-d aging. Similarly, Snowder et al. (2007) did 
not observe a signi ﬁ cant correlation between BRD 
and WBSF, or tenderness, juiciness, and ﬂ avor scores. 
However, no differences in marbling were observed in 
those cattle. They suggested that, based on a moderate 
genetic correlation between BRD and WBSF, selection 
for resistance to BRD might have an undesirable effect 
on WBSF. Holland et al. (2010) noted a trend for a lin-
ear decrease in marbling score when cattle were fed to 
a targeted 12th-rib fat thickness of 1.27 cm. There were 
no differences in proportions of USDA Yield Grades or 
Quality Grades; however, meat color score and overall 
appearance of strip loin steaks decreased as the number 
of times treated for BRD increased. There was no effect 
of number of BRD treatments on WBSF, but there was 
a trend for an increased amount of connective tissue de-
termined by trained sensory panelists as the number of 
treatments increased.
It is apparent that BRD has negative effects on 
carcass characteristics. Data show that fat deposition 
by cattle is the most directly affected; however, de-
creased growth and anorexia also occurs as evidenced 
by decreased HCW. It may be possible to achieve simi-
lar HCW, although animals would need to be sorted by 
number of times treated for BRD and fed for additional 
days (Holland et al., 2010). Other than in chronically in-
fected cattle, this would indicate that BRD constraints on 
growth are relieved with time. Thus, pro ﬁ tability could 
be increased if treatments minimized the time required 
to produce an acceptable carcass in BRD-infected cattle.

ACUTE PHASE IMMUNE RESPONSE
Although cattle must be treated multiple times 
for BRD before exhibiting decreased carcass quality, 
it would seem likely that acute responses to infection 
initiate physiological changes ultimately leading to de-
creased growth. After breach of host defenses by viral 
and bacterial pathogens, trauma, or stress, the innate im-
mune system initiates a rapid and potentially systemic 
acute inﬂ ammatory reaction. This acute phase response 
(APR) usually occurs within the ﬁ rst 48 h of infection 
or injury. Pathogen associated molecular patterns are de-
tected by toll like receptors which stimulate the produc-
tion of proinﬂ ammatory cytokines [e.g., tumor-necrosis 
factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6] by phagocytic im-
mune tissues and leads to APR, fever, anorexia, muscle 
catabolism, coagulation, increased glucocorticoid hor-
mones, changes in liver protein synthesis, and leukocy-
tosis (Gruys et al., 2005; Cray et al., 2009; Eckersall and 
Bell, 2010). Whereas such a response is invaluable to 
the health of the animal, it can have severe growth con-
sequences. If the systemic immune response cannot be 
regulated and becomes chronic, it can be detrimental to 
the animal by increasing morbidity and mortality, organ 
failure, and risk of sepsis (De Maio et al., 1998; Selzman 
et al., 1998).
The APR provides an early non-speci ﬁ c defense 
against pathogen challenge through a dynamic process 
that involves both systemic and metabolic changes in the 
body (Peterson, 2004). Acute phase proteins ( APP) are 
produced in the liver as part of an early defense mecha-
nism in response to cellular injury (Eckersall and Conner, 
1988). This injury can be a result of infection, in ﬂ am-
mation, or advanced malignancies (Saini et al., 1998). 
Acute phase protein production is initiated by IL-1, IL-
6, and TNF-α  (Baumann and Gauldie, 1994). Recently, 
Cooke and Bohnert (2011) demonstrated that increased 
circulating concentrations of cortisol in response to 
corticotrophin-releasing hormone infusion resulted in 
increased concentrations of both IL-6 and haptoglobin 
(Hp), indicating that the APR is activated by systemic 
increases in cortisol. As monocytes, macrophages, and 
neutrophils initially react to pathogens, these cytokines 
are produced, signaling multiple changes. Interleukin-1, 
IL-6, and TNF-α signal local production of low molecu-
lar weight mediators, such as PG, which induce dilation 
of local vasculature and blood leaking causing the red-
ness and edema associated with infection. Systemically, 
they can induce the production of PGE 2, which acts on 
the preoptic area of the hypothalamus to induce and 
regulate the febrile response. As a component of the 
APR, the liver alters metabolism to increase or decrease 
the production of APP. Among the proteins whose pro-
duction is increased are α-1-acid glycoprotein ( α-GP), 
C-reactive protein, ﬁ brinogen, Hp, and serum amyloid-A 
(SAA), whereas albumin and transferrin are decreased.
Multiple APP have been measured in cattle with the 
intended application of providing an alternative method 
for monitoring animal health, including BRD (Peterson, 
2004). Horadagoda et al. (1999) measured α-GP, Hp, 
and SAA in 81 cattle that were diagnosed with acute 
or chronic in ﬂ ammation, each from a number of dif-
ferent causes, and concluded that SAA and Hp would 
be the most valuable for discrimination between acute 
and chronic in ﬂ ammation. Research in this area rela-
tive to feedlot cattle has focused on serum Hp and SAA 
concentrations (Wittum et al., 1996; Carter et al., 2002; 
Burciaga-Robles et al., 2010b). Serum Hp has been 
shown to increase in response to experimental infection 
with M. haemolytica  (Conner et al., 1989; Burciaga-
Robles et al., 2010b) or BVDV (Gånheim et al., 2003). 
Burciaga-Robles et al. (2010b) reported that exposing 
naïve calves to PI-BVDV calves did not cause an in-
crease in serum Hp concentration. In that study, intra-
tracheal challenge with M. haemolytica  resulted in el-
evated serum Hp concentration, peaking 18 h after chal-
lenge and remaining greater than control animals for 96 
h. Animals challenged with both BVDV and M. haemo-
lytica had increased concentrations of pro-inﬂ ammatory 
cytokines, especially IL-1 and TNF- α, compared with 
non-challenged controls and both BVDV and M. haemo-
lytica challenge alone.
As the name of this immunological response would 
indicate, the APR is typically thought to be relatively 
short acting. Thus, it is unclear how an acute response 
can negatively impact long-term growth and carcass 
characteristics. It should be noted, however, that Berry 
et al. (2004) observed sustained increased Hp concentra-
tions in calves requiring multiple treatments for BRD. 
Haptoglobin concentrations remained increased for 28 
d indicating calves receiving multiple treatments also 
experience a sustained APR. In contrast, Burciaga-
Robles et al. (2010b) showed a relatively short APR in 
a BRD model. Nonetheless, there is limited information 
regarding APP levels or the APR throughout multiple 
treatments for naturally acquired BRD and subsequent 
harvest. Because the APR can be stimulated by cortisol 
(Cooke and Bohnert, 2011), it might be dif ﬁ cult to dis-
tinguish between increased concentrations of APP in re-
sponse to animal handling vs. increased concentrations 
due to illness. It is clear that increased APP are associ-
ated with decreased ADG (Qui et al., 2007; Cooke et 
al., 2009), and the APR and in ﬂ ammatory responses, re-
gardless of initiator, likely alter metabolic function in a 
variety of tissues. Future studies are needed to determine 
how long the APR is sustained in calves that naturally

Hepatic Protein Metabolism
The hepatocytes of the liver are responsible for the 
production of APP. These can be classiﬁ ed into 2 groups; 
the positive APP are those that are stimulated and the 
negative APP are those that are inhibited during the APR 
(Gruys et al., 2005; Cray et al., 2009). The APP are in-
duced by pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines and can be further 
categorized into Type I and II depending on the stimulus. 
Type I APP are stimulated by the IL-1 like cytokines (i.e., 
TNF-α, IL-1α, and IL-1β) and include Hp and α-GP. Type 
II APP, such as α 2-microglobulin, ﬁ brinogen, and SAA, 
respond to IL-6-like cytokines (i.e., IL-6 and IL-11; 
Jeschke and Herndon, 2004). While there is a plethora of 
APP identiﬁ ed in other species, ruminants, particularly 
cattle, are unique from other species in that Hp and SAA 
are the major APP observed to increase during infections 
(Cray et al., 2009; Eckersall and Bell, 2010). Changes 
in Hp concentrations have been seen in several bacte-
rial and viral infections, including M. haemolytica and P.  
multocida, BVDV, and BRSV, all of which are common 
BRD pathogens (Peterson et al., 2004; Eckersall and 
Bell, 2010). In addition, SAA is a second deﬁ nitive posi-
tive APP in cattle and has been shown to be increased 
during acute inﬂ ammation and BRD (Horadagoda et al., 
1999; Nikunen et al., 2007). It appears that IL-1β works 
as a regulator of APP production by hepatocytes in re-
sponse to IL-6 stimulation by dictating which APP are 
up- or down-regulated (Bode et al., 2011), although this 
phenomenon has not been well characterized in domes-
tic ruminants.
The picture that emerges from these studies is that 
signiﬁ cant changes in protein synthesis occur in the liver, 
which likely modiﬁ es AA requirements for the host ani-
mal. Waggoner et al. (2009) experimentally challenged 
beef steers with endotoxin, and plasma concentrations 
of isoleucine and leucine decreased 4 h post-infusion 
with LPS when compared with unchallenged controls 
(Waggoner et al., 2009). Similarly, clearance kinetics 
in human subjects intravenously perfused with AA in-
dicated greater removal of several AA in patients with 
microbial sepsis when compared with healthy controls, 
presumably as a result of hepatic extraction (Druml et 
al., 2001). Notably, branched chain and gluconeogenic 
AA clearance was 97% greater in septic patients than 
in control subjects (Druml et al., 2001). Low amounts 
of branched-chain AA transferase in the liver make me-
tabolism of branched-chain AA ( BCAA) to their keto-
acids negligible because perfused liver does not read-
ily metabolize BCAA (Smith and Elia, 1983). Little is 
known of hepatic metabolism of BCAA during acute 
inﬂ ammation; however, it may be that these AA are in-
corporated into nascent proteins or involved in signal-
ing protein translation (Kimball and Jefferson, 2006). 
Interestingly, Reeds et al. (1994) compared the AA con-
tent of major APP (C-reactive protein, ﬁ brinogen, α-GP, 
α1-antitrypsin, SAA, and Hp) and muscle protein and 
observed a signiﬁ cant disparity between the two. They 
calculated that the demand for the aromatic AA (i.e., 
phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan) would lead to 
more than twice the amount of AA in skeletal muscle 
needed to be mobilized to accommodate APP synthesis. 
This need for speciﬁ c AA indicates the detriment that the 
APR may have on muscle in beef cattle with BRD.
HEPATIC ENERGY METABOLISM
Carbohydrates. The hypermetabolic state that oc-
curs during acute phase of illness also results in altera-
tions in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism (Chioléro et 
al., 1997). Lipopolysaccharide depletes carbohydrate 
stores in animals. In cattle infused with LPS or recombi-
nant bovine TNF-α (rbTNF), there was an initial hyper-
glycemic state as early as 30 min post-infusion with hy-
poglycemia occurring at 3 h and sustaining for 6 to 10 h 
(Werling et al., 1996; Kushibiki et al., 2000). In addition, 
Montgomery et al. (2009) observed that heifers treated 
for apparent BRD had decreased glucose concentrations 
compared with those that were not treated. In rodent 
models using endotoxin challenge, researchers demon-
strated that LPS leads to hypoglycemia and hepatic gly-
cogen depletion in fasted rodents (McCallum and Berry, 
1973; Filkins and Cornell, 1974). Furthermore, using 
radioactively labeled substrates (i.e., alanine, pyruvate, 
and glucose), these rodent models established that LPS 
led to decreased gluconeogenesis, glycogenesis, and gly-
coneogenesis in vivo. Similarly, in vitro enzyme activity 
and kinetic experiments indicate that endotoxemia de-
creased activity and substrate ﬂ ux of phosphoenolpyru-
vate carboxykinase (PEPCK), the rate limiting enzyme 
in gluconeogenesis; glycogen synthase, the primary 
enzyme in glycogenesis; and pyruvate kinase ( PK), an 
important enzyme in glyconeogenesis (McCallum and 
Berry, 1973; Jones and Titheradge, 1993).
Decrease in enzyme activities is potentiated by the 
pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines. Tumor necrosis factor- α is 
a transcriptional regulator of PEPCK gene expression in 
endotoxemic rodents and is capable of repressing gluco-
corticoid-, glucagon-, and cAMP-mediated induction of 
this gene (Hill and McCallum, 1991; 1992; Waltner-Law 
et al., 2000). In transition dairy cattle administered daily 
small doses of TNF-α  for 7 d, glucose-6-phosphatase 
measured in liver tissue decreased 50%. Additionally, 
PEPCK expression tended to be suppressed by this in-
ﬂ ammatory cytokine, supporting that gluconeogenesis 
suppression in cattle is similar to that in other species

(Bradford et al., 2009). However, these results are equivo-

in mid-lactation tended to exhibit increased conversion of

istered in vivo. This ﬁ nding led the authors to conclude

concentrations observed in the animals.
Other cytokines involved in the APR have been im-
plicated in these changes in carbohydrate metabolism. 
Interleukin-6 mitigated the mRNA transcription, abun-
dance, and enzyme activities of PEPCK and glucokinase 
by glucagon and insulin, respectively, in cultured rat he-
patocytes (Christ et al., 1994; 1997). In a similar study, 
IL-1β and TNF-α both virtually abrogated the glucagon 
induction of PEPCK transcription and activity, which re-
sulted in decreased glucose formation from radiolabeled 
lactate (Christ and Nath, 1996). These effects on PEPCK 
appear to be potentiated by posttranscriptional degrada-
tion of mRNA transcripts and decreased translation of 
these genes (Christ et al., 1994; Christ and Nath, 1996).
Furthermore, in ﬂ ammatory cytokines are known 
to alter glycogen metabolism. In rat hepatocytes stimu-
lated with insulin, IL-6 and IL-1 β lessened the increase 
of glycogen deposition (Lee et al., 1993; Kanemaki et 
al., 1998). These cytokines inhibited activity of gly-
cogen synthase and stimulated glycogen phosphory-
lase activity, thereby attenuating the effects of insulin 
on these 2 enzymes (Lee et al., 1993; Kanemaki et al., 
1998). Kanemaki et al. (1998) demonstrated that IL-6 
was very effective, as it decreased glycogen increase by 
30% within 1 h and nearly abolished its increase within 
4 h of cytokine treatment, whereas IL-1 β showed no 
signiﬁ cant effects until 4 h.
Research has established that in ﬂ ammatory condi-
tions, such as sepsis, can increase whole body resting 
energy expenditure (Chioléro et al., 1997). Furthermore, 
pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines alter transcription, trans-
lation, and activity of metabolic enzymes involved in 
glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glyconeogenesis, glyco-
genesis, and glycogenolysis. The overall picture is that 
de novo glucose production and utilization is inhibited; 
similarly, glycogen synthesis is inhibited while its deg-
radation is promoted. This shift in carbohydrate mobili-
zation supports the theory that hepatic tissues diminish 
carbohydrate utilization to meet the energy demands of 
peripheral tissues.  With markedly increased concentra-
tions of proinﬂ ammatory cytokines in cattle with BRD 
during the APR (Burciaga-Robles, 2010b), one can pre-
sume that such an increase would greatly alter expres-
sion of metabolic genes in cattle. However, there is no 
known literature that addresses the effects of BRD on 
speciﬁ c genes in carbohydrate metabolism in liver of 
cattle. Additional research would be of interest given 
the important differences in carbohydrate metabolism of 
ruminants compared with monogastric species.
Lipids. Increased lipid metabolism is a common 
characteristic in pyogenic bacterial infections, particu-
larly those that induce sepsis. These infections increase 
fatty acid oxidation, particularly by peripheral tissues, 
and decrease glucose usage (Stoner, 1987). In lactating 
cows, LPS administration lead to an increase in NEFA 
within 2 h of administration with return to basal con-
centrations 1 h later (Waldron et al., 2003). Steiger et al. 
(1999) used a more prolonged infusion of LPS (i.e., 100 
min) to dairy heifers and observed a biphasic response 
of NEFA and glycerol in plasma. There was an initial 
increase occurring within 1 h of infusion, a decrease 
to concentrations similar to control heifers by 3 h, and 
another peak around 4.5 to 5 h post-infusion. However, 
NEFA were increased for the duration of the study (i.e., 
22 h). Triglyceride levels of heifers were reduced 2.5 h 
post-infusion with LPS and remained decreased for 10 
h, but surprisingly increased to greater than control con-
centrations at 22 h (Steiger et al., 1999). This response 
is mediated in part by TNF- α. In studies with Holstein 
heifers, administering rbTNF led to an early increase 
in triglyceride concentration at 30 to 45 min after dos-
ing that was followed by diminished concentrations 
at 2.5 h through 8 h after treatment with the cytokine 
(Kushibiki et al., 2000; 2002). 
Administration of LPS 
lessened plasma concentration of β-hydroxybutyrate 
(BHB) within 2 h of infusion (Werling, et al., 1996; 
Steiger et al., 1999; Waldron et al., 2003). There appears 
to be a variable longer term response in BHB concentra-
tions with LPS infusion. After the initial decrease, BHB 
remained suppressed in lactating cows (Waldron et al., 
2003). In studies with beef heifers, BHB concentrations 
were decreased and remained decreased for 6 h after in-
fusion (Werling et al., 1996). In contrast, dairy heifers 
with a similar infusion time, exhibited an initial decrease 
similar to the other studies, but BHB concentrations in-
creased by 6 h post-infusion to match those in healthy 
control heifers (Steiger et al., 1999). On the contrary, 
in dairy cattle with clinical mastitis, there were no sig-
niﬁ cant changes in NEFA or ketone concentrations, but 
there were chronic decreases in cholesterol (Huszenicza 
et al., 1998). Even with these variable results, it is evi-
dent that endotoxin can disrupt intermediary metabo-
lism of fatty acids.
In healthy animals, the liver is actively involved in pack-
aging NEFA by producing very-low-density lipoproteins 
(VLDL; Jones, 1982). In rodents treated with endotoxin 
or TNF, there was an increase in triglycerides and choles-
terol in serum after administration (Feingold and Grunfeld, 
1986; Feingold et al., 1992). In addition, increased fatty

acid and cholesterol content in the liver was observed with 
TNF-α treatment, and in vitro analysis of liver explants re-
vealed increased incorporation of acetate into fatty acids 
without concomitant changes in acetate oxidation to carbon 
dioxide (Feingold and Grunfeld, 1986). Interleukin-1 also 
affects lipid metabolic aberrations. Injection of IL-1 into 
chow-fed and sucrose-fed rats resulted in increased serum 
concentrations of tritiated triglycerides while decreas-
ing glycerol concentrations, indicating that the cytokine 
stimulated triglyceride secretion by liver while suppressing 
lipolysis (Feingold et al., 1990). In addition, Feingold et 
al. (1992) observed that IL-1 injected rats had increased 
hepatic fatty acid and cholesterol content when compared 
with pair-fed controls. Therefore, IL-1 increased fatty acid 
synthesis and decreased fatty acid catabolism in liver, pre-
sumably supplying the energy substrate to peripheral tis-
sues. Closely related to this is secretion of VLDL. Studies 
with septic and endotoxemic rats and liver explants indicat-
ed increased concentrations and secretion of apolipoprotein 
B (apoB) protein, which represents an increase in VLDL 
and probable role in the hypertriglyceridemia commonly 
observed with sepsis (Tripp et al., 1993; Aspichueta et al., 
2005; Bartolomé et al., 2008). Similarly, in vitro stimula-
tion of HepG2 and mouse primary liver cells by IL-1β and 
IL-6, but not TNF-α, resulted in increased mRNA and pro-
tein abundance of apoB (Yokoyama et al., 1988; Bartolomé 
et al., 2008).
Many of the changes in intermediary metabolism 
brought about by the APR and several cytokines, includ-
ing TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6, are due to changes in gene ex-
pression in hepatic tissues that alter fatty acid, cholesterol, 
and sphingolipid metabolism as well as the lipoproteins 
involved in their transport (Khovidhunkit et al., 2004). 
Previous research has focused on the activity of hepatic 
enzymes in response to TNF- α and showed that the ac-
tivities of acetyl-CoA carboxylase ( ACC) and fatty acid 
synthase (FAS ), the rate limiting enzymes of fatty acid 
synthesis, were increased 58% and 35%, respectively, in 
rats 16 h after treatment (Grunfeld et al., 1988). Further, 
studies demonstrate that such a response was due to a co-
ordinated increase in citrate concentrations, an allosteric 
modulator of ACC, and a decrease in acyl-CoA, an allo-
steric inhibitor of the same (Grunfeld et al., 1988; 1990). 
A more recent study showed that in vivo stimulation with 
LPS in rodents, and TNF-α  and IL-1, but not IL-6, stim-
ulation in Hep3B cell lines suppresses transcription of 
ACC, FAS, and PK (Feingold et al., 2011). These changes 
in gene expression were due, in part, to the reduction of 
mRNA and protein abundance for the transcription fac-
tor, carbohydrate response element binding protein. Other 
transcription factors in the liver are decreased in response 
to LPS and other APR cytokines and hormones. Namely, 
the PPAR-α and γ  are suppressed resulting in decreased 
abundance of their target genes, fatty acid transporter pro-
tein, fatty acid translocase ( CD36/FAT), heart-fatty acid 
binding protein, and carnitine palmitoyl transferase-I α, 
resulting in decreased fatty acid transport into and within 
the liver cell, as well as decreased fatty acid oxidation 
(Khovidhunkit et al., 2004). Therefore, during the APR, 
hepatic tissues increase lipogenesis somewhat indepen-
dently of transcription as a result of allosteric modula-
tors and possibly the stability of key fatty acid synthesis 
enzymes, and decrease lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation. 
This results in hypertriglyceridemia and increased VLDL 
concentrations that carry the fatty acids to peripheral tis-
sues for utilization.
In summary, the increased demand for AA for pro-
duction of APP by the liver are likely supplied by mus-
cle protein catabolism and subsequent AA uptake by the 
liver, whereas the nucleotides required for the synthesis 
of the positive APP are at the expense of the transcrip-
tion and mRNA degradation of the negative APP (Christ 
et al., 1994). Furthermore, the liver likely provides en-
ergy substrates to the peripheral tissues in exchange for 
the AA substrates needed for protein synthesis. To fur-
ther complicate the issue, Lecchi et al. (2012) identiﬁ ed 
widespread mRNA transcripts for Hp and SAA in cattle 
indicating that other tissues might be involved in the 
APR and likely experience altered metabolic activities. 
Whether this occurs in the bovine in response to BRD 
remains to be determined, as does the potential for long-
term consequences on muscle and adipose tissue accre-
tion. Ultimately
, future research is needed to describe 
acute and sustained metabolic changes in the liver and 
peripheral tissues in calves affected by BRD and the im-
munological signals that are responsible for long-term 
growth impairment.
MOLECULAR ASPECTS OF
 MUSCLE ATROPHY
Similar to cattle with chronic BRD, research in 
human pathologies with chronic in ﬂ ammation, such 
as chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, Duchenne 
muscular dystrophy, sepsis, cancer, acquired immunode-
ﬁ ciency syndrome, and chronic renal disease, produces 
severe weight loss and cachexia in patients (Hong et al. 
2008; Peterson and Guttridge, 2008). Researchers have 
shown that in vivo effects of in ﬂ ammation resulted in 
increased protein degradation in skeletal muscle (Voison 
et al., 1996; Cai et al., 2004; Acharyya et al., 2007). In 
vitro experiments have shown that protein synthesis is 
attenuated in skeletal muscle undergoing atrophy (Eley 
and Tisdale, 2007).
The protein disequilibrium observed in cachexia 
has been shown to be mediated by nuclear factor- κB 
signaling (NF-κB; Cai et al., 2004; Bakkar et al., 2005; 
Acharyya et al., 2007). Nuclear factor- κB signaling has

been identiﬁ ed in diverse cell types and activates several 
signaling pathways, the most well characterized being 
inﬂ ammation and cell survival. In brief, NF- κB activa-
tion occurs through two convergent pathways. The clas-
sical pathway indicates that multiple extracellular stim-
uli, such as TNF-α , IL-1, and LPS, interact with their 
cognate receptors [i.e., TNF receptor, IL-1 receptor, and 
toll-like receptor-4, respectively] leading to downstream 
signal transduction through mitogen-activated protein 
kinase kinase 3 and phosphorylation of the β-subunit of 
the inhibitor of NF-κB complex kinase (IKK). Activated 
IKK phosphorylates inhibitor of NF-κ B (IκB) bound to 
NF-κB, thereby freeing it to be degraded through the 
ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Upon its release, NF-κB 
crosses into the nucleus where it acts as a transcription 
factor for multiple genes (Hong et al., 2008; Peterson 
and Guttridge, 2008). The alternative pathway is medi-
ated through extracellular stimuli interacting with their 
receptors (e.g. CD40, a co-stimulatory receptor on anti-
gen presenting cells; B-cell activating factor of the TNF 
family receptor; or lymphotoxin-β receptor) to promote 
signaling through NF-κ B-inducing kinase, which acts 
on IKK-α leading to phosphorylation of p100 (a subunit 
of IκB) causing it to be degraded through the ubiquitin-
proteasome pathway resulting in transnucleation of NF-
κB (Hong et al., 2008; Peterson and Guttridge, 2008).
Nuclear factor- κB signaling has been shown to in-
crease muscle catabolism by inducing proteins in the 
ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, which is the principle 
route of protein degradation in the cell. Researchers 
have shown that in vivo and in vitro exposure to TNF-α 
resulted in increased ubiquitin mRNA and protein deg-
radation in rat skeletal muscle (Garcia-Martinez et al., 
1995; Llovera et al., 1997). Others have shown that 
atrogin-1 (also known as muscle atrophy F-box) and 
muscle RING ﬁ nger 1, which are muscle-speci ﬁ c ubiq-
uitin ligases, are increased in atrophying skeletal muscle 
(Bodine et al., 2001; Gomes et al., 2001). In ﬂ ammatory 
ligands such as LPS, dexamethasone, and TNF- α have 
been shown to upregulate expression of these atrogenes 
with long-term effects, but interestingly inhibition of 
these pathways does not mitigate their expression (Frost 
et al., 2007). Frost et al. (2007) observed that the ana-
bolic hormone, IGF-I, could suppress atrogin-1, but not 
muscle RING ﬁ nger 1 expression. Proteolysis inducing 
factor has been shown to increase the “chymotrypsin-
like” activity of the proteasome in murine myotubes 
(Whitehouse and Tisdale, 2003). Another ubiquitin-li-
gase, Nedd4, has been shown to be upregulated in at-
rophying muscle and is speci ﬁ c for suspended muscle 
(Koncarevic et al., 2007). Several ubiquitin ligases can 
be induced during skeletal muscle atrophy and are spe-
ciﬁ c for the type of stimuli received.
In chronic in ﬂ 
ammation, serum concentrations of 
pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines (i.e., TNF- α and IL-6) in-
crease and are associated with decreased muscle mass 
(Tracey et al. 1988; Visser et al., 2002). In rats injected 
with TNF-α , anorexia soon appeared, but whole-body 
protein reserves were depleted signi ﬁ cantly more than 
in pair-fed control rats (Tracey et al., 1988). Endotoxin 
and increased concentrations of TNF- α and IL-1β  lead 
to decreased concentrations of IGF-I and GH, both of 
which are involved in myogenesis (Fan et al., 1995, 
1996; Frost et al., 2003). Furthermore, these cytokines 
decrease IGF-I mediated protein synthesis by utilizing 
ceramide as a second messenger to downregulate myo-
genin and MyoD expression, 2 myogenic proteins (Strle 
et al., 2004). It has been demonstrated that NF-κB signal-
ing, mediated by proteolysis inducing factor and angio-
tensin II, can affect translation ef ﬁ ciency by inhibiting 
binding of methionyl-tRNA to the 43 S ribosome (Eley 
and Tisdale, 2007). To complicate the situation, research 
indicates that protein degradation and decreased protein 
synthesis are responsible for the increased muscle pro-
tein turnover during acute sepsis; however, compensato-
ry protein synthesis occurs during chronic sepsis with a 
concomitant increase in proteolysis (Voison et al., 1996). 
Lastly, pro-inﬂ ammatory cytokines, namely TNF- α 
and IL-1β, can inhibit differentiation and decrease pro-
tein content of myoblasts through NF-κB transactivation 
(Langen et al., 2001). What is of interest is that NF- κB 
signaling may be necessary for myoblast proliferation. 
Typically, myoblasts will differentiate into myotubes by 
fusing with existing myo ﬁ bers, but ablation of NF- κB 
results in decreased myoblast proliferation and differen-
tiation by inducing cyclin D expression and promoting 
cell cycle progression from G1 to S phase (Guttridge 
et al., 1999). Speci ﬁ cally, NF-κB may exert its inhibi-
tory effects during the late stages of differentiation. 
YinYang1 is a negative regulator of myogenesis and was 
discovered to be induced by NF- κB activation resulting 
in decreased abundance of myo ﬁ brillar genes encoding 
troponins, myosin heavy and light chains, and α-actin, 
thereby preventing maturation into myotubes (Wang et 
al., 2007). Activation of NF- κB has been shown to de-
stabilize MyoD transcripts through the inducible nitric 
oxide synthase-nitric oxide pathway (Sitcheran et al., 
2003; Di Marco et al., 2005). Under these circumstances, 
repair of differentiated myo ﬁ bers would be suppressed 
by NF-κB activation. This was demonstrated in a study 
by Mourkioti et al. (2006), who observed that inhibiting 
IKK2 activity improved morphology and size of myo-
ﬁ bers. Overall, NF-κ B activation is necessary for myo-
blast proliferation, but its activity prevents myoblast dif-
ferentiation and may impair muscle regeneration after 
injury or chronic disease.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
There is a gap in the understanding of how BRD di-
rectly impacts muscle (i.e., decreases ﬁ nal BW and HCW), 
subcutaneous fat, and marbling, even though it has been 
well documented that sickness impacts growth and car-
cass quality. With greater incidences of clinical signs of 
BRD, there are decreases in ADG, BW, HCW, 12th-rib fat 
thickness, and marbling score. Decreased growth and car-
cass quality results from inadequate intake during times 
of sickness, or it could be that gene expression and me-
tabolism in economically important tissues are changing 
priorities. Instead of utilizing nutrients to increase in size, 
muscle and fat tissue may be repartitioning nutrients to-
ward areas that have a greater demand (i.e., mounting an 
immune response leading to organism survival). This has 
been observed in human, mouse, and swine models, and 
more research is needed in cattle.
In human and mouse models, as well as in vitro, 
the acute phase cytokines have negative effects on the 
precursors to metabolism. These cytokines have been 
shown to be highly expressed in damaged tissues, dur-
ing immune response, and by stimulation in vitro. These 
cytokines at appropriate concentrations are needed, but 
if over-expressed could be causing excessive damage. 
For example, TNF-α  can inhibit IGF-I and has been 
shown to cause muscle wasting, anorexia, and insulin 
resistance, each of which could slow growth and perfor-
mance of cattle. Focused research is needed to increase 
our understanding of how BRD and chronic muscle 
wasting impacts cattle growth and carcass quality.
