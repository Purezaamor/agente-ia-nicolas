abstract
Inorganic nitrogen fertilisers are commonly applied to crops and pastures to increase or maintain produc-
tivity. The beneﬁts of N application must be balanced with the potential for environmental damage. At
Lincoln University, Canterbury, New Zealand a split plot experiment with two irrigation levels (irrigated
and dryland) and two N fertiliser application rates (0 and 800 kg N/ha/y (2003/2004) or 1600 kg N/ha/y
(2004/2005) was established on a 9-year-old cocksfoot (Dactylis glomerata L.) dominant pasture to vali-
date the N dilution curve for temperate grass species. The extent of N deﬁciency of pastures which had
suboptimal N was quantiﬁed by calculation of a ratio between measured N% of herbage and optimum
N% from the N dilution curve. The N dilution curve had the form N% = 4.8 DM−0.35 (R2 = 0.65) and the NNI
ranged from a maximum of 1.2, which indicated luxury uptake, to a minimum of 0.2, which has been
proposed as the minimum NNI required to result in net growth. When moisture was adequate for growth,
the main cause of yield differences between +N and −N pastures was radiation use efﬁciency with −N
pastures producing 0.54 g DM/MJ PAR in spring or less than half that of +N pastures (1.16 g DM/MJ PAR).
The intrinsic link between water availability and N uptake in dryland and irrigated pastures was explained
(R2 = 0.88) by the relationship between the water use efﬁciency and N uptake ratio. Periods of low N uptake
(N deﬁciency) were associated with low water use efﬁciency.

1. Introduction
Suboptimal nitrogen (N) is the most limiting nutrient for dry
matter (DM) production in plants (Grindlay, 1997; Azam, 2002;
Galloway et al., 2003). It reduces productivity indirectly through
restriction of leaf size and consequently canopy light interception
(Belanger, 1998) and directly by reducing photosynthesis rates per
unit leaf area (Novoa and Loomis, 1981; Peri et al., 2002). To over-
come N limitations inorganic fertilisers are used intensively for crop
and pasture production. The need to increase or maintain produc-
tion must then be balanced with the potential effects of N losses
because leaching of NO3− to groundwater pollutes waterways and
gaseous loss of N2O contributes to stratospheric ozone depletion
(IPCC, 2001; Oenema et al., 2005).
The FAO (2000) reported 65–80% of the N2O component of
greenhouse gas emissions resulted from agricultural activity and
estimated 58% was contributed by OECD countries (IPCC, 2001).
Global N2O emissions from agriculture have been predicted to
increase 35–60% by 2030, compared with emissions in 2000, due

to increased N fertiliser use and urine returns by grazing livestock.
To reduce the potential for environmental damage by NO3− and
N2O, mitigation strategies for N management are required. These
include increasing N use efﬁciency by using nitriﬁcation inhibitors,
use of slow release N fertilisers, improving timing of N applications
and avoiding N applications in excess of plant demand (McSwiney
and Robertson, 2005; IPCC, 2007). In on-farm pasture management
these last two represent a low cost opportunity to minimise N
losses.
Nitrogen use efﬁciency (kg DM/kg N) is commonly used to deter-
mine agricultural efﬁciency. However, diminishing response curves
between DM yield and the rate of applied N fertiliser are agro-
nomic descriptions. These approaches are environment speciﬁc,
highly variable and dependent on the time of application and form
of N fertiliser used (Novoa and Loomis, 1981; Smith et al., 2000).
Extrapolation of these results may induce N applications in excess
of plant requirements due to the differences in the main environ-
mental drivers of temperature, rainfall and solar radiation receipts
between sites, seasons and years.
Quantiﬁcation of expected DM responses to applied N may be
improved using a nitrogen nutrition index (NNI). This accounts for
the decrease in herbage N concentration (%N) as yield accumu-
lates (Lemaire et al., 1989), which has previously been incorrectly

with non-limiting N and sufﬁcient water for growth.
N(%) = a(DM)−b (1)
The ‘a’ coefﬁcient represents the optimum N concentration for a
1.0 t/ha yield and the ‘b’ coefﬁcient describes the decrease in N% as
DM yield accumulates (Belanger and Gastal, 2000). This relation-
ship allows the optimum N concentration (Nopt) for a known yield
to be identiﬁed. These relationships are usually generated from
sequential harvests within a deﬁned regrowth cycle. For cocksfoot,
the form of this relationship was reported as N% = 4.8 (DM)−0.32
(Lemaire et al., 1989).
This equation provides a stable relationship between N% and DM
yield across a range of environments but may be species speciﬁc
(Justes et al., 1994; Lemaire et al., 2007). An NNI is then calculated
as the ratio between measured N concentration (Nact) and (Nopt)
for a known yield identiﬁed from the N dilution curve (Belanger
and Gastal, 2000). The extent of any N deﬁciency is then quan-
tiﬁed and, if necessary, amendment N applications can be made.
An NNI of 1.0 indicates optimum N nutrition, values ≥1.0 rep-
resent luxury consumption (Justes et al., 1994) which gives no
additional increase in DM yield for the higher N concentration. Val-
ues <1.0 indicate DM production is compromised by N deﬁciency
(Belanger and Gastal, 2000). Farruggia et al. (2004) deﬁned the
‘adequate’ NNI range for growth between 0.8 and 1.0 and Lemaire
and Gastal (1997) indicated the minimum NNI for net growth was
0.2.
Practical application of an NNI for fertiliser management
requires the ability to combine it with the other drivers of produc-
tion. For example, temperature has a major inﬂuence on seasonal
DM production, when soil moisture is nonlimiting. Thus, the objec-
tives of this study were to (1) validate the form of the nitrogen
dilution curve proposed for cocksfoot and then subsequently cal-
culate the NNI, (2) identify the mechanisms responsible for yield
differences between +N and −N pastures and (3) create an N
uptake ratio to explain the interaction between soil moisture and
nitrogen availability. The practical application of these results will
be improved by matching of fertiliser-N application with pasture
demand, thus reducing the potential for adverse environmental
impacts.
2. Materials and methods
A split plot experiment was imposed on a 9-year-old ‘Wana’
cocksfoot dominant monoculture at Lincoln University, Canterbury,
New Zealand (43◦38′S and 172◦28′E, 11 m a.s.l.) on 30 September
2003. The soil was a templeton silt loam (Udic Ustochrept, USDA
Soil Taxonomy) of 0.6–0.8 m depth overlying alluvial gravels (Cox,
1978; U.S.D.A., 1984; Watt and Burgham, 1992).
Main plot treatments were dryland (D) or fully irrigated (I) to
maintain the actual soil moisture deﬁcit at <50 mm in the top 0.5 m
of the soil proﬁle. Irrigation was applied with a removable over-
head sprinkler system ﬁtted with a ﬂow meter. Soil moisture was
measured with TDR (0–0.2 m) (Trace Systems, Model 6050X1, Soil
Moisture Equipment, Santa Barbara, CA, USA) and neutron probe
(0.2–2.3 m) (Troxler Electronic Industries Inc., Triangle Research
Park, NC, USA) every 7–14 d. Subplots were N fertiliser, applied as
urea, at 0 kg N/ha/y (−N) or 800 kg N/ha/y (+N) in the 2003/2004

Regrowth periods were ∼25–35 d during active growth and
extended to a maximum of 52 d over winter. At the end of every
rotation DM cuts were taken from a 0.2 m2 quadrat with a set of
electric shears to a residual pasture height of 30 mm. The remainder
of each plot was then mown to a 30 mm residual height and herbage
removed. Botanical composition was determined from subsam-
ples at every harvest. Subsamples of green cocksfoot herbage were
retained and ground to pass through a 1 mm stainless steel sieve
(Cyclotec Mill, USA). Nitrogen concentration was determined by
near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) by the Agriculture and Life Sci-
ences Division, Lincoln University.
To account for seasonal temperature variation, DM production
was summarised using thermal time (◦Cd) calculated after the iden-
tiﬁcation of an appropriate base air temperature (Jones and Kiniry,
1986). Annual thermal time accumulation was calculated for the
period 30 September 2003 to 5 October 2004 for the 2003/2004
growth season and from 6 October 2004 to 17 October 2005 for
the 2004/2005 season. This allowed growth rate to be described
in a standardised form (kg DM/◦Cd/ha) to allow extrapolation
of results to environments with different seasonal temperature
regimes.
Interception of photosynthetically active radiation (MJ inter-
cepted PAR/m2) by the canopy was measured at ground level with
a Sunscan plant canopy analysis system ﬁtted with a BF2 beam
fraction sensor. This allows simultaneous measurement of inci-
dent and transmitted PAR (Delta-T Devices Ltd, Burwell, Cambridge,
UK). System settings were a leaf absorption parameter of 0.85 and
an ellipsoidal leaf angle distribution parameter of 1.0 indicating a
canopy tending towards randomness, or equivalent to an extinction
coefﬁcient of 0.57 (Belanger et al., 1992).
To allow the Sunscan sensor measurements to be made at
ground level, and prevent the sensor from disturbing the canopy,
metal channels (1.0 m × 0.04 m × 0.03 m) were installed in a rep-
resentative area of each plot. Measurements were initiated 6
weeks after installation, which allowed the surrounding pasture
to recover, and continued at 7–14 d intervals from 01/2004. At the
start of each regrowth cycle, measurements were made to quan-
tify light interception by residual biomass (leaf, pseudostem and
dead material) which commonly exceeded 70% of incident PAR, and
represented a plant area index of ≥2.0 (Mills, 2007), except when
dryland pastures were exposed to periods of water stress. Changes
in PAR interception between sequential measurements throughout
each regrowth cycle were then interpolated to calculate the daily
rate of increase in canopy interception. Incident total solar radia-
tion (MJ/m2) was recorded daily at the Broadﬁelds Meteorological
Station and multiplied by 0.5 (Monteith, 1972) to determine daily
PAR.
Because of the intrinsic link between moisture availability and
N uptake from the soil an N uptake ratio was calculated between N
recovered in herbage per mm of water consumed (kg N/mm) from
the 0–1.2 m soil layers. The N uptake ratio was then used as a pre-
dictor of water use efﬁciency (WUE, kg DM/mm/ha) to quantify
the combined effect of insufﬁcient soil moisture for growth and N
deﬁciency on DM production.

4. Results
4.1. Yield
Total annual yield and yield of the green cocksfoot component
are shown in Table 3. Total annual DM yield (t DM/ha/y) was 21.9
(I+N), 9.8 (I−N) and 15.3 t DM/ha/y from D+N pastures. The three
way interaction (p ≤ 0.05) between irrigation (I), nitrogen (N) and
year (Y) was caused by D−N pastures which produced 7.5 t DM/ha
in 2003/2004 compared with 5.0 t DM/ha in 2004/2005. The main
effect of N accounted for 79% of the total sums of squares compared
with 1% for the interaction.
4.2. Accounting for seasonal temperature variation
To account for seasonal temperature variations, thermal time
(Tt) was used as a predictor of DM yield. The base air temperature
(Tb) for growth, which corresponded to the lowest CV and had a
slope closest to zero (Draper and Smith, 1998), was 3 ◦C. The opti-
mum temperature for growth was set at 23 ◦C(Mills, 2007) which
was within the temperature range of 19–23 ◦C measured for maxi-
mum cocksfoot leaf photosynthesis (Peri et al., 2002).
The impact of temperature on DM production was summarised
through accumulated Tt for irrigated pastures (Fig. 1). The effects
and timing of water stress on DM production of the dryland pas-
tures were explained previously (Mills et al., 2006) and subsequent
analysis showed that both +N pastures produced DM at a near con-
stant rate of 7.0 kg DM/◦Cd/ha above Tb when periods of water stress
were excluded. For −N pastures the rate was 56% lower (p ≤ 0.001)
at 3.3 kg DM/◦Cd/ha.
4.3. The N dilution curve
The N dilution curve (Fig. 2) is similar to the curve pro-
duced by Lemaire et al. (1989). The response curve indicates
that for a 1.0 t DM/ha yield of green cocksfoot herbage the Nopt
was 4.8%. At 3.0 t DM/ha the Nopt is 3.3%, or 99 kg N/ha in green
herbage.

4.4. Quantifying the extent of N deﬁciency
The NNI (Fig. 3) showed that pastures which received no N
fertiliser were consistently N deﬁcient (NNI < 1.0). In both growth
seasons, the NNI of dryland pastures deviated from their respec-
tive irrigated control pastures during periods of water stress. After
rainfall decreased the actual soil moisture deﬁcit to less than the
critical limiting deﬁcit of 78 mm (0–0.8 m) (Mills et al., 2006) the
NNI of dryland pastures recovered to similar or greater values to
those of irrigated control pastures. Interactions between irrigation
and N occurred as the soil moisture deﬁcit increased in dryland pas-
tures in both years. Speciﬁcally, only the I + N pastures maintained
an NNI ≥ 0.8. All other pastures showed N was deﬁcient with NNI
between 0.2 and 0.5.

4.5. Shoot radiation use efﬁciency (RUE)
Shoot RUE was averaged for each season and was 1.16, 1.00,
0.80 and 0.89 g DM/MJ PAR in spring, summer, autumn and win-
ter, respectively, for the I+N pasture (Fig. 4). For all pastures, RUE in
autumn was lower than all other seasons. Absolute values ranged
from 0.26 (D−N pastures in autumn) to 1.27 g DM/MJ PAR (D+N
pastures in winter). In all seasons, RUE was affected by irrigation,
N-supply, or their interaction but the effect of N was greatest. The
NNI obtained from the green cocksfoot herbage was a strong predic-
tor (R2 = 0.80) of the effect of N on pasture production (Fig. 5). The
form of the relationship was RUE = −0.23 (0.08) + 1.26(0.10) × NNI.
4.6. Relationship between N uptake and water availability

by the relationship between relative yield and the actual soil mois-

water use and N uptake, the relationship between the N uptake
ratio and WUE was assessed (Fig. 6). The relationship (WUE = 6.7

5. Discussion
The N dilution curve provided an appropriate basis for analysing
nitrogen response (Fig. 2). The derived curve was similar to the
general relationship previously published for temperate C3 grass
species (Lemaire et al., 1989; Greenwood et al., 1990; Belanger
and Gastal, 2000). Our study used N concentrations determined
from green cocksfoot herbage, typical of the situation in a rota-
tionally grazed pasture. It is not recommended that reproductive
crops/pastures are evaluated by this method (Duru et al., 1997).
This is because of changes in partitioning and/or remobilisation of
root reserves which occurs as pastures change from the vegetative
to reproductive phase (Parsons and Robson, 1981). This results in a
‘summer slump’ in pasture productivity (Radcliffe and Baars, 1987)
caused by low vegetative tiller populations. In this study the repro-

seedheads. The relationship then accounted for variations caused
by site, season and across years (Lemaire et al., 2007) making it
more robust for extrapolating results to regions outside of those
used for derivation than purely agronomic approaches.
Thermal time proved to be a useful way of accounting for the
effects of seasonal temperature variation when water was nonlim-
iting to growth (Fig. 1). The 7.0 kg DM/◦Cd/ha (I + N) was about half
that reported for vegetative spring growth by Lemaire and Salette
(1982). Data for the current experiment summarised the annual
response froma9to10-year-old prostrate ‘Wana’ cocksfoot sward.
In contrast, Lemaire and Salette (1982) reported only spring pro-
duction from a 2 to 3-year-old ‘Lucifer’ cocksfoot pasture. In both
studies the strong linear relationship between accumulated DM
production and thermal time supports its use to summarise the pro-
cesses associated with carbon supply and demand. Speciﬁcally, leaf
appearance (carbon demand) is intrinsically linked to carbon sup-
ply, through canopy expansion and radiation interception, driving
vegetative growth.
Seasonally, RUE of the I+N pastures ranged from 0.80 to 1.16 g
DM/MJ PAR (Fig. 4). Generally, RUE of all pastures was highest
in spring and lowest in autumn. Belanger et al. (1994) showed
shoot RUE of tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea Schreb.) varied season-
ally however, RUE based on total biomass (root + shoot) was stable
throughout the year. This indicates that seasonal RUE variation is
associated with partitioning between roots and shoots.
Our calculated RUE values are lower than those previously pub-
lished (Belanger et al., 1994; Yunusa et al., 1995). This probably
reﬂects differences in methodology and the inherent difﬁculty in
accurate measurement of intercepted radiation. In this study PAR
interception was estimated from all canopy components because
the equipment did not differentiate between green and non-green
material. Also, placing the Sunscan sensor at ground level we were
able to estimate PAR interception by residual biomass. At times this
was predominantly leaf lamina with a high photosynthetic capac-
ity. At other times, and in different treatments, it was pseudostem
and senescing or dead material. Lamina N% on 17 October 2005 was
5.0% for +N pastures compared with 2.7% for −N pastures. Similarly,
pseudostem contained 3.8 and 1.2% N for the +N and −N pastures,
respectively. These differences can be expected to affect photosyn-
thetic capacity (Peri et al., 2002) and therefore RUE. It is likely that
68 A. Mills et al. / Europ. J. Agronomy 30 (2009) 63–69
true RUE of established pastures lie somewhere in between the
conservative values presented for this study and the higher values
published elsewhere.
A major effect of insufﬁcient N, when soil moisture was nonlim-
iting, was to reduce RUE. The RUE of the I−N pastures was less than
half that of the I+N pastures in all seasons. It is likely that insufﬁcient
NinI−N pastures reduced canopy photosynthesis as highlighted
by Peri et al. (2002). In contrast, during periods of water stress
there were interactions between water availability and N nutri-
tion. Fig. 3 shows that as water stress developed the NNI of both
D+N and D−N pastures declined relative to their respective irri-
gated control pastures. Reduced N availability would have caused
photosynthesis per unit area to decline (Novoa and Loomis, 1981;
Greenwood et al., 1990) while water stress reduced canopy expan-
sion, though effects on individual leaf expansion and suppression
of tillering (Mills, 2007). The cumulative effect is a reduced surface
area available for canopy photosynthesis and gas exchange.
A comparison of N uptake ratio with WUE conﬁrmed that water
stress limited N uptake (Fig. 6) and DM production. The I−N pas-
tures generally had lower WUE and N uptake ratios than the I+N
pastures. This indicates that N deﬁcient pastures used available
water less efﬁciently in the production of DM than pastures sup-
plied with nonlimiting N. In addition, as the soil dried the N uptake
ratio and WUE of dryland pastures declined relative to their irri-
gated control pastures. This was expected because both factors are
intrinsically linked which can make it difﬁcult to attribute results
to an individual factor (Stark and Firestone, 1995). As the soil dries,
symptoms of N deﬁciency may be observed because nutrient supply
from soil solution is unable to meet plant demand. The majority of
N was stored in the top 0–0.2 m of the soil (Mills, 2007). Therefore,
even if sufﬁcient water for growth can be extracted from deeper
soil layers, N deﬁciency may occur as the topsoil dries (Garwood
and Williams, 1967).
High WUE values (>49 kg DM/mm/ha) from the D+N pastures
(Fig. 6) consistently occurred after water stress conditions were
alleviated. This resulted from a higher tiller population (Mills, 2007)
and RUE (Fig. 5) than the I+N pasture which resulted in rapid
canopy expansion and uptake of previously unavailable N (Fig. 3).
This produced a greater surface area for photosynthesis. These
results are consistent with Volaire et al. (1998) who showed water
stressed cocksfoot had increased leaf appearance, leaf expansion
and tiller population than irrigated plants when growth resumed
post-drought. In addition, a reduction in the evaporation compo-
nent of evapotranspiration associated with stomatal closure may
have resulted in greater apparent WUE when soil moisture was
still insufﬁcient to match pasture demand in the recovery phase
(Hsiao, 1973; Silcock and Wilson, 1981). It is possible that the high-
est WUE (60 kg DM/mm/ha) for the D+N pasture in autumn 2005
was an artefact of the weekly measurement period. During the ﬁve
weeks of regrowth 103 mm of rain fell with 40 mm over a three day
period. Of this total ∼35 mm was attributed to drainage to depths
below the rooting zone based on preferential ﬂow (Brown, 2004).
If 15 mm of this water actually contributed to growth rather than
drainage then the WUE of the D+N pasture in this rotation would
have dropped to 31 kg DM/mm/ha. Soil moisture was not measured
daily and therefore, it is not possible to determine an accurate value
for WUE.
In 2003/2004, annual yield of green cocksfoot herbage of −N
pastures was 8.3 t DM/ha/y when irrigated and 4.6 t DM/ha/y for
dryland pastures (Table 3). Nitrogen recovered in herbage indicated
that I−N pastures accounted for 282 kg N/ha/y in green herbage
while D−N pastures contained 196 kg N/ha/y (Mills, 2007). This
amount represents the annual contribution of soil N mineralisation.
The N dilution curve and cocksfoot yields from individual regrowth
cycles were used to calculate the amount of N necessary to pro-
duce these DM yields with nonlimiting N conditions. This showed
that pastures which received no N fertiliser in 2003/2004 contained
∼66% of the N required. Effectively soil N supply to −N pastures
from mineralisation was insufﬁcient for the DM yields produced.
This suggests that the metabolic N pool, associated with N storage,
cell division and formation of the photosynthetic system within
the plant (Caloin and Yu, 1984; Jamieson and Semenov, 2000), was
reallocated at the expense of photosynthetic efﬁciency to support
structural/non-photosynthetic N requirements, above a critical
minimum threshold when moisture was adequate for growth.
The rate of N fertiliser applied to +N pastures in the second year
was more than double that required for the yield achieved. How-
ever, the objective of the study was to ensure that N was not a
limitation to growth at any time and 12 months later visual differ-
ences were still present at the site. In the ﬁrst year pH increased in
all pastures as a result of lime application. The pH of the +N pas-
tures declined from 6.4 to 5.3 (Table 2) in the second year, which
was expected as urea fertiliser acidiﬁes soil during nitriﬁcation
(McLaren and Cameron, 1996).
In practice, if N fertiliser is applied, a change of approach to graz-
ing management is required for both irrigated and dryland farming
systems to reduce the potential for N loss to the environment. Use
of a combination of growth rate summarised with thermal time
and the NNI will allow strategic N applications. This will reduce the
potential for environmental damage which may occur when N is
applied in excess of plant growth requirements (Reay et al., 2003;
McSwiney and Robertson, 2005; IPCC, 2007).
These results have important implications for both irrigated and
dryland pastoral farming systems. Relationships between DM yield
and either intercepted radiation or water use are physiological con-
cepts used to explain productivity. As both concepts can be used to
explain DM production they are indirectly related. Radiation use
efﬁciency, as an indirect measure of net canopy photosynthesis,
was the main cause of yield differences of pastures exposed to sub-
optimal levels of N nutrition. The water used during that growth
was used less efﬁciently in the production of DM and emphasises
the importance of adequate N nutrition when water is available
for growth. In dryland systems this will be in early spring, as tem-
peratures increase prior to water stress, and in late summer/early
autumn when rainfall alleviates drought conditions but before tem-
perature becomes the main constraint to pasture production in
winter.
6. Conclusions
• The optimum N requirements for cocksfoot growth were
explained by the relationship between N% and yield. The rela-
tionship had the form N% = 4.8 × DM−0.35.
• The main inﬂuence of N deﬁciency was due to differences in RUE
and this was adequately explained by relating RUE to the NNI
when water was nonlimiting.
• The intrinsic link between water availability and N uptake was
used to explain variation in WUE. The ratio showed regrowth
periods when N uptake was low also had low WUE. This acknowl-
edges the link between water uptake and mineral nutrition as the
soil dries which can confound results in ﬁeld-based studies.
• The NNI exceeded 1.0 in early spring and after alleviation of water
stress conditions. The minimum of 0.2 supports the proposal that
an NNI of 0.2 is the minimum required for growth to occur.
