Efﬁciency of microbial protein synthesis on red
clover and ryegrass silages supplemented with barley
by rumen simulation technique (RUSITEC)

Institute of Grassland and Environmental Research, Aberystwyth, Wales SY23 3EB, UK

Abstract
Bacterial protein supply from the rumen is frequently low in animals fed on silage-based diets, par-
tially because of the lack of rumen available sources of energy. An in vitro experiment was designed to
determine the optimum rate of inclusion of rolled barley to optimise rumen efﬁciency of bacterial pro-
tein synthesis (EBPS) of diets comprising a typical ryegrass silage (GR; crude protein (CP) = 139 g/kg
dry matter (DM); water soluble carbohydrates (WSC) = 71 g/kg DM; NH3–N = 133 g/kg N; pH 4.23)
or red clover silage (RC; CP = 168 g/kg DM; WSC = 28 g/kg DM; NH3–N = 80 g/kg N; pH 4.12). Both
silages were mixed with increasing proportions of barley grain (0, 150, 300 and 450 g/kg DM). Treat-
ments were tested using the rumen simulation technique (RUSITEC), replicated in three independent
10-day incubation periods (blocks). Artiﬁcial saliva was infused at a rate of 0.5 ml/min, and on day
4 of incubation 15NH4SO4 was added as a microbial marker. Ammonia–N and pH of the efﬂuent
were above 50 mg/l and 6.6, respectively, in all treatments and at all time points measured. Inclu-
sion of barley grain reduced the efﬂuent concentrations of acetic, propionic and total volatile fatty
acids (P ≤ 0.06). Apparent digestibilities of DM (DMAD ) and organic matter (OMAD ) did not differ

Keywords: Microbial protein synthesis; Red clover silage; Ryegrass silage; RUSITEC
1. Introduction
There is unequivocal evidence that diets containing a high proportion of silage support
low levels of microbial protein synthesis in the rumen (ARC, 1980; Thomas and Thomas,
1985; Dewhurst et al., 2000). Despite the lack of a complete explanation for this, the
poor ATP yield of silage fermentation products (Thomas and Thomas, 1985), the reduced
availability of rumen degradable carbohydrates, the high proportion of total nitrogen (N)
present as non-protein N (NPN, Thomas and Thomas, 1985), and the limited supply of
dietary amino acids (AA, Oh et al., 1999), all contribute to reduce the efﬁciency of N
utilisation by rumen microorganisms and thus to the whole animal. Silages frequently have
a poor balance between rapidly rumen-degradable sources of energy and the concentration
of highly soluble forms of N. Forage legumes typically contain low concentrations of water-
soluble carbohydrate (WSC) and dry matter (DM), and have a high buffering capacity, which
makes them particularly susceptible to poor fermentation accompanied by high levels of
proteolysis during ensilage. Thus, the addition of readily available energy such as sugar or
starch to diets based on fresh or ensiled grass has positive effects on microbial protein supply
and N utilisation by ruminants (Chamberlain et al., 1985; Keady et al., 1998; Reynolds
et al., 2001). With the exceptions of maize and sorghum, more than 90% of the starch in
cereal grains is normally fermented in the rumen (Castillo, 1999), making cereals good
supplements to silages in ruminant diets. Hence, carbohydrate availability in the rumen of
legume silage-fed ruminants has the potential of improving the efﬁciency of N capture by
rumen microorganisms, reducing not only the requirement of expensive sources of dietary
protein, but also reducing N excretion from the animal and subsequently reducing the output
of N to the environment.
Degradation of plant proteins in the silo results from plant and microbial enzyme activity
(McDonald et al., 1991). Proteolytic activity can break down an important proportion of
the original plant protein, but there is a wide variation between species. For example,
despite similar inherent proteolytic activity in red clover (RC; Trifolium pratense) and
lucerne (Medicago sativa, Papadopoulos and McKersie, 1983), RC silage usually contains
signiﬁcantly less NPN than similar silages made from lucerne (Papadopoulos and McKersie,
1983; Albrecht and Muck, 1991). Polyphenol oxidases present in RC leaves appear to

convert phenols into reactive o-quinones that reduce proteolytic activity, and consequently
the NPN content (Jones et al., 1995). It has also been shown that the supply of rumen by-pass
AA in RC silages is considerably greater than that of lucerne (Komprda et al., 1996).
It is therefore hypothesised that an increase of rumen available energy will contribute to
increased microbial protein yield in grass and legume silages. However, it is expected that
microbial protein yield from RC would be higher than from grass silages. The aim of this
experiment was to assess microbial synthesis in the rumen from diets comprising ryegrass
(GR; Lolium perenne) and RC silages when supplemented with rolled barley as a source of
rumen degradable energy.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Experimental design
Two silages (GR and RC) were tested as basal diets, to assess the rumen microbial N
yield response under increased levels of barley grain inclusion: 0, 150, 300 and 450 g/kg
DM. Treatments were replicated in three independent 10-day incubation periods (blocks)
using the rumen simulation technique (RUSITEC, Czerkawski and Breckenridge, 1977).
Efﬂuents were sampled on day 8 of each period at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 24 h to quantify
rumen parameters (i.e. pH, volatile fatty acids, VFA and NH3N), and on the last day of each
period 48-h residues from the ﬁnal set of fermented feed material were used to estimate diet
digestibility.
2.2. Production and preparation of feeds for RUSITEC
The GR silage was a second cut silage made from a perennial ryegrass-dominated sward
harvested in June 1999, at a target vegetative growth stage of 50% ﬂower-head emergence.
The RC silage was made from the ﬁrst harvest of a monoculture sward of RC (cv. Merviot)
cut in May 1999, at a target vegetative stage of late bud/early bloom. Both forages were
cut and wilted for 48 h before being baled with the aid of an inoculant (Livesystem; Genus
PLC, Nantwich, UK) applied at a rate of 106 colony-forming units/g fresh matter, followed
by wrapping with at least six layers of plastic ﬁlm. The barley grain was a commercially
available rolled barley.
The silages were stored for 8 months before being sampled, and sufﬁcient silage was
collected to carry out three incubation periods. Silages were stored cold (4 ◦C) while an
oven DM estimation (100 ◦C, overnight) was carried out on the barley grain and silages. The
following day, self-seal plastic bags were ﬁlled with approximately 12 g total DM of silage
and barley grain in the proportions required for each treatment. Treatment bags were ﬁlled
sequentially by period, and representative samples of silage for each period were collected
and frozen for subsequent chemical characterisation. All manipulations were carried out in
a manner aimed at minimising silage deterioration by reducing the time samples were at
room temperature. After the bags had been ﬁlled, they were frozen and stored at −18 ◦C
until required, when their contents were transferred to nylon bags and thawed overnight at
4 ◦C.

2.3. RUSITEC technique
The study was carried out using RUSITEC apparatus (Czerkawski and Breckenridge,
1977) consisting of eight airtight vessels with an effective volume of 1 l each, immersed
in a water bath maintained at 39 ◦C. Rumen liquor (ca. 4.5 l) and solid digesta (ca. 700 g)
were collected from a rumen ﬁstulated dairy cow fed on ad libitum quantities of GR silage
and supplemented with 8 kg concentrate per day. Digesta was transported to the laboratory
within an hour in pre-heated vacuum ﬂasks, and strained through two layers of cheesecloth
into a CO2-ﬁlled baker. Each vessel was charged with 500 ml of strained rumen liquor,
200 ml of artiﬁcial saliva (McDougall, 1948, pH 8.23), two nylon bags (40-/H9262m pore size;
one containing ca. 80 g of rumen digesta solids and the other containing a sample), topped
up with artiﬁcial saliva, and sealed. Artiﬁcial saliva was supplied to each vessel at a ﬂow
rate of 0.5 ml/min using a peristaltic pump (model 202U, Watson-Marlow Ltd., Falmouth,
Cornwall, UK). After 24 h the vessels were opened and the rumen digesta solid bags replaced
by a new sample bag (ca. 12 g DM), the removed bags were squeezed, washed in artiﬁcial
saliva and the washing returned to the vessel. Subsequently, each feed bag was replaced by
a new one after 48 h incubation period.
On day 4 of each period after replacing the nylon bags containing fresh feed samples,
70 mg of (15NH4)2SO4 (containing approximately 10 at.% 15N) was added to each incuba-
tion vessel. (15NH4)2SO4 was also added to the McDougall buffer at a rate of 70 mg/l, which
was infused until the end of each 10-day incubation period. Efﬂuent collected on day 4 (be-
fore the addition of 15N) was kept for background analysis of 15N, and then used to calculate
15N enrichment. From day 5 on, the digested nylon bag feed residues were washed under
cold running tap water for 3 min, frozen and freeze-dried to estimate DM disappearance.
Efﬂuent volume and pH were recorded daily throughout the entire experiment.
On day 8 of each period, liquid from each vessel was sampled at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8,
and 24 h to analyse for pH, VFA and NH3–N. Samples (3–4 ml) were taken from each
vessel’s sampling pipe into a sample tube. pH was immediately recorded and samples were
then decanted into two microcentrifuge tubes for VFA (1.1 ml of efﬂuent plus two drops of
concentrated orthophosphoric acid), and NH3–N (1 ml of efﬂuent plus 0.1 ml of 2 M HCl)
analyses.
From day 9, after replacement of the sample bags, the bottles for efﬂuent collection
were placed into a 4 ◦C water bath. On day 10, the nylon bags removed from the system
were squeezed without buffer, and the liquids were returned to the vessels. Solid-associated
bacteria (SAB) were extracted from the digesta residues left in the nylon bags by extracting
twice with 150 ml of saline solution (0.9% NaCl) for 5 min each using a Stomacher (Seward
Ltd., Thetford, UK). Total volumes of efﬂuents and vessel contents (containing the liquid
associated bacteria), and SAB extracts were recorded, and proportionally subsampled (af-
ter homogenisation at low speed) to make up 500 ml of composite samples, which were
preserved at −18 ◦C.
2.4. Estimation of microbial protein yield
The (15NH4)2SO4 was used as microbial marker. After thawing for approximately 24 h,
composite and background samples were gently mixed and 200 ml were transferred into a
G. Jaurena et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 118 (2005) 79–91 83
centrifuge ﬂask. Total bacteria samples were obtained from the samples as follows. Feed
residues were removed by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 10 min (4 ◦C). The supernatant
was removed carefully into another centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 30,000 × gfor 15 min
(4 ◦C) to remove bacteria from suspension. The supernatant from the second centrifugation
was discarded and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in saline solution, and centrifuged
at the higher speed as above. This was repeated once, and the resultant pellet was ﬁnally
resuspended in distilled water. A total solids sample was obtained in the same way, but
avoiding the ﬁrst 1500 × g centrifugation. The pellets were freeze-dried, weights were
recorded and the samples were ground with pestle and mortar.
Bacteria and total solids samples were analysed for total N content and 15N to esti-
mate microbial N enrichment using a mass spectrometer (ANCA/SL 20/20, PDZ Europe
Ltd., Crewe, Cheshire, UK). Bacterial protein yield (BPY, mg/day) was estimated as daily
bacterial yield (BY, g/day) multiplied by the bacterial pellet crude protein (CP) content.
Efﬁciency of bacterial DM (EBDMS) and protein (EBPS) synthesis per kilogram of organic
matter apparently digested (OMAD ) were estimated as BY and BPY divided by OMAD in
a 48-h period, respectively.
2.5. Estimation of digestibility variables
Digesta residues were immediately frozen after hand washing or stomaching, and freeze-
dried. Residues from days 8 and 9 of the incubation period were used to estimate 48-h DM
apparent digestibility (DMAD ), and were pooled and later analysed to determine residual
organic matter, N, neutral detergent ﬁbre (NDF), and acid detergent ﬁbre (ADF). Thus,
apparent (i.e. DMAD ,OMAD and N, NAD ) and true (i.e. NDF, NDFD and ADF, ADFD )
digestibilities were estimated over a 48-h period.
2.6. Analytical procedures
Freeze DM analysis was carried out by drying approximately 40 g of fresh silage in
a freeze-drier for 48–72 h until a constant weight was achieved. Neutral detergent ﬁbre
concentration was determined using amylase (Sigma-Aldrich Co. Ltd., Gillingham, UK,
No. A-1278; using 40 and 290 mg amylase per 5 l batch of neutral detergent solution for
silage and grain samples, respectively) and 2-ethoxyethanol (Van Soest et al., 1991). Acid
detergent ﬁbre concentration was determined according to the method of Van Soest and
Wine (1967) using the Fibretec System equipment (Tecator Ltd., Thornbury, Bristol, UK).
Both NDF and ADF results took residual ash content into account. All other analyses were
carried out on feed and residue samples as described by Lee et al. (2003).
2.7. Statistical analysis
Data were analysed (GenStat, 2000) according to a complete block design (blocking by
incubation) with a factorial arrangement (2 × 4) of forage species (GR or RC) and barley
grain inclusion (0, 150, 300 and 450 g/kg DM). Main effects (forage and barley grain in-
clusion), and the respective interactions were included in the model. Planned orthogonal
contrasts (linear and quadratic) were performed to examine the effects of level of grain

inclusion and statistical signiﬁcance was declared at P ≤ 0.05. Efﬂuent ammonia–N con-
centration was regressed on diet CP concentration.
3. Results
The chemical composition of the rolled barley grain (Table 1) was within the typical
range, although its starch concentration was above the expected value (MAFF, 1986). Silage
chemical composition (Table 1) indicated that GR silage had a moderate fermentation
(85 g/kg DM total fermentation acids, pH 4.2), with dominance of lactic acid yielding
organisms (80% lactic acid and a lactic/acetic ratio of 7). Similar characteristics were found
for RC silage, but considering that RC is a legume, its fermentation was superior to the GR
silage as evidenced by the ammonia–N concentrations (80 and 130 g/kg DM, respectively).
Both silages had similar concentrations of propionic acid, and concentrations of butyric
acid that were higher than is considered to be desirable (Harrison et al., 1994). Inclusion of
barley at 0, 150, 300 and 450 g/kg DM resulted in decreasing concentrations of CP in the
whole diet (139, 133, 126 and 120 g CP/kg DM for GR and 167, 156, 146 and 135 g CP/kg
DM for RC).
The RUSITEC efﬂuent characteristics were highly inﬂuenced by both silage type and
inclusion of grain (Table 2; Fig. 1). The efﬂuent of the RC silage had higher minimum
pH (GR = 5.6, RC = 6.7; P < 0.01), ammonia–N concentrations (GR = 150, RC = 166 mg/l;
P < 0.01) and minimum ammonia–N concentrations (GR = 104, RC = 122 mg/l; P < 0.01). In
contrast, the GR silage produced efﬂuents with higher acetic (GR = 55, RC = 51; P < 0.05),
propionic (GR = 22, RC = 19; P < 0.001), butyric (GR = 11, RC = 9; P < 0.001) and total
volatile fatty acids (GR = 88, RC = 79; P < 0.01) than RC silage.

Inclusion of rolled barley grain in the silages linearly (P < 0.001) reduced the mean
ammonia–N concentration in the efﬂuent as a consequence of the reduction in dietary
CP concentration (Fig. 2), and induced a quadratic reduction (P < 0.01) of the minimum
ammonia–N concentration. Both silage treatments with the highest rates of barley inclusion
(450 g barley/kg DM) led to NH3–N concentrations below 100 mg/l by 6 and 8 h of incu-
bation for GR and RC, respectively. Increasing the proportion of barley in the mixed diets
reduced linearly (P < 0.06) the efﬂuent acetic, propionic and total VFA concentrations, but
increased the butyric acid concentration (P < 0.06), which brought about a reduction of the
glucogenic:lipogenic VFA ratio (propionate/(acetate + butyrate), P < 0.001).
Red clover silage had lower (P < 0.001) NDF (GR = 0.67, RC = 0.59 g/g) and ADF
(GR = 0.69; RC = 0.58 g/g) digestibilities than GR silages. In contrast, NAD was higher
for RC than for GR silages (GR = 0.85, RC = 0.88; P < 0.001). Inclusion of barley grain
linearly increased (P < 0.001) DMAD ,OMAD and NAD .
Daily BY and BPY were also associated with barley inclusion (Table 2). Similarly,
EBDMS was linearly associated (P < 0.05) with the rate of barley inclusion (Fig. 3), but EBPS
increased linearly only up to the inclusion of 300 g barley/kg DM for RC silage, plateauing
at this point (P Sp×quad < 0.05). There was no clear response in EBPS to increasing the rate
of barley inclusion in the GR silage treatments.
4. Discussion
The apparent digestibilities of feed DM, OM and N all increased linearly with barley
grain inclusion because of the addition of a highly digestible concentrate. In contrast, ﬁbre
fraction digestibilities were not affected by grain inclusion, but there was a difference
between GR and RC silages as expected (Van Soest, 1994).

Grass silage diets produced slightly more acidic efﬂuents than RC silage diets, and
despite the wide variation in fermentable carbohydrates available among treatments,
the drop in pH between the initial state and the minimum value was small, and pH
was always above the threshold value of 6, below which microbial activity may be
limited (Dawson and Allison, 1988). In accordance with these results, GR silage led
to higher total VFA, acetic, propionic and butyric acid concentrations in the efﬂu-
ent, as well as lower mean and minimum ammonia–N concentrations than RC silage
treatments, which is in agreement with the lower CP concentrations of the GR silage
diets.
Inclusion of barley grain linearly reduced the efﬂuent concentrations of acetic, propi-
onic and butyric acids, hence reducing the proportion of substrate carbons diverted to-
wards fermentation products. As the proportion of carbon diverted to gaseous products
was not expected to vary signiﬁcantly among treatments; these results suggest that in-
creasing the availability of starch in the substrate induced an increasing accumulation of
starch by rumen microorganisms. Bacteria accumulating, rather than hydrolysing and fer-
menting, the starch consequently reduced the glucogenic:lipogenic fatty acid ratio. Ru-

men microorganisms have shown the capability of storing large amounts of cytoplasmatic
polysaccharide storage granules (Russell and Wallace, 1988) and using them when am-
monia concentrations increase (Van Kessel and Russel, 1997). Furthermore, the addition
of concentrates to a diet can lead to an increase in the number of rumen protozoa (Hynd
et al., 1985), which can account for between 20 and 45% of the amylolytic activity in
the rumen (McAllister and Cheng, 1996). The rumen protozoa population regulates the
rate of starch digestion (McAllister and Cheng, 1996) and reduces the post-feeding drop
in pH (Veira et al., 1983) by engulﬁng starch granules (Coleman, 1986) and reducing
the population of amylolytic bacteria through predation (Kurihara et al., 1968). However,
both the RUSITEC system and nylon bag pore size have been shown to reduce protozoa
populations (Carro et al., 1995). Unfortunately, lack of sufﬁcient bacterial sample pre-
vented us from analysing its starch content in the current study, and we can only speculate
that rumen bacteria stored a signiﬁcant proportion of the starch supplied by the barley
grain.
It appears that EBPS in GR treatments was N-limited as the minimum ammonia–N con-
centration at the highest rate of barley inclusion was close to the breakpoint (50 mg/l)
suggested by Satter and Slyter (1974), below which “the efﬁciency of ammonia utilisa-
tion drops precipitously”. Other authors have also reported positive microbial responses
to higher rumen ammonia–N concentrations (Odle and Schaefer, 1987; Van Soest, 1994).
Furthermore, it is apparent that amylolytic bacteria growth is dependent on the availabil-
ity of preformed AA (Russell et al., 1992). Thus, the lack of response to starch addi-
tion in GR silage treatments could be associated with the expected low concentrations
of the remaining AA and peptides in GR silage, as suggested by the silage ammonia–N
concentration.
The EBPS for RC silage increased with barley inclusion to a rate of 300 g barley/kg DM,
which represents a 37% increase. No clear trend in EBPS was observed with increasing rates
of barley inclusion for GR treatments, although at similar dietary CP concentrations the GR
mix diets led to higher ammonia–N concentrations in the efﬂuent than the RC counterparts.
This could be interpreted as a consequence of a reduction in proteolysis of RC proteins as
reported by Hatﬁeld and Muck (1999), although GR silage had a signiﬁcantly higher NPN
content than RC silage. Additionally, the autocorrelation between dietary CP and starch
concentrations prevents the interpretation of changes in the ammonia–N concentration of
the RUSITEC efﬂuent as an indication of improvement of EBPS (Chamberlain et al., 1993;
Dhiman and Satter, 1994).
5. Conclusions
This study has shown that the efﬁciency of bacterial protein synthesis can be substantially
increased by supplying rolled barley grain as a source of rumen-degradable carbohydrates
for RC silages. However, no similar response was observed for diets based on GR silage.
It also appears that despite a greater availability of rumen-degradable carbohydrates at
higher rates of barley supplementation, rumen microorganisms reduced the amount fer-
mented, probably by diverting a higher proportion of the available starch to their own
reserves.
