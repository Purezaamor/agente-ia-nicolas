19.1 INTRODUCTION 
Ensiling is a method of moist forage preservation whereby lactic acids bacteria 
(LAB) ferment water-soluble carbohydrates to organic acids, mainly lactic acid, 
under anaerobic conditions. As a result, the pH decreases, inhibiting detrimental 
anaerobes, and so the moist forage is preserved. This is actually lactic acid 
solid-state fermentation of forage crops. It is similar to the fermentation which 
takes place during making of sauerkraut. This chapter briefly describes 
biochemical, microbiological and technological aspects of ensiling. 
19.2 The need for forage preservation 
Cattle, especially dairy cows, require a continuous supply of high-quality feed 
throughout the year. Forage crops comprise a substantial portion of their ration 
and they serve mainly as roughage. They supply fiber, energy, proteins and 
minerals which ruminants need for their maintenance and production. However, 
the availability of forage crops in many cases is seasonal. In temperate climate 
zones crops and grazing pastures are not available during the cold winters; in 
semi-arid areas they are not available during the dry season; in other areas, 
rain and wet soil interfere with the harvesting operations. Therefore, it is best 
to harvest the crops at the optimal time when yields and nutritional quality 
peak and weather conditions are favorable, and to preserve them for the rest 
of the year. 
In modem agriculture, both forage crops and dairy farms are industrialized, 
which means that large quantities of forage crops are produced on large areas 
for large herds of dairy and beef cattle. In such practice, large quantities of 
crops are produced during the harvesting season which must be preserved in 
order to ensure continuous supply of forage throughout the year. Preservation 
of forage crops enables to keep reserve stocks on the farms, and sometimes 
shipping forages from growing areas to remote cattle farms. If no preservation 
action is taken the crops eventually spoil and rot.

19.2.1. Preservation 
The purpose of preservation is to inhibit spoilage factors in any given food 
commodity. Food spoilers include microorganisms, insects, enzymes and chemical 
reactions (e.g., oxidation, browning). Preservation is achieved by applying 
conditions or treatments that inhibit the development and activity of spoiling 
factors. The abiotic conditions which affect microorganisms (as well as insects 
and enzymes) include temperature, pH, the presence/absence of air (aerobic/ 
anaerobic conditions) and the availability of moisture (expressed as water activity, 
Aw). In general, some microorganisms can tolerate cold temperatures as low 
as 0~ and they are referred to as psychrotropic, some are mesophylic (optimal 
temperature for their development is 20-40~ and some are thermophylic (can 
act at 50-60~ Most microorganisms require a high level of moisture (water- 
activity, Aw>0.90), but some, especially yeasts and molds can tolerate drier 
conditions (as low as Aw--0.70). Molds are also strict aerobes, which means 
that they require air for their activity. They can tolerate more acidic conditions 
than most bacteria. Microorganisms in any given environment are in a constant 
competition and those for which the entire abiotic conditions are optimal become 
the dominant microbial population, whereas other groups are suppressed. 
Food preservation aims at changing the ideal conditions for the spoiling agents. 
Preservation alternatives include heat treatments, cold and frozen storage, drying, 
acidification, modified atmospheres, irradiation and application of preserving 
chemicals. 
Preservation of forage crops should fit the large bulk of the crops and should 
be cost effective. The most common methods of forage crop preservation 
include hay making and ensiling. The former is based on sun drying, while 
ensiling is based on anaerobic lactic acid solid-state fermentation. For both 
methods, the crops should be harvested at the optimal stage of maturation 
when their nutritional quality is best. A delay of even a week may lower their 
digestibility for ruminants because of advanced lignification of the cell wall. 
Hay making is suitable especially for thin crops which can dry quickly in 
sunny seasons. Losses of nutritional materials occur during hay making due 
continued respiration in the drying plants (Figure 1), and detachment of dry 
leaves which become brittle. In forage legumes (such as alfalfa, clover and 
vetch) the protein is present in the leaves and therefore, they are most susceptible 
to nutritional losses during racking and tedding of the drying crop. Rain is yet 
another factor which may interfere with drying or cause damage during storage. 
Silage can be made of almost any crop and is less weather dependent. It is 
more consistent than hay in nutrient content. However, silage making requires 
high capital investment in storage structures (silos) and machinery.

19.3 RETROSPECTIVE OF SILAGE MAKING 
Ensiling has been practiced since ancient times. This is evidenced by an Egyptian 
mural that dates back to 1000-1500 BC that shows ensiling of whole-crop 
cereals. Silos were also found in ancient Carthage. Little is known about silage 
making in the Medieval Ages. Interest in ensiling of forage crops was renewed 
in Europe in the 19 th century because it was similar to production of sauerkraut. 
In 1877 a French farmer, Auguste Goffart, published his results on ensiling of 
chopped whole-crop forage corn. This publication had a great impact on spreading 
the ensiling technology in Europe and North America. A setback to ensiling 
was afflicted with the publication of the book Sweet Silage (Fry, 1885) who 
advocated that silages reach 50~ before sealing in order to eliminate detrimental 
microorganisms. This practice which was used till the 1960s resulted in substantial 
losses in nutritional constituents. When more knowledge on silage biochemistry 
and microbiology was attained this mal practice was abandoned. In the 1920s 
Virtanen from Finland developed direct acidification of moist grass silage by 
using a mixture of hydrochloric and sulfuric acids. This process helped to 
reduce fermentation losses and proteolysis in such silages. For this achievement 
he was awarded the only Noble Prize in Agriculture so far. In the 20 th century 
tower and horizontal silos were developed as well as plastic sheeting for sealing. 
The recent trend is to use big plastic bales and sleeves for storing silage 
which permit flexibility in storage and use. Machinery that include harvesters, 
choppers and silage unloaders were developed. Numerous silage additives were

that improve the ensiling fermentation and preservation and in some cases also

The current production of silage in Europe and North America is estimated at 
200 million tons of dry matter, as compared with 300 million tons of hay. The 
estimated areas of crops harvested for silage total 43 million hectares (Wilkinson 
et al., 2003). 
19.3.1. The ensiling process 
The biochemical and microbiological events that occur during ensiling can be 
divided into four distinct stages: 
Initial-aerobic, during filling of the silo and immediately after sealing, 
while air is still present between the plant particles and the pH is similar 
to that in the fresh crop (6.0-6.5). During this stage plant respiration 
and proteolysis continue, as well as activity of aerobic microorganisms. 
, Fermentation, lasting several days to several weeks after oxygen is depleted, 
during which various lactic acid bacteria (LAB) develop and convert 
water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) to organic acids, mainly lactic acid. 
As a result the pH decreases to 3.8-5.0. 
. Stable, when relatively few changes occur in the sealed silage, given no 
air penetrates into the silage. 
. Unloading for feeding, the silage is re-exposed to air and aerobic 
microorganism are re-activated and spoilage processes begin. 
Each of these phases are characterized by biochemical and microbiological 
processes which are described in the following paragraphs. 
19.3.2. Biochemical processes during ensiling 
The desired process during ensiling is the conversion of simple sugars (water- 
soluble carbohydrates, WSC) to organic acids, mainly lactic acid, through lactic 
acid fermentation. The acids suppress undesirable microbial activity and preserve 
the silage. The composition of the ensiled crop should promote LAB during 
ensiling. The crops composition depends on crop type, stage of maturity at 
harvest, fertilization and extent of wilting. The requirements for successful 
ensiling include adequate dry matter (DM) and WSC content that should be 
between 300 and 400 g kg -1 and at least 30-50 g kg -1 DM, respectively. The 
buffering capacity of the ensiled biomass should be low. The buffering capacity 
is a measure of a chemical/biochemical system to resist decrease in pH even 
when acids are present. High ash (mineral) and protein content contribute to

Warm temperatures and high moisture content of the plants enhance respiration,

necessary for the ensiling fermentation. Respiration and aerobic microbial activity 
at this stage results in heating (temperature might rise to >40~ and loss of 
nutrients in the form of CO2. Excessive heating leads to browning and Maillard 
type reactions which further decrease protein and WSC availability of the silage.

Proteolysis is yet another process that occurs during this phase. It results in 
breakdown of proteins to shorter peptides, free amino acids and eventually 
ammonia. The ammonia is formed by microbial activity. Proteolysis continues 
until the pH of the ensiled crop decreases below 5.0; it is affected by both 
temperature and moisture content of the herbage; high tannin content protects 
proteins against proteolysis. The extent of proteolysis might reach 20% of the 
total protein. Proteolysis reduces the amount of by-pass protein (protein that 
by-passes the rumen and reaches the intestine) which is important for the 
ruminant nutrition. 
Both respiration and proteolysis are undesirable processes in which losses occur 
and they should be minimized by rapid and efficient ensiling followed by a fast 
decrease in pH.

storage, little changes occur, given no air penetrates into the silage when the 
silage is opened for feeding its face is exposed to air which penetrates 1-2 m 
inside the silage. Dormant aerobic microorganisms, such as yeasts and molds, 
are reactivated and their activity results in heating and losses. 
19.3.2.1. Losses 
Ensiling should preserve the crop so that its nutritional value is as close as 
possible to the value of the fresh crop. However, as mentioned above, losses 
in DM and energy occur at various stages of ensiling. Dry matter losses occur 
due to the conversion of organic matter (usually sugars) to carbon dioxide. 
Energy losses are usually smaller than DM losses because energy is retained in 
the fermentation products. The extent of losses depend on the crop's composition 
and on silage management, especially on compaction, sealing and rate of unloading 
for feeding. They include respiration and proteolysis losses in the initial stage, 
fermentation losses (depending on type of fermentation), losses that occur 
during feedout when the silage is exposed to air, and effluent losses in moist 
silages. In concrete bunker silos the top and parts adjacent to the walls are the 
most susceptible to air penetration and spoilage. Accepted levels of accumulated 
ensiling losses are around 10% of the DM which are considered inevitable; in 
poorly managed silages losses can accumulate to much higher values with 
further effects of spoiled feed on animal performance and health. Table 1 
summarizes the potential sources for losses (Zimmer, 1979).

19.3.3. Silage microbiology 
Silage making is based on solid-state lactic acid fermentation whereby LAB 
metabolize WSC under anaerobic conditions to gain energy (ATP) for their 
growth. Enzymes that participate in the fermentation ensure a balanced oxidation- 
reduction potential. The fermentation pathways depend on the enzymatic makeup 
of the various LAB, on the available substrates and on the presence/absence of 
oxygen. The fermentation products include organic acids, mainly lactic acid 
and also some neutral products such as ethanol. As a result the pH decreases 
and the forage is preserved. Hence it is desirable that LAB become the dominant 
microbial population in the silage. 
The microbial population of the standing crop comprises a variety of aerobic 
microorganisms such as yeasts, molds, enterobactria as well as endospores of 
bacilli and clostridia. LAB comprise only a small fraction of the plants epiphytic 
microflora. Once the crop is chopped the number of LAB increases x l000 or 
more. It is hypothesized that on the standing crop LAB are in a dormant state 
because of various peroxides which are present under the aerobic conditions 
and are toxic to LAB. During chopping, superoxide dismutase and manganese 
ions (Mn +2) are released in the plants sap which detoxify the peroxides and 
thus enhance their multiplication and activity in the ensiled crop. If conditions 
in the silage are favorable LAB become the dominant microflora by exclusion 
competition. These conditions include anaerobiosis, temperatures between 25 
and 40~ and adequate DM and WSC contents. 
19.3.3.1. Lactic acid fermentation 
The LAB are a large group comprising several genera of Gram-positive, non- 
motile, non sporing micro-aerophilic bacteria all of which produce lactic acid.

The genera associated with silage include Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus, 
Enterococcus, Lactococcus and Streptococcus. The distinction of different LAB 
is based on morphology, ability to grow at different temperatures and under 
different aerobic and acidic conditions. Based on fermentation products, two 
groups can be distinguished: homo and hetero-fermentative. The former produces 
mainly lactic acid during anaerobic fermentation from hexoses (>85%) while 
the latter produces additional products such as acetic acid, CO2 and ethanol. 
Leuconostoc and some Lactobacilli are heterofermentative while the other genera 
are homofermentative LAB. It should be noticed that under aerobic conditions 
also homofermentative LAB ferment hexoses to both lactic and acetic acids. 
More modem classification and taxonomy of LAB are based on polygenetic 
relationships established by using ribosomal RNA sequence. However this 
classification and taxonomy is not relevant to the current description.

Some lactobacilli of the homolactic group (sometimes referred to as facultatively 
heterofermentative) posses both aldolase and phosphoketolase and therefore, 
they are able to ferment also pentose sugars. The result of pentose fermentation 
depends on the redox potential: under anaerobic conditions lactic acid and 
ethanol are formed while under aerobic condition acetic acid is produced along 
with lactic acid. 
The obligate heterolactic LAB ferment pentose and hexose sugars to lactic 
acid and acetic acid, ethanol and carbon dioxide. The fermentation products 
depend on the redox potential in the silage, the type of fermented sugar and 
the enzymatic make up of the specific LAB. 
The homolactic fermentation is more efficient in terms of silage preservation 
because CO2 represents losses of nutrients; ethanol has little preservation efficiency 
in the silage and acetic acid is weaker as compared with lactic acid (pK 4.8 
vs. 3.8, respectively). However, it should be mentioned that acetic acid inhibits 
aerobic yeasts and molds while lactic acid can be assimilated by the fungi. 
Therefore, it is desirable to have some acetic acid (and other volatile fatty 
acids such as propionic and butyric acids) in the silage which protect it upon 
aerobic exposure during feedout.

The lactic acid fermentation in the silage comprises a succession of LAB activity. 
In the initial aerobic phase, when air is still present between the silage particles 
and the pH is close to neutral Leuconostoc and Enterococci are active. At this 
stage the temperature in the silage rises to 35-45~ due to residual respiration 
activity and aerobic fermentation. Such temperatures are adequate for Enterococci. 
When the pH decreases below 5.0 and air is depleted these genera are replaced 
by Lactobacilli and Pediococci which are more acid tolerant. The latter produce 
more lactic acid in the silage and the pH decreases further to values of 3.6 in 
some silages, and then their activity ceases. In the beginning of the fermentation 
the total number of LAB in the silage increases to 108-109 CFU g-l, but when 
the silage stabilizes their numbers decrease gradually to 105-106 CFU g-1. 
The LAB species which are mostly associated with silage fermentation include 
L. casei, L. plantarum, L. pentosus, L. buchneri, L. fermentum, P. acidilactici, 
P. pentosaceus, Leuc. Mesentroides, Ent. faecium and Lac. Lactis. 
19.3.3.2. Other microorganisms relevant to silage 
Different microorganisms which originate from the standing crop are active at 
various stages of the ensiling process. The type of microorganisms and the 
duration of their activity depend on silage composition and the abiotic conditions 
prevailing in the silage. 
Enterobacteria are Gram-negative, rod shaped, facultatively anaerobes which 
are indigenous to the intestine of mono-gastric animals. They are present in 
relatively large numbers (up to 10 4 g-l) in grass crops. Their numbers increase 
to 108-101~ g-1 in the initial aerobic stage of ensiling. In well-prepared silages 
they usually disappear when the pH decreases below 5.0. Their fermentation 
of hexoses results in a mixture of acetic and formic acids, ethanol and butandiol 
and gases (CO2 and H2). They produce amines and ammonia from amino 
acids by decarboxylation and deamination. Some Enterobacteria possess endotoxin 
which is detrimental to the fed animals and can cause mastiffs and diarrhea in 
susceptible young calves. Enterobacteria in silage are undesirable because they 
compete with LAB on carbon sources and result in ensiling losses and spoilage. 
However, because of their ability to reduce nitrates to nitrite and nitric oxide 
which inhibit clostridia, they play an important role in moist silages in which 
clostridia may develop. 
Clostridia are Gram-positive, endspore-forming strictly anaerobic rods. Some 
species ferment carbohydrates and they are referred to as saccharolytic (e.g., 
C. tyrobutyricum and C. butyricum), and some ferment proteins (proteolytic, 
e.g.C, butulinum) and many ferment both (e.g., C. sporogenes). Saccharolytic 
or dual species are associated with silage. The importance of clostridia in

silage has two aspects: clostridial vegetative cells result in spoilage, DM and 
energy losses. C. tyrobutiricum ferments lactic acid to the weaker butyric 
acid, and as a result the pH increases, which triggers further spoilage. This 
process is referred to as 'secondary fermentation'. The intake of butyric silages 
by ruminants is low. Butyric fermentation occurs mainly in moist silages made 
of crops with high buffeting capacity. The second aspect pertains to clostridial 
spores which find their way to the alimentary tract of the cattle and are shed 
in the manure which eventually contaminates the udder. Thus, heat resistant 
spores of C. tyrobutiricum may enter the milk, germinate in ripe cheese and 
produce gases and cause a defect called 'late blowing'. 
Therefore, it is desirable to inhibit clostridia in silage as much as possible. 
Control of clostridia depends on the crops ensiling potential (a function of DM 
content and the ratio WSC: Buffeting capacity). In general, rapid lactic acid 
build up followed by pH decrease to below 4.2 and wilting to above 300 g 
kg -1 DM reduce the chance of butyric silages, as clostridia needs higher pH 
values and high moisture content for its development (Aw>0.97). A minimal 
concentration of nitrates (1-2 g N-NO 3 kg -1 DM) in the silage which is reduced 
to NO also inhibits clostridia. 
Clostridium botulinum, a protein fermenting species, produces a very potent 
neuro-toxin, which is fatal to animals and humans. This bacterium may contaminate 
the ruminants ration through occasional carcasses of rodents or birds found 
sometimes in broiler litter or from grazing pastures fertilized with manure. In 
Israel, most cattle are vaccinated to control this problem. 
Yeasts are single-cell eukaryotes. During the anaerobic phases of ensiling they 
ferment sugars and produce ethanol and CO2. Under aerobic conditions lactate- 
assimilating yeasts become active and they result in increase in pH and further 
aerobic spoilage. They are the most important spoilers of silage upon aerobic 
exposure, and in most silages they initiate the aerobic deterioration. Yeasts can 
be controlled in silage by volatile fatty acids (VFA, such as acetic, propionic 
and butyric acids) added directly or produced by selected bacteria (e.g., the 
heterofermentative L. buchneri) or by antimycotic chemicals. If the silage 
undergoes homolactic fermentation it is more susceptible to aerobic spoilage 
by fungi, because of the lack of inhibiting VFA. The most important yeast 
genera associated with silage include Candida, Hansenula, Pichia and 
Saccharomyces. 
Filamentous fungi (molds) are obligate aerobic microorganisms which produce 
micellium in the silage. They develop in the silage upon aerobic exposure, if 
the sealing is inadequate or during feedout. The most susceptible parts of 
silage to molding are the top and near the walls in bunker silos, or the outer

the silage, and high losses. Certain molds may produce mycotoxins in the 
silage which are hazardous to the animals and humans. The mold genera associated

Acetic acid bacteria include acetobacter and gluconoacetobacter which sometimes 
initiate aerobic spoilage in corn silages. They are obligate aerobes which oxidize 
ethanol to acetic acid. The former can oxidize acetic and lactic acids to CO2 
and water while the latter cannot. 
19.3.4. Practical aspects of silage making 
Silage making involves a series of actions which start at the harvesting of the 
crop and ends at the feed-out step. Silage making is a sophisticated preservation 
method of forage crops which usually requires infrastructure (silos) and machinery. 
It is important that all actions should be performed in the optimal precise 
manner in order to insure the success of ensiling and preservation. Negligence 
at Critical Control Points might result in failure, spoilage and losses. Synchronization 
between the actions is a key factor for success. The steps involved in silage 
making include: harvesting, wilting, chopping, enrichment, transportation, 
compaction, sealing, feed-out. 
19.3.4.1. Crops for silage 
Forage crops are ensiled in various parts of the world with various climate 
conditions. In every geographical region crops which are best adapted to the 
local conditions and give the highest profits to the farmers are grown. In 
general, forage crops should be harvested at the time point when their yields, 
ensiling properties and nutritional value are at peak. The major forage crops 
for silage worldwide include maize (Zea mays), alfalfa (Medicago sativa) and 
various grasses. In some parts of the world various cultivars of sorghum 
(Sorghum bicolor) are used for silage; In Israel wheat (Triticum aestivum) is 
popular. Corn is an optimal crop for silage with yields up to 20 tons of DM 
per hectare, with high nutritional value for ruminants (its digestibility is over 
700 g kg -1 and it also serves as an energy source due to the starch in the 
grains, with net energy for lactation energy content of 1.5 Mcal kg-1). It is 
harvest for silage at about the half dent stage of maturity (which means that 
half of the starch in the grains hardened), at 300-400 g kg -1 DM and it can be 
ensiled very well without wilting. The final pH of well preserved corn silage is 
3.7-4.0. 
Sorghum is suitable for semi-arid areas because of its drought resistance and 
is less fastidious than corn with regard to fertilization. Its yields are 10-15

Alfalfa, a perennial legume, is very popular in area with abundant water. It is 
harvested for silage several times during the growing season at 10-20% bloom 
when nutritional value is maximal. If the alfalfa is harvested at a later stage its 
NDF (neutral detergent fiber) content increases and its nutritional value decreases.

make it difficult to ensile. In addition, its DM content at harvest is too low 
(only 150-250 g kg -1) and therefore, in order to avoid effluent losses and 
clostridial fermentation, alfalfa should be extensively wilted to about 500 g kg- 
1 DM. Wilting also increases the relative WSC content in the crop. Such silage 
is referred to as 'haylage' in which the fermentation is slow and the final pH 
values are 4.5-5.0. 
Other forage legumes that can be ensiled include clover (Trifolium spp., red 
and white), field peas, vetch (Vicia sativa) and sulla (Hedysarum coronarium). 
Perennial grasses are common crops for ensiling and they include temperate 
and warm (tropical) species which are numerous. The former have a higher 
nutritional value. Examples for grasses in temperate climate zones are Italian 
ryegrass (Loluim multiflorum), perennial ryegrass (Lolium perene), cocksfoot 
(Dactylis glomerata), timothy (Phleum pretense) and meadow fescue (Festuca 
pratensis). Examples for tropical grasses include guineagrass (Panicum maximum), 
Bermuda grass (Cynodon spp.) and elephantgrass (napier grass, Pennisetum 
purpureum). As the grasses mature the rumen digestibility of the grass decreases 
because of lignification process. Grasses contain enough WSC needed for the 
ensiling fermentation, and their buffering capacity is low. 
Grass and legume are sometimes co-ensiled because they can be grown and 
managed together in the field, the mixtures have improved ensiling properties 
and higher nutritional value, as compared with silages made from the separate 
crops. In Israel it is not possible to grow mixed crops in the same field because 
of religious prohibition. 
Small grain cereals include wheat, barley, oat, rye and triticale. They are used 
as forage crops for ensiling and hay in areas with a short rain season. In 
Israel wheat for silage is very popular. It is grown on winter rain and harvested 
in spring at the milk ripening stage. Its yields at this stage are around are 
10 tons of DM per hectare and its DM digestibility is 640-680 g kg -1.

19.3.4.2. Harvesting 
The time of harvesting forage crops for silage affects the quality of the silage 
and the profit obtained by both crop growers and cattle farmers. The decision 
when to harvest is influenced by factors such as expected yields, nutritional 
value of the crops, ensiling characteristics and weather conditions. The later 
the harvest is the higher the yields. However, as mentioned earlier, as plants 
mature their digestibility decreases because of lignification processes. In addition, 
harvesting at a later maturity stage might result in a biomass which is too dry 
and its WSC content is too low. Plants which are too dry are resilient and 
therefore, difficult to compact in the silo and air can penetrate more easily. 
Fermentation of dry crops is slow, resulting in high ensiling losses and molding 
upon aerobic exposure. On the other hand, harvesting at an early stage may 
lead to moist plants that result in effluent and clostridial fermentation. Therefore, 
the time window for harvesting crops for silage is limited. For cereals the 
status of the starch in the grains is an indicator of maturity; the milk stage of 
maturity (when the grain is still semi-liquid) is recommended. For legumes and 
grasses the maturity is determined according to the extent of blooming (flowering). 
Such crops should be harvested between early and mid-bloom (10-50% flowering). 
The cutting height should be adjusted in order to avoid mixing with soil and 
debris which can damage the knives and decrease the nutritional value of the 
harvested biomass. The lower parts of the plants are inferior in quality and 
therefore, a higher cut of about 7 cm above ground is recommended.

19.3.4.3. Wilting 
Wilting refers to drying of the crops in the field before they are picked up. As 
mentioned above plants that are harvested with a high moisture content should 
be wilted in order to achieve the desirable DM content required for successful 
ensiling. Wilted plants should have an appropriate "thin" structure that enable 
rapid loss of moisture. Weather conditions should be favorable for rapid drying. 
Legumes and grasses are usually wilted before ensiling. In Israel, wheat for 
silage is usually wilted for a few hours and this practice is preferred over 
direct harvesting because of efficiency considerations of the harvesting combines. 
19.3.4.4. Chopping 
Chopping refers to the action of cutting the plants to small pieces in order to 
enable their compaction (consolidation) in the silo with subsequent removal of 
air. Chopping increases the surface area of the biomass and some WSC-rich 
juice is released to the outer surface which enhances the lactic acid fermentation. 
The recommended particle length of the cuts is 10-30 mm; shorter pieces 
may result in excessive effluent and they may not supply long enough fibers 
which are necessary for the rumen functionality; pieces which are too long 
may inhibit adequate compaction in the silo, especially of dry plants which 
resist compaction, which enables air penetration into the silage and spoilage. 
Chopping is usually carried out at pick-up and the chopped particles are blown 
into the shipping wagon which follows the pick-up. As mentioned in the Microbiology 
section, chopping increases the LAB population in the ensiled biomass. It is 
possible to apply silage additives at this step which enables adequate mixing. 
The chopped forage is transported to the silo in trucks or wagons. Transportation 
costs depend on the distance of the field from the silo and truck size. 
19.3.4.5. Filling and compaction 
Filling should be fast in order to exclude air quickly and avoid respiration and 
aerobic losses. It may take several days to fill a large silo. Bunker silos are 
filled in a series of slopped wedges which are compacted by wheel tractors 
which move slowly back and forth continuously during the filling operation. 
In tower silos compaction is accomplished by the weight of the forage. The 
DM density of a silage depends on silage DM content, silo dimensions and 
geometry and the intensity of compaction. The drier the biomass is, it is more 
resilient and the more difficult it is to compact it. The estimated DM density 
of a well-compacted wheat silage at 350 g kg -1 DM in a horizontal bunker silo 
is 230 kg m -3. Other density values found in the literature vary between 100 
and 430 g kg DM m -3.

19.3.4.6. Sealing 
Sealing protects the top layer against air penetration. Sealing of bunker and 
stack silages is usually accomplished with polyethylene sheeting of various 
thickness (0.1-0.2 mm), and the thicker it is less air penetrates. The plastic 
sheeting is susceptible to physical tears, sometimes caused by birds and animals. 
It is possible to protect the sheeting with a layer of paper pulp or other materials 
that also keep it from flapping. Used tires are also spread over the sheeting to 
anchor it to the silage. 
19.3.4. 7. Unloading 
In horizontal silos the face of the silage is exposed to air during unloading and 
aerobic spoilage is enhanced. It is possible to use scrapers or front-mounted 
bucket tractors for unloading. The latter tends to produce cracks in the face 
through which air can penetrate deeper into the silage, as compared with a 
smooth face obtained with a scraper. Block cutters leave the unloaded silage 
intact, and therefore, the blocks can be stored for several days. Experimental 
data reveals that air penetrates 1-2 m deep into the silage from the face. In 
order to minimize the time that the face is exposed to air it is recommended to 
renew it every three days in wide bunker silos, or to unload a depth of 20-30 
cm everyday. The plastic cover should not be removed further than necessary 
during the daily unloading of the silage. In tower silos the unloading can be 
designed from the bottom or the top of the silo.

19.3.4.8. Ensiling facilities 
Silage facilities include various types of silos in which the ensiled crops are 
fermented and stored until used for feeding. Two types of stationary silos can 
be used: tower silos made of concrete or metal, and horizontal bunker silos 
made of concrete floor and walls. The construction of such silos requires

large capital investments. Silo size should be designed according to feeding 
needs and the expected daily removal of silage from it. Other ensiling facilities 
include heavy duty plastic sleeves into which the chopped crop is pressed by 
a special machine. Stack silage consists of a long heap of chopped and 
consolidated crop which is prepared on the ground or on plastic sheeting and 
sealed with plastic sheeting. It is also possible to store silage in 0'5-0.75 ton 
bales. Bales are prepared by wrapping four to eight layers of polyethylene 
stretch around the units by a special machine. The crop (usually grass) is not 
chopped, and bale silage is sensitive to aerobic exposure. Round or square 
bales are available and they enable flexible use as these relatively small units 
that can be opened one at a time according to needs and they can be brought 
to pasture areas. In Israel, the recent trend is to construct large feeding centers 
which serve several farms and supply total mixed rations (TMR) which are 
formulated according to requirements of the animals and the feed which is 
available. Such feeding centers operate with relatively large bunker silos which 
contain several thousands tons of silage with a very fast turnover. 
Experimental data reveals that it is also possible to store silage in small plastic 
bags (10-20 kg ) which can be used by small holder cattle farmers in Africa 
and other areas that cannot afford the facilities required for common ensiling. 
19.3.5. Additives for silage 
Silage making is a multi-step process which is affected by many factors which 
are related to the characteristics of the crops, to management practices and to 
weather conditions. As mentioned before the crops composition should be adequate 
and allow a rapid development of LAB under anaerobic conditions. Ensiling 
steps should be synchronized and avoid delays which are detrimental to the 
process. Cooperative weather conditions are necessary for undisturbed harvest 
and rapid wilting. These factors are not always optimal for the ensiling process 
and cannot be controlled. For these reasons, various additives for silage have 
been developed which should improve the silage fermentation and preservation 
and its nutritive value. Some farmers feel that it is beneficial to use additives 
"just to be on the safe side". It should be emphasized that additives may be 
helpful under suboptimal conditions, however, they cannot compensate for poor 
crop quality or inadequate silage management. 
In the beginning of the 20th century farmers in Europe used molasses to 
enhance the WSC content in low sugar crops. In 1929-~933 Virtanen from 
Finland developed the mineral acid additives that decrease the pH rapidly and 
arrest any microbial activity and thus preserve very moist silages of northern 
Europe (For that he won the only Nobel Prize in Agricultural Sciences so far). 
Since then, many more additives were developed, both chemical and biological.

 Pandey A, Soccol CR, Larroche C. (Ed) Springer, 2008
The latter comprise bacterial inoculants and enzymes. It was estimated that in 
2001 silage additives were used in over 50% of the silages produced in the 
USA (Hoard's Dairyman). 
Silage additives can be classified according to their effect on the silage fermentation. 
The additive categories include fermentation inhibitors, fermentation enhancers, 
inhibitors of aerobic deterioration, nutritional additives and moisture absorbents. 
Additives should be used according to the specific conditions and needs which 
exist in a given silage. Additives should be effective, easy to use, non-corrosive 
to machinery, non-pollutant, should not be detrimental to human or animal 
health and their use should be price effective. The following paragraphs shortly 
describe the various classes of additives. 
19.3.5.1. Fermentation inhibitors 
It is aimed at partial or full inhibition of the microbial activity in the silage. 
These additives include sulfuric acid, formic acid and formaldehyde and they 
are used in moist silages resulting in a rapid decrease in pH. Their rate of 
application is 2-4 liter per ton (wet weight). Formic acid and formaldehyde 
bind proteins and so increase by-pass protein in ruminants. Formic acid is 
corrosive to machinery, increases effluent, it is volatile and inconvenient to use. 
19.3.5.2. Fermentation enhancers 
It is aimed at speeding up the ensiling fermentation and so minimize losses. 
Such additives include molasses, bacterial inoculants and enzymes. Molasses 
directly add fermentable sugars and can be used in low-sugar crops. Bacterial 
inoculants comprise mainly homofermentative LAB, single or multi-strain. Species 
that are most often used include Lacobacillus plantarum, Pediococcus pentosaceus 
and Enterococcus faecium. They are applied at 105-106 microorganisms per g 
crop, and when established in the silage produce rapidly lactic acid which 
results in fast decrease in pH. In some studies it has been observed that 
feeding silages inoculated with specific LAB strains also improved animal 
performance (a probiotic effect?). This is manifested by a 5-10% increase in 
feed intake, weight gain, milk yield and feed efficiency. 
Enzymes include cell-wall degrading enzymes such as cellulases, hemicellulases 
and pectinases from fungal origin. Their optimal activity is at pH 4.5-5.0 which 
is compatible with silage conditions. Partial degradation of the cell-wall results 
in release of WSC necessary for the ensiling fermentation. In addition, silage 
digestibility is improved. However, high application rates are required for significant 
effects and these are usually not economical.

19.3.5.3. Inhibitors of aerobic deterioration 
These additives are aimed mainly at inhibiting yeasts and molds which are the 
major spoilage microorganisms upon aerobic exposure of silage. They include 
both chemicals and bacterial inoculants. Chemicals most often used are volatile 
fatty acids and sulfur based chemicals which produce in the silage sulfur- 
dioxide. In Israel, some farmers use salt (NaC1) at 3 kg per m E on the top of 
silages to inhibit molds. Sometimes, a bacterial inoculant is used which includes 
Lactobacillus buchneri, a heterofermentative LAB that produces high concentrations 
of acetic acid in the silage to inhibit aerobic fungi. Another available bacterial 
additive includes Serratia rubidaea, which secretes an antifungal peptide. 
Silage additives which are available are numerous. Their labels claim various 
improvements of silage fermentation, enhanced aerobic stability and improved 
animal performance. At present there are no legal standards with regard to 
these claims. However, in various countries there is a voluntary process of 
testing silage additives in controlled experiments performed by independent research 
laboratories. An example is the UK Forage Additive Approval Scheme which 
sorts and approves silage additives according to their claimed actions, as follows: 
A~ Silo trials: improving fermentation, improving aerobic stability, reducing effluent, 
reducing ensiling losses. 
B~ Animal trials" improving intake, improving digestibility, improving feed 
efficiency and increasing milk production. 
Such schemes can help farmers choose silage additives according to their needs. 
19.3.6. Nutritional aspects 
Silages supply energy, protein and fiber to ruminants. Fibers are necessary for 
proper function of the rumen, whereby the rumen microorganisms degrade 
fibers to volatile fatty acids which are absorbed through the rumen walls and 
serve as energy source for the animals. The nutritional quality of silage depends 
on the crops that are used to make it, the maturity of the crop at harvest, and 
on silage fermentation and management. Silage is different from fresh forage 
or hay by its low pH value and short particle size. The advantage of feeding 
silage is the ease it can be mixed into total mixed rations (TMR) due to the 
high moisture content and short particle size. On the other hand, short particle 
size may result in insufficient chewing and saliva secretion which helps to 
buffer the rumen. The digestibility of silages that are made from crops which 
are too mature is reduced due to high lignin content. The intake of silages 
which are too moist and poorly fermented is reduced, probably because of 
reduced palatability. High levels of acetic and butyric acids also reduce silage 
intake by the animals.

19.3.7. Silage and health 
Forage crops and their preserved forms are the first step in the food production 
chain for human beings. Their quality also affects animal health and well- 
being. Therefore, it is very important that silages should be safe for use. 
However, growing and preserving forage crops take place under field and 
farm conditions which are difficult to control and whereby many complex 
factors exist which might affect their safety. 
In the past, we have witnessed fatal incidences which were caused by feeding 
cattle various types of fodder, the most conspicuous case was the out break 
of bovine spongiform encelophathology ('the mad-cow disease') which was 
caused by feeding animal waste. In Israel, a fatal heart syndrome in beef 
cattle was caused by feeding broiler litter which contained high levels of 
maduromycin (Sygro| a poultry coccidiostat). 
Hazards to health associated with silage include detrimental microorganisms, toxic 
chemicals and excess of acidity. Excess of acidity might impair feed intake. The 
list of undesirable microorganisms in silage includes E. coli some strains of 
which are pathogenic to both animals and humans. Enteropathogenic strains of 
E. coli (such as O157:H7) may cause acute diarrhea in calves or mastiffs. Such 
strains may acquire acid resistance and survive in silage with pH values above 
4.2. Listeria monocytogenes may develop in spoiled aerated parts of silages with 
pH values above 5.0, and in baled silages which are more susceptible to air 
ingress. The organism may cause various diseases in animals and humans, including 
meningitis, encephalitis and abortions. Sheep are the most affected animals by 
this microorganism. Clostridium botulinum may produce an extremely potent 
and fatal neurotoxin in silages with pH values above 4.5. The incidence of botulinum 
in silage is low and this microorganism may origin from dead small animals 
which find their way into the silage. Molds are yet another group of microorganisms 
which develop in silages that are exposed to air. Some molds may produce 
mycotoxins in the silage which cause various acute or chronic diseases. The 
genera that produce mycotoxins include Penicillum, Aspergillus and Fusarium. 
Nitrates, which some forage crops accumulate are toxic to animals. Under 
mild acid conditions prevailing in the silage during early phases of ensiling 
nitrates are reduced to nitric oxide (NO). Nitric oxide may then be oxidized to 
nitrogen dioxide (NO2) which forms nitrous and nitric acids. These substances 
cause respiratory irritation and affect humans ('silo-fillers disease') and animals 
alike. Other toxicants include phyto-estrogens that can cause fertility problems 
which may origin from foreign plans that contaminate the fields. 
Hazards are not always predictable. In order to ensure silage (and feed) safety 
the hygiene of the forage crops and silage should be maintained. Application of

can minimize the incidence of forage related health problems. HACCP (Hazard 
Analysis and Critical Control Points) focuses on prevention of problems at 
critical stages of production. Such control system should identify hazards that 
may occur during silage preparation and storage. Any new technology or change 
from the conventional practice should be assessed for potential risks. For example, 
new silage additives which should be carefully tested before approved for sale. 
Another example pertains to the safety of forage crops irrigation with recycled 
sewage water. Much research on chemical and microbiological aspects is conducted

19.4 PRESERVATION OF AGRICULTURAL BY-PRODUCTS BY 
ENSILING 
Agricultural by-products are obtained from food processing, surplus fruit and 
vegetables, or animal excreta. Examples include hulls of various nuts, apple, 
grape and tomato pomace, beet, coffee pulp, citrus peels and pulp, bakery 
waste, broiler litter and more. These by-products can be utilized by ruminants 
for their energy, protein, mineral and fiber content. 
Agricultural by-products are usually cheap, and in many cases seasonal. During 
the season they might be produced in amounts too high to be used on the 
farm. Because of their high moisture, carbohydrate or nitrogen contents, they 
spoil rapidly. Therefore, they cannot be stored in their raw form for a long 
time. Accumulation of spoiling materials might cause serious environmental 
problems: effluent from moist heaps with high BOD values might pollute freshwater 
sources. Large piles of rotting material might emit noxious odors and attract 
insects and rodents. The benefit from utilization of by-products for animal 
fee~ing arises from both their nutritional value and from reducing the environmental 
load. Efficient utilization of by-products requires application of adequate 
preservation technologies which are also cost-effective. Ensiling is actually the 
only preservation method that can~ be applied (by-products are too bulky for 
preservation by sun-drying). Ensiling technology should be especially adjusted 
according to the composition and unique characteristics of each by-product. 
The research conducted on citrus peels and pulp is an example for the complexity 
of the problem. This by-product is very moist (>80% moisture), rich in sugars 
(20-30% in dry matter) and high in yeast population (104-106 cfu g-l). Storing 
the peels in sealed containers resulted in much effluent and high DM losses 
(up to 30%). The major fermentation product was ethanol (15% in DM) which 
reflected the yeast activity. Application of sorbic acid or blanching of the peels 
to 80~ reduced the yeast numbers and significantly minimized losses. However, 
such treatments were not economical relative to the value of the peels. For

19.5 SILAGE QUALITY 
Many factors affect silage quality, which include weather conditions at harvest 
and ensiling, plant species, stage of maturity, dry matter content of the crop 
and silage management (wilting, chopping, sealing, application of additives). 
The overall objective of ensiling is to maintain quality of the crops as close as 
possible to that of the fresh plants. Silage quality is assessed by preservation 
and nutritional parameters. The preservation quality of a silage can be assessed 
by sensory evaluation and by chemical and microbiological analysis. Sensorial 
appraisal is the primary and simplest indication of silage quality and it examines 
color, odor, texture, visible molds and sense of heat. Color usually turns into 
light green (khaki) due to loss of Mg-ion from the chlorophyll when the pH 
decreases; odor should be typical with no unpleasant notes; the silage particles 
should be free flowing and not sticky with no visible molds. Warm silages 
indicate aerobic spoiling activity. 
Laboratory analysis should include only relevant measurements according to 
silage type and characteristics. The basic chemical analysis includes pH, 
determination of lactic acid and volatile fermentation products (ethanol and 
volatile fatty acids) and ammonia nitrogen. The final pH value of the silage 
depends on the initial sugar content of the crop, its DM content and buffeting 
capacity. For cereal silages it should be below 4.0, for legumes 4.5-5.0. The 
ratio lactic : acetic acids should be at least 2:1, which indicates proper fermentation. 
Butyric acid is undesirable and it indicated clostridial secondary fermentation. 
Ammonia results from protein degradation in the silage and NH3-N should not 
exceed 10% of total N. Losses can be assessed by weight loss during ensiling 
or from the difference in the content of a specific constituent between the 
fresh and ensiled crop. 
Microbiological evaluation of the silage usually includes the enumeration of 
lactic acid bacteria, enterobacteria, yeast, molds and clostridia. These are determined 
in appropriate selective media which are incubated at specific temperatures.

The assessment of aerobic stability of the silage is based on two measurable 
parameters: the time it takes for the temperature to increase by l or 2~ above 
the ambient temperature, or CO2 production during aerobic exposure of the 
silage. The latter is measured in a 'bottle system' whereby the upper part 
contains the silage sample and the lower part contains 25% KOH which absorbs 
the CO2 (Figure 4). 
The nutritional quality of the silage is evaluated by various analyses: for example, 
ash content above the normal values may indicate soil contamination which is 
undesirable. Fiber level is an indicator of the maturity of the plants and correlates 
with silage digestibility. Fiber content is measured after refluxing in neutral or 
acid detergent solutions to obtain NDF (neutral-detergent fiber) and ADF (acid- 
detergent fiber), respectively. The former comprises cellulose, hemicellulose, 
lignin ash and some of the pectin whereas the latter does not include hemicellulose 
which is extracted in the acid solution. Thus, the difference between NDF and 
ADF gives an estimate of the hemicellulose. By further acid hydrolysis of the 
ADF fraction the cellulose is hydrolyzed and after ashing it is possible to 
obtain the lignin content (ADL, acid-detergent lignin). High ADF content correlates 
with poor digestibility.

analytical novelty of the last decade is the development of NIRS (near infra- 
red spectroscopy). It is based on spectra ('fingerprints') which are obtained 
from absorption of infra-red light (in the wavelength region from 750 to 2500 
nm) by specific molecular groups in the analyzed commodity (such as carbonyl,

Digestibility is a measure of the forage fraction which is absorbed and utilized 
by the animal. It is possible to measure the digestibility of any constituent of 
the forage, the most common digestibility measurements include dry matter, 
organic matter and NDF. Digestibility can be measured in vivo (involving animal 
feeding trials) or in vitro. The latter involves a two-stage incubation of dried 
samples in rumen liquor and with pepsin. 
19.6. RESEARCH NEEDS 
New developments and changes are introduced into silage technology constantly 
which should be tested and approved for their efficacy. This is true for all 
aspects of ensiling. New crop cultivars are introduced for higher yields, resistance 
to drought, resistance to lodging or for seed rotation purposes. The new crops 
and cultivars should be studied for their ensiling characteristics, aerobic stability 
and nutritional value. New methods for storing silage are developed, such as 
bale silage which are more susceptible to aerobic spoilage than conventional 
silage. Data on such new technologies needs to be generated. The same applies 
to new silage additives. 
More basic aspects include the interactions between various microbial populations 
during ensiling. For example, how do lactic acid bacterial inoculants for silage 
exert beneficial (probiotic) effects on ruminants? For research purposes novel 
molecular techniques can be used which enable to follow up the fate of 
microorganisms in the silage or in the rumen. They include the use of genomic 
primers for specific strains or marking a target strain with a special trait. An 
example is a modified strain of E. coli which possesses the green fluorescence 
protein (GFP) and is resistant to kanamycin which was used to follow its fate 
in silage and hay. 
Ensiling experiments can be performed in different ways: in mini-silos which 
serve as models and enable to analyze replicate samples during ensiling (time- 
course studies). Another way is to bury Dacron bags with control and treated 
silage samples in various sites in the silo on the day of ensiling. When the 
unloading front reaches the bags they can be retrieved and analyzed. Such 
experiments are performed in a 'true' silage environment. It is also possible to 
use Whole silos to test specific treatments (such as new additives) compared 
to control un-treated silos. Such experiments enable also to conduct animal
