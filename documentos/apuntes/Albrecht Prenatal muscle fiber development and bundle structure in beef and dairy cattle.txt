ABSTRACT: Muscle fiber development during 
gestation determines the muscle structure at birth and 
establishes the conditions for muscle development in 
growing cattle. Differences in muscle structure among 
beef cattle breeds and between beef and dairy cattle are 
obvious already shortly after birth. The objective of the 
study was to investigate the development of muscle fibers 
and muscle fiber bundle structure in semitendinosus 
muscle of divergent cattle breeds from 3 mo of gestation 
until birth. Fetuses of German Angus (GA), Galloway 
(GW), Belgian Blue (BB), and Holstein Friesian (HF) 
were harvested at 3, 4.5, 6, or 9 mo of gestation. Muscle 
sections were analyzed for fiber size and types as well as 
for bundle structure. The results confirmed that primary 
muscle fiber development occurs mainly during the first 
trimester of gestation. All fibers were initially positive 
for fetal fast myosin. Slow myosin as a marker for 
fiber maturation was detected in primary fibers at 3 mo 
of gestation showing a weak immunostaining. During 
the second trimester, the intensity of immunostaining 
strongly increased indicating increased slow myosin 
protein expression. Concurrently, the shape of primary 
fibers changed from myotubes to myofibers whereas the 
size stayed nearly constant. The main increase in muscle 
mass during the second trimester was caused by secondary 
fiber development. As an example, the ratio between 
secondary and primary fibers increased in Holstein 
Friesian fetuses from 5.9 at 4.5 mo of gestation to 21.6 at 
6 mo of gestation. Primary and secondary fibers continued 
to growth during the third trimester. Regional differences 
in the density of slow muscle fibers were detected leading 
to greater variation within the muscle than among breeds. 
Structural organization of muscle fibers in muscle fiber 
bundles developed early in fetal life. At first, large main 
bundles were visible. Smaller structural units defined as 
primary bundles were measurable at 6 mo of gestation 
when most fibers were developed. The size of primary 
bundles nearly doubled from 6 mo of gestation to birth 
in all breeds. In summary, differences among breeds in 
the early fetal muscle fiber development were detected 
in contractile differentiation and partly in muscle fiber 
bundle structure. A prolonged secondary fiber generation 
and altered contractile differentiation may be involved in 
breed differences of postnatal muscle development.
Key words: bundle structure, cattle, fast myosin, fetal muscle growth, muscle fiber composition, slow myosin

INTRODUCTION
Differences in muscle structure among beef cattle 
breeds and between beef and dairy cattle are obvious 
already at birth (Wegner et al., 2000). The basis for 
these differences is established during embryonic and 
fetal development, which is influenced by genetic and 
environmental factors (Bellows et al., 1993). Muscle 
fiber size and muscle fiber number determine muscle 
mass (Martyn et al., 2004) and muscle fiber type 
composition influences meat quality of slaughter 
animals (Klont et al., 1998). Metabolic and contractile 
characteristics of muscle fibers are subjected to 
changes, especially shortly after birth (Wegner et al., 
2000; Picard et al., 2006), whereas the muscle fiber 
number in cattle is already fixed at birth (Luff and 
Goldspink, 1970). Furthermore, the bundle structure is 
determined early in life and does not change between 2 
and 24 mo of age in bulls of the same breeds as in the 
current study (Albrecht et al., 2006). Consequently, the 
development of muscle fibers and organization in fiber 
bundles during gestation is critical for postnatal muscle

was to investigate the prenatal development of muscle 
fiber and muscle bundle structure in 4 cattle breeds 
with different growth impetus and muscularity. Muscle 
samples were taken at 3 time points during gestation to 
identify the period of gestation when breed differences 
manifest. The time points of investigation were 3 mo 
after conception, the time of primary fiber development 
(Robelin et al., 1993); 6 mo after conception, the time of 
secondary fiber development; and at birth. In addition, 
1 group [Holstein Friesian (HF); see below] was 
investigated at 4.5 mo of gestation to further analyze 
changes during the second trimester of gestation. The 
study focused on composition and formation of primary 
muscle fiber bundles during prenatal life and aimed to 
investigate how the prenatal development reflects the 
postnatal muscle bundle structure.
MATERIAL AND METHODS
All animals were reared and slaughtered according 
to German rules and regulations for animal care. The 
experiment was approved by the institutional authorities 
and by the responsible office of the State of Mecklenburg-
Western Pommerania, Germany.
Animals
Eighty cows were artificially inseminated, fed, and 
managed to meet their nutritional requirements. Pregnant 
cows were slaughtered under normal processing 
conditions at 3, 4.5 or 6 mo postconception and fetuses 
were removed. Newborn calves were referred to 9 mo of 
gestation and slaughtered immediately after birth. Five 
to 9 fetuses and calves of 4 cattle breeds with different 
muscle growth potential were used (Table 1). The breeds 
represented German Angus (GA), a typical beef breed; 
Galloway (GW), a smaller, environmentally resistant 
producer of beef; Belgian Blue (BB), an extreme type 
for muscle growth; and HF, a dairy type having specific 
beef characteristics.
Fetuses were weighed and dissected into carcass, 
organs, muscles, and body fat. Carcass, organs, and 
tissues weight development was previously published 
by Mao et al. (2008). The presented study included 1 
additional group of HF fetuses at 4.5 mo of gestation 
and focused on prenatal muscle fiber development 
and muscle structure of semitendinosus muscle during 
gestation. Previously published data of BW and muscle 
weight (Mao et al., 2008) were extended by the 4.5 mo 
of gestation group and are presented in the current study 
to give background information about the growth of 
investigated fetuses.
Samples
The semitendinosus muscle was removed from the 
right side of the carcass, weighed, measured for length 
and circumference at the thickest part of the muscle, cut 
into pieces, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in either 
liquid nitrogen or at –70°C until use. Muscle cross-
sectional area was calculated from muscle circumference. 
Muscle transverse sections were cut 10 μm thick using 
a cryostat microtome CM3050 S (Leica, Bensheim, 
Germany). Sections were stained with hematoxylin/
eosin (hematoxylin: Dako, Glostrup, Denmark; eosin: 
Chroma Gesellschaft, Munster, Germany) and embedded 
with Roti-Histokit (Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany). Serial 
sections of newborn calf muscle were reacted for 
actomyosin Ca2+ adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) 
stability after alkaline pre-incubation (pH 10.4) as 
described by Wegner et al. (2000) for fiber typing. Fetal 
muscle sections were immunostained for slow (MYH7) 
and fetal fast skeletal myosin heavy chain (MYH4).
Immunohistochemistry
Fetal muscle sections were double stained for both 
myosin isoforms. Initially, muscle sections were fixed in 
4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Unspecific bindings 
of the first secondary antibody were blocked using 10% 
normal goat serum in PBS-Triton x100 (PBST) for 15 
min. Sections were incubated with the first primary 
antibody against fetal MYH4 (ABIN485670; Antibodies-
online, Aachen, Germany) for 1 h at room temperature in 
a humidity chamber. Specific binding of primary antibody 
was detected with the respective goat anti-rabbit IgG 
secondary antibody labeled with MFP 590 (MoBiTec, 
Göttingen, Germany). The procedure continued with 
blocking unspecific bindings of the second secondary 
antibody with 10% normal rabbit serum in PBST again for 
15 min. The second primary antibody was against MYH7 
(clone NOQ7.5.4D, NB120-11083; Acris Antibodies 
GmbH, Herford, Germany), which was detected with an 
Alexa Fluor 488 labeled rabbit anti-mouse IgG (MoBiTec, 
Göttingen, Germany) secondary antibody. Sections were 
counterstained with 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33 258 (Sigma-
Aldrich, Munich, Germany) nuclear stain. Finally, slides 
were covered using Mowiol mounting medium (Carl

Image Analysis
The area percentage of slow or fast myosin, the number 
of muscle cells per area, the number of nuclei per area, and 
the size of muscle fibers and muscle fiber primary bundles as 
well as the number of muscle fibers per primary bundle were 
determined using computerized image analysis. Muscle 
fiber primary bundles were defined according to Judge et al. 
(1989) as smallest structural units in a muscle consisting of 
muscle fibers surrounded by connective tissue. The image 
analysis system was equipped with a Nikon Microphot SA 
fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Dusseldorf, Germany), 
a CC-12 high resolution color camera (OSIS, Munster, 
Germany), and CELL^F image analysis software (OSIS, 
Munster, Germany). For each measurement, the sequence 
of analysis steps was organized in newly developed 
individual macro programs or the interactive measurement 
module of the software was used. The sequence of the 
measurement of slow and fast myosin area percentage was 
as follows: the green fluorescence image was taken, the 
contrast optimized, and the green channel extracted and 
filtered with a median filter. After shading correction a 
region of interest was defined, a threshold was set for slow 
myosin area and measured. The same procedure was done 
for the red fluorescence image of fetal fast myosin in a next 
step. Finally, the ratio between both values was calculated. 
For measurement of slow muscle fiber size at 4.5 and 6 mo 
of gestation, a detection step was included after threshold 
setting for slow myosin area. After interactive exclusion 
of false detected particles, the slow muscle fibers were 
measured. This procedure could not be used in samples of 
3 mo of gestational age. Muscle fiber size was measured 
in those samples using the interactive tool of interpolating 
polygon. In any case, muscle fibers of at least 10 different 
images were measured, between 200 and 1,500 fibers for 
each fetus in total. Additionally, the number of secondary 
fibers per primary fiber was counted interactively in 4.5 
and 6 mo old fetuses.
The number of nuclei per area was determined 
using the blue fluorescence image of Hoechst 33 258 
staining. The fluorescence image was taken, the contrast 
optimized, the blue channel extracted, and the threshold 
set. The nuclei were detected after ultimate erosion and 1 
dilatation step. The overlay was copied into the original 
image and missing nuclei were interactively added. The 
image area and the number of nuclei were determined.
Muscle fiber bundles were measured in bright field 
images (Fig. 1) using the interactive tool of interpolating 
polygon. After measuring the size of a bundle, the number 
of all fibers within the bundle was counted (Fig. 1a). The 
green fluorescence image of the same area was used in 
samples from fetuses at 6 mo of gestation to determine 
the respective number of slow fibers within the bundle. 
The primary bundle size and number of muscle fibers 
within a bundle was determined in ATPase reacted slides 
(Fig. 1b) for newborn calves.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Body weight (Fig. 2a, adapted from Mao et al., 2008) 
increased in all breeds significantly from 3 to 6 mo of 
gestation and from 6 mo of gestation to birth. An exponential 
growth can be assumed as reported earlier for fetal growth 
in cattle (Prior and Laster, 1979; Robelin et al., 1991). 
During the third trimester of gestation, BW increased 3- to 
4-fold in GA, GW, and HF whereas it increased 5-fold in 
BB. In agreement with the study of Deveaux et al. (2001), 
fetuses of large framed breeds such as HF and BB had 
similar BW during the early fetal life. At 6 mo of gestation, 
BW was similar among all investigated breeds. Differences 
in the growth potential became obvious at birth, when BW 
was greatest in BB followed by HF, GA, and GW.
The intensive muscle development during the 
second trimester was accompanied by an increase of 
body protein content from about 8 to 13% in all 4 breeds 
(data not shown). A further increase of body protein 
content from 6 mo of gestation to birth was not observed.
The increase of semitendinosus muscle mass (Fig. 2b, 
adapted from Mao et al., 2008) was similar during the first 
trimester of gestation for all breeds but differed during the 
third trimester. It was 3.4-, 4.4-, 5.1-, and 6.5-fold in GW, 
GA, HF, and BB, respectively, leading to a significant 
difference in muscle weight between BB and the other 
breeds at birth. In contrast to the study of Deveaux et al. 
(2001), we could not find significant differences between 
BB and HF in semitendinosus muscle weight before 
birth. To clarify whether the differences at birth were 
reasoned in the number and/or size of developing muscle 
fibers and whether the bundle structure of the muscle is 
already fixed at birth in concordance with the muscle 
fiber number, we investigated semitendinosus muscle 
cross-sections of these animals.
The muscle cross-sectional area (Table 2) increased 
according to the weight development. It was larger in 
BB than in GW and HF at birth but was similar in all 4 
breeds at 3 and 6 mo of gestation. Large variations in 
muscle cross-section areas of BB fetuses at 6 and 9 mo 
of gestation (2.4 to 4.5 and 9.6 to 20.4 cm2, respectively) 
indicated that we had fetuses of both phenotypes normal 
and double muscled in the present study. This may have 
reduced the probability to find significant differences 
between breeds in fetal muscle development.
The muscle fiber size developed nearly equally in 
fetuses of all 4 breeds and did not show breed differences 
at birth in contrast to breed differences obvious later in 
postnatal life (Wegner et al., 2000). Slow muscle fibers 
were smaller and fewer in BB during gestation and the 
ratio between slow and fast fiber area was less than 
in the other breeds at birth. This may indicate a delay 
in contractile differentiation in agreement with the 
observation of Deveaux et al. (2001). Earlier, Picard et al. 
(1995) compared double muscled and normal fetuses and 
concluded concordantly that double muscled cattle have 
prolonged proliferation phase and delayed differentiation 
of fibers. This can explain why less primary fibers can be 
found at the same time after conception in double muscled 
fetuses compared with normal. In our study, the number 
of nuclei per area (data not shown) was not different 
between breeds at both gestational ages, indicating 
similar proliferative activities in all investigated breeds. 
Results regarding the duration of the proliferation phase, 
however, were not obtained.
During gestation after the first trimester, primary fibers 
were observed in all 4 breeds having a myotube shape. The 
myotubes were positive for fetal fast myosin and partly 
positive for slow myosin. A weak immune-fluorescence 
was detected as shown in Fig. 3a. During and after the 
second trimester, a strong, bright fluorescence indicating 
slow myosin was observed in primary fibers (Fig. 3b and 
3c). Most of them showed a decreased intensity in the 
staining for fetal fast myosin compared with surrounding 
fibers (Fig. 4). In accordance with observations of Picard 
et al. (1995), the shape of primary fibers changed during

Concurrently, the number of slow fibers per square 
millimeter decreased, because newly developing fibers 
appeared around them and were usually not positive for 
slow myosin (Fig. 3b). This stage is described as second 
wave of muscle fiber development (reviewed by Picard 
et al., 2002) and the fibers as second generation fibers or 
secondary fibers. At 4.5 mo of gestation all primary fibers 
were surrounded by secondary fibers.
The apparent total number of slow fibers per 
muscle and the muscle fiber size further increased 
until birth (Table 2). Secondary fibers expressing slow 
myosin (Fig. 3c) contributed to this increase. In some 
HF, secondary fibers positive for slow myosin could 
be observed at 6 mo of gestation (Fig. 3c). This is in 
accordance with observations of Robelin et al. (1993) 
for semitendinosus muscle of a cattle fetus at 180 d of 
gestation. They concluded that secondary fibers develop 
2 types of muscle fibers. Most of them express fast 
myosin, but some secondary fibers begin differentiation 
during expression of fast myosins and continue to 
express slow and fast myosins later during development. 
Such fiber transitions during fetal life were reported by 
Picard et al. (2006) in 2 Charolais sire lines with different 
muscle growth rate. Their results indicated a connection 
between growth rate and muscle fiber composition.
The number of all primary and secondary fibers of 
a structural unit, which may form a muscle fiber bundle 
later in development, were counted and used to calculate 
the ratio between secondary and primary fibers at 4.5 and 
6 mo of gestation. Primary and secondary fibers were 
discriminated according to their different fiber size at 
these gestational stages. The ratio increased in HF from 
5.9 to 21.6. The ratio between secondary and primary fi-
bers at 6 mo of gestation in the other breeds was 25.2, 
22.1, and 29.4 for GA, GW, and BB, respectively. These 
values were greater than ratios reported by Deveaux et al. 
(2001) for HF and double muscled BB fetuses. Reasons 
for this discrepancy may be the mode of ratio determi-
nation, a deviation in the exact gestational age between 
the studies, or the region of the muscle considered. We

The number of slow muscle fibers per square millimeter 
was partially doubled in medial compared with superfi-

pronounced in the mature muscle in cattle (Totland et al., 
1988; Totland and Kryvi, 1991) and pig (Lefaucheur et

Muscle fiber bundles begin to form with the develop-
ment of muscle fibers. Primary fibers at 3 mo of gestation 
formed groups and are suggested to develop large, main 
bundles, sometimes referred to tertiary bundles (Lepetit 
and Culioli, 1994), later in development. Primary bundles, 
according to the definition used by Judge et al. (1989) and 
in our previous study (Albrecht et al., 2006), could be ob-
served and measured after the second trimester (Fig. 5). 
The size of primary bundles nearly doubled from 6 mo 
of gestation to birth in all breeds (Table 3). This increase 
was mainly caused by growth of muscle fibers but also by 
formation of new fibers in GA. This suggests a prolonged 
secondary fiber development in beef type cattle. Most of 
the muscle fibers were already apparent at 6 mo of gesta-
tion in GW, HF, and BB in accordance with Gagnière et 
al. (1999) and Picard et al. (2002). The number of muscle 
fibers per primary bundle determined at birth was signifi-
cantly smaller in HF than in BB. Except for BB, the num-
ber was similar to that reported in an earlier study for the 
same breeds later in life (Albrecht et al., 2006). This indi-
cates that also the primary bundle structure is determined 
at birth beside the muscle fiber number. In contrast, BB 
had more muscle fibers per bundle later in life (Albrecht 
et al., 2006) than we determined in the present study (200 
to 300 vs. 105 at birth). In investigations of the postnatal 
muscle structure development, BB bulls used exhibited 
the double muscled phenotype. The fetuses in the pres-
ent study, however, were male and female and obviously 
of both phenotypes, normal and double muscled. On the 
other hand, connective tissue was shown to be thinner and 
less in BB than in other breeds (Boccard, 1981; Albrecht

The increasing number of slow fibers per bundle 
during the third trimester of gestation indicates fiber 
maturation to slow fibers in this period as described by 
Picard et al. (1995). As mentioned before, secondary 
fibers expressing slow myosin were observed in some 
samples at 6 mo of gestation. These fibers may increase 
the number of slow fibers visible at birth. Additionally, 
fiber conversions take place during gestation but also 
after birth (Picard et al., 2006). For the breeds in our study, 
Wegner et al. (2000) reported further fiber conversions in 
the early postnatal life; however, we noticed then a fiber 
conversion from slow to the fast phenotype.
The results confirmed that primary muscle fiber de-
velopment occurs mainly during the first trimester of ges-
tation. The development of primary fibers is followed by 
secondary fiber development during the second trimester 
and maturation of primary fibers to the slow phenotype. 
Primary fiber maturation is obvious in strong staining 
for slow myosin and in changes of the shape of primary 
fibers from myotubes to myofibers. The main increase 
in muscle mass during the second trimester was caused 
by secondary fiber development. As reviewed by Du et 
al. (2010), the period of secondary fiber development is 
crucial and provides the basis for further muscle devel-
opment. Furthermore, the formation of connective tis-
sue by fibroblasts during fetal development is involved 
in establishing the bundle structure with consequences 
for meat quality in slaughtered animals. Structural or-
ganization of muscle fibers in muscle fiber bundles was 
determined at 6 mo of gestation in our study. Most of the 
fibers were already developed at that time.
Differences between breeds in the early fetal muscle 
fiber development were detected in contractile differentia-
tion and partly muscle fiber bundle structure. Differences 
were larger at birth than during early stages of gestation, 
suggesting the third trimester is crucial for skeletal mus-
cle development. A prolonged secondary fiber generation 
and altered contractile differentiation in beef breeds may 
be involved in divergent postnatal muscle development.
