animal welfare issue

Abstract
An experimental contingent valuation (CV) survey of university students was undertaken
to explore the impact of social consensus information on people’s stated willingness to pay
(wtp) to address a farm animal welfare issue. The survey found that additional information
presented to respondents on social consensus concerning the moral dimensions of the issue
led to a greater perception of social consensus by respondents. This greater perception of so-
cial consensus appeared to result in a higher level of moral intensity associated with the issue
and a higher stated wtp by respondents for policy to address the issue. However, as for many
CV studies of public goods, a question remains as to whether the estimated wtp is a true mea-
sure of people’s preferences and relative values or merely a measure of attitudes on an arbi-
trary monetary scale. /C211 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

1759, amongst others) and various approaches to incorporating moral consider-

considerations should be taken into account within economics (Sen, 1987). More-
over, researchers have explored whether the contingent valuation (CV) method for
valuing non-market goods can be used to elicit the value that people place on goods
that have a moral dimension (for example actions to reduce environmental pollution,
conserve species and habitats or improve animal welfare). Spash (2000) provides
a useful critique of the use of the CV method where moral issues are involved. He
notes that human value formation (with respect to goods that have moral dimen-
sions) combines ethical and economic aspects in a more complex way than most
economists have assumed.
There are various social psychological models of behaviour that attempt to ex-
plain how moral considerations aﬀect both behavioural intentions and decision mak-
ing (see Plous, 1993; Ranyard, Crozier, & Svenson, 1997). For example, Schwartz
(1977) considers diﬀerent types of explanation for prosocial behaviour within a
framework that states that such behaviour is inﬂuenced by the intensity of moral
(personal) behaviour which is determined by an individual’s cognitive structure of
norms and values and, prior to overt action, may be mitigated by defences of the ap-
propriateness of the obligation. Jones (1991) puts forward an issue-contingent model
of ethical decision-making containing characteristics of the moral issue itself, which
he also collectively refers to as moral intensity. He argues that six characteristics of a
moral issue – social consensus, temporal immediacy, probability of eﬀect, concentra-
tion of eﬀect, proximity and magnitude of consequences – will determine the level of
moral intensity associated with it (Jones, 1991).
This paper presents the method and results of an experimental survey designed to
explore the link between social consensus, moral intensity and people’s willingness to
pay (wtp) in the context of animal welfare. The hypotheses to be tested are (i) the
greater the perceived social consensus concerning a moral issue then the greater
the moral intensity people attach to that issue and (ii) the higher the level of moral
intensity associated with an issue then the higher people’s wtp will be for a policy
measure to address the issue.
2.Backgroundtotheresearch
There are three main inter-related aspects of the research reported here. These
are: (i) animal welfare as a moral issue; (ii) the use of the CV method of eco-
nomic valuation, speciﬁcally where moral issues are involved; (iii) the incorpora-
tion of moral considerations in decision making, speciﬁcally the concept of moral
intensity and the importance of social consensus. Each of these is a major area of
debate and research and it is not within the scope of this paper to provide a com-
prehensive review of the substantial literature on each of these areas. Rather, the
aim is to put the research reported here into context with respect to these three main
aspects.

2.1. Animal welfare as a moral (and economic) issue
Human treatment and welfare of animals has long been debated. The current
debate largely revolves around animal sentience and cognition, various forms of
contractualism and utilitarianism, and how they relate to the question of rights.
Scientists such as Dawkins (1980, 1998) and Rollin (1992) present both scientiﬁc ev-
idence and cogent argument of the ability of many animals to experience both plea-
sure and pain. The contractualism argument does not hinge on the issue of sentience.
It is highlighted by Carruthers (1992) who argues that animals are not rational
agents capable of agreeing rules, cannot enter into contracts with duties and respon-
sibilities and so lack moral standing and moral rights. The issue of animal sentience
is an important one for the utilitarian argument (which is, for example, the basis of
Singer’s, 1989, ‘equal consideration of interests’ concept). Indeed, the importance of
the acceptance of animal sentience has been highlighted within the European Union
by changes to legislation (agreed in 1997 as part of the Treaty of Amsterdam) in the
form of revision to the Treaty of Rome (which established the European Community
in 1957) by means of a Protocol on Animal Welfare which states the need to ‘ensure
improved protection and respect for the welfare of animals as sentient beings’.
The utilitarian argument for improving animal welfare is one that is potentially
compatible with neoclassical economic theory and the concept of wtp. McInerney
(1993) notes that, within an economic framework, animal welfare is just a subset
of human welfare since it aﬀects human utility (i.e. there is no explicit framework
for incorporating the utilities of other species). However, Blackorby and Donaldson
(1992) go as far as considering how animal utility functions could be incorporated
into a social value function. Bennett (1995) considers how human concerns for ani-
mal welfare might be considered within an economic framework, whilst Bennett and
Larson (1996) explore the use of the CV method to measure the value that people
place on improvements to animal welfare.
2.2. CV and moral issues
CV is a widely-employed technique used for providing an indication of the value
of non-market goods by eliciting people’s wtp for them using hypothetical markets
(Mitchell & Carson, 1989). Carson, Flores, and Meade (2000) provide a useful recent
overview of the merits and limitations of the CV method (whilst Carson, Wright,
Carson, Alberini, & Flores, 1995, provide a bibliography of CV applications). There
has been much debate amongst economists and others as to whether the CV method
is an appropriate one to use to value complex goods with moral dimensions (e.g. see
Milgrom, 1993). Certainly it has been used for that purpose. However, a number of
researchers have questioned the validity of wtp estimates from such CV studies. For
example, Kahneman, Ritov, Jacowitz, and Grant (1993) conclude that wtp for 16
diﬀerent environmental issues were ‘expressions of attitudes rather than indications
of economic value’. A similar conclusion is also reached by Spash (2000) who found
that half of respondents giving a positive wtp bid held rights-based views that endan-
gered birds have the right to protection, which he stated was inconsistent with

providing a money value to achieve that protection. Kahnemann and Knetsch (1992)
conclude that responses in a CV study reﬂected wtp for the moral satisfaction of con-
tributing to public goods, not the economic value of the goods. Diamond and Haus-
man (1994) state that ‘to date CV surveys do not measure the preferences they
attempt to measure’ and do not provide economic values but rather expressions of
attitudes on a dollar scale. These and other studies question the ability of CV to elicit
wtp measures that are a true reﬂection of preferences, especially where moral dimen-
sions are involved.
2.3. Moral intensity and social consensus
Moral intensity (Jones, 1991) is a construct that captures the extent of issue-
related moral imperative in a situation and its component parts are the six charac-
teristics identiﬁed above, including social consensus. Moral intensity is likely to vary
substantiallyfrom one issue to another and will generally increase with an increase in
any one or more of its components. Components are expected to have interactive ef-
fects and there may be threshold levels for components that must be reached before
moral intensity varies signiﬁcantly. The social consensus characteristic is ‘the degree
of social agreement that a proposed act is evil’ (Jones, 1991). These social references
may come from anumberofsources, suchas existing national laws, pronouncements
from experts, public ﬁgures, or pressure groups. Some people might refer to pub-
lished opinion polls, or the opinions of acquaintances. It is possible that information
from sources that the individual does not identify with could be discounted. How-
ever, a high degree of social consensus about the morality of an act will reduce
any ambiguity that an individual might feel about what is deemed an ethical way
to act (Jones, 1991). Social consensus can also act in a more subtle way by inﬂuenc-
ing the way people perceive the other aspects of moral intensity, especially where
there is limited information available, such as magnitude of consequences and prob-
ability of eﬀect.
Research within psychology suggests that the perceived level of consensus on an
issue greatly inﬂuences people’s decision-making processes (Baron, 1994). Morris
and McDonald (1995) found that perceived social consensus was a very important
element of the moral intensity associated with ethical issues in business. Economists
have sometime previously noted the importance of the social consensus element. For
example, Smith (1759) notes the importance ofpropriety (i.e. the standards ofbehav-
iour considered correct within society) in the decision making of individual economic
agents.
3.Methods
Following pilot surveys a CV survey of 164 students at The University of Reading
was conducted. The student sample consisted of two undergraduate classes, one each
from the Department of Economics and the Department of Psychology, and a ran-
dom sample of students. The students from the classes were given the questionnaire
504 R. Bennett, R. Blaney / Journal of Economic Psychology 23 (2002) 501–520
to complete in class. The students from the random sample were interviewed face-to-
face. The latter enabled some assessment of respondents’ interpretation and under-
standing of the questions presented to them and a check that classroom responses
did not diﬀer radically from face-to-face interviews.
Following a small preliminary survey of people’s concerns about farm animalwel-
fare, the scenario chosen for consideration concerned the slaughter of pigs. A ques-
tionnaire was designed that consisted of three sections. The ﬁrst section contained
questions about age, sex, nationality, and existing purchasing behaviour with regard
to animal products. It then asked respondents to score on a scale of 0–10 the impor-
tance of eight farm animal welfare issues, with 10 being ‘very important’. The second
section provided information about the existing pig slaughtering techniques used in
the UK, including some of the potential animal welfare problems, and an alternative
‘humane’ slaughter technique known as the Head to Back system. This system kills
pigs instantly by using a high voltage electrical current that induces cardiac arrest.
Respondents were asked whether they would support government legislation to
make it compulsory for slaughterhouses to use the Head to Back system. Following
a reminder concerning budget constraints, they were then asked the amount of
money they would be willing to pay, as an increase in their weekly food bill, to sup-
port this legislation (this payment vehicle reﬂects the public good nature of the
legislation, rather than being tied to pig meat consumption). A single-bounded
dichotomous choice format was used for the wtp question (i.e. would you be willing
to pay £x, yes or no?), with amounts ranging from 25 pence to £2, which were chosen
after pre-testing using open-ended responses (i.e. how much would you be willing
to pay?). This is a common and recommended elicitation method for CV studies
(Arrow et al., 1993). The third section asked eight, related, attitudinal questions
(see Table 1), which respondents were asked to score on a scale of 0–10, with 10
being ‘very much like my way of thinking’. These attitudinal questions also included
questions about the morality of the pig slaughtering techniques. Finally, respondents
were asked to give some rationale for their wtp responses and their considerations
behind the scores they gave to the attitudinal questions.
There were two versions of the questionnaire, a high social consensus and low so-
cial consensus version. They were identical except for the social consensus informa-
tion provided in relation to the pig slaughter scenario. Both had the same factual

4.Results
Of the three groups sampled, 64 psychology students, 48economics students, and
52 random students completed the questionnaires. The age range of the students was
18–55 years, with a mean of 21 years. Nine percent of the students were from outside
the UK, and 7% were vegetarian. The gender proﬁle was 56% female, 44% male.
One-third of the students lived in university halls and half of the students sampled
were in receipt of a grant. The mean expenditure on food consumption was £21.73
a week, but this excludes the portion ofhall fees paidby students in catered halls that
is allocated for meals. Nearly 49% of the sample population avoided buying some
animal products because of their concerns about the welfare of the animals involved
in making those products, 34% avoided eggs from hens kept in battery cages and
23% avoided fur. The two most important animal welfare issues (out of the eight is-
sues presented to respondents) were identiﬁed as hens kept in battery cages, and the
slaughter of pigs without adequate stunning.
Just over 61% of the sample population supported the proposed pig slaughter leg-
islation and 12% were against (27% expressed no opinion). In terms of the two levels

of social consensus information, just 5% were against the legislation with the high
social consensus information and 21% for the low social consensus information with
a similar level of ‘no opinion’ responses for both. Chi-square analysis indicates that
the degree of support for the legislation is related to whether the respondent is given
high or low social consensus information (P < 0:01).
Student’s t-tests were carried out on a number of variables, and where there were
unequal variances the Cochran test statistic was used. In terms of the attitudinal
variables (Table 1), statements (a) and (b) were negatively correlated (P < 0:0001),
as were (c) and (d) (P < 0:0001). This is as expected since they are opposites. Surpris-
ingly perhaps, there was little correlation between (a) or (b) and the other state-
ments, (e) or (f) and the other statements or (g) or (h) and the other statements.
However, for the high consensus sample there was a correlation between (c) and
(g) (P < 0:0001). These last two attitudes are important as they show the link be-
tween the perceived opinion and the individual’s own views. Indeed, a comparison
of high and low social consensus groups shows a strong correlation between social
consensus and scoring on statement (g) (5.17 v. 6.68; P < 0:0001) and social consen-
sus and scoring on statement (a) (7.93 v. 8.11; P < 0:0001) (i.e. those respondents
given the high social consensus questionnaires gave a higher score of agreement).
This indicates that the respondents were interpreting the social consensus infor-
mation as was expected. The rationale given by respondents (in response to the
open-ended debrieﬁng questions contained within the questionnaire), as to why they
had answered the wtp and attitudinal questionsthe way they had,generally exhibited
a logical and consistent reasoning behind their responses. Spearman correlation
analysis indicated correlations between wtp and age-group (P < 0:0006), belief that
pigs have a right to aspainless death aspossible (P < 0:0001), beliefthat public opin-
ion is that current pig slaughter techniques are wrong (P < 0:0001), and concern
about other farm animal welfare issues (P < 0:0004).
Further analysis of the ‘no opinion’ responses concerning support of the legisla-
tion revealed (i) a lack of internal consistency of responses to the attitudinal ques-
tions (ii) low scores/expressions of concern about the eight animal welfare issues
(iii) a relatively low response rate and diversity of comments to the debrieﬁng ques-
tions. Twenty-one percent of ‘no opinion’ responses were from overseas students,
presumably reﬂecting a perceived lack of relevance of UK animal welfare issues to
them. A higher proportion of low consensus compared to high consensus respon-
dents did not respond to the debrieﬁng question.

5.Discussion
Some tentative conclusions can be drawn from the results of the experiment out-
lined above: (i) additional information on social consensus concerning the moral di-
mensions of an issue presented to respondents in a CV study leads to a greater
perception of social consensus by respondents; (ii) this greater perception of social
consensus appears to result in a higher level of moral intensity associated with the
issue and (iii) the higher level of perceived social consensus and resultant increase
in moral intensity gives rise to a higher wtp by respondents to address the issue. Ev-
idence to support these conclusions include: (i) results showed a strong, statistically
signiﬁcant correlation between the level of social consensus information supplied to
respondents and their perceived level of social consensus, shown, for example, by re-
sponse scores to the statement ‘Current opinion is that the present method of slaugh-
tering pigs is wrong’; (ii) respondents with the high social consensus information had
statistically signiﬁcant higher levels of agreement to moral statements such as ‘Pigs
have a right to die in as painless a death as possible’ than those with the low consen-
sus information; (iii) in the behavioural models of wtp, moral variables were more
important in explaining wtp in the high social consensus model than in the low social
consensus one and, estimated wtp to address the issue was around two and a half
times higher for the high consensus respondents than for the low consensus ones.
The question is how should these ﬁndings be interpreted? Does a higher wtp truly
reﬂect a higher level of moral intensity associated with the issue because of increased
social consensus and does that wtp provide a measure of value of the proposed leg-
islation or is it distorted by a ‘warm glow’ (Andreoni, 1990; Kahnemann & Knetsch,
1992) or other bias? The diﬃculty is in fully understanding the cognitive processes
involved in the formulation of people’s wtp responses. Green and Tunstall (1999)
provide a critical assessment of the CV method and its application from a psycholog-
ical perspective (drawing attention to what they call the untestable and na€ııve theories
of psychology and sociology invented by economists). Schkade and Payne (1993)
used thetechnique of verbal protocol analysis tobetter understand the cognitive pro-
cesses involved in responding to CV questions and found that people referred to
many diverse considerations when trying to decide on their responses to wtp ques-
tions. Baron (2001) notes that responses to wtp questions (and weightings given to
non-monetary attributes) are often ‘internally inconsistent’ and, in particular, insen-
sitive to quantity.
There is much evidence in the CV literature concerning information bias and it’s
impact on wtp (e.g. Bergstrom, Stoll, & Randall, 1989, 1990; Munro & Hanley,
1999). Changes in the information supplied to respondents concerning an issue
can result in substantial changes to wtp. The higher wtp could be simply explained
as an upward information bias caused by the social consensus information. Clearly,
510 R. Bennett, R. Blaney / Journal of Economic Psychology 23 (2002) 501–520
this information had a relatively large impact on wtp, which begs the question as to
whether such information should be presented to respondents in CV studies (given
that there is the potential for ‘bias’ of wtp responses depending on exactly what
and how information is given).
However the diﬀerence in wtp responses is explained, the apparent increase in
moral intensity with thehigh consensus information suggeststhat a complex decision
making process is involved. Clearly, people’s stated wtp appears to reﬂect this in-
crease in moral intensity caused by the high consensus information but on what
scale? Is the higher wtp merely a measure of attitudes on an arbitrary monetary scale
rather than a true measure of preferences that reﬂects relative values (Kahneman &
Ritov, 1993)? There was some evidence of economic rationality in responses. For ex-
ample, wtp varied with bid amount, preferences and beliefs (as exhibited by stated
levels of concern etc.) and by expenditure variables. However, the latter inﬂuence
was relatively weak and the other factors would also inﬂuence wtp responses that
were based on attitude statements rather than resource values.
There is likely to be a varying and complex set of attitudes and beliefs concerning
the moral aspects of animal welfare which people may ﬁnd diﬃcult or inappropriate
to articulate within the CV method of wtp elicitation. The number of ‘no opinion’
responses may be an indicator of this. No speciﬁc tests for embeddedness, warm
glow or other potential problems of the CV method were undertaken within the ex-
periment. Thus, although the experiment clearly shows the links between social con-
sensus information, moral intensity and wtp, there is no conclusive evidence that wtp
responses constitute a true measure of wtp (in common with many other CV studies).
6.Conclusion
The experimental survey outlined in this paper found that additional information
on social consensus concerning the moral dimensions of an animal welfare issue pre-
sented to respondents in a CV study gave rise to a greater perception of social con-
sensus by respondents. Furthermore, this greater perception of social consensus
appeared to result in a higher level of moral intensity (moral imperative) associated

Pig slaughtering techniques in the UK
• The slaughterhouses Act 1974 provides that animals must be stunned before they
are killed to render the animal insensible to pain until death supervenes.
• Pigs are usually killed in a two-stage process. Firstly they are stunned, either by
using two electrodes applied to the head or by immersion in a tank of carbon di-
oxide gas. Secondly, their necks are cut. Death is then caused by loss of blood.
• There are over 13 million pigs slaughtered in the UK every year.
• There is some evidence that a high proportion of pigs in the UK might not be ad-
equately stunned before slaughtering. However, some scientists state that both gas
and electrical stunning are acceptable methods.
• An alternative method of slaughter called ‘Head to Back’ stunning kills the ani-
mals by cardiac arrest, resulting in a quicker death and eliminating the risk of
the animal regaining consciousness.
• The ‘Head to Back’ system is little used by the industry as it can lead to carcass
damage, and consequently farmers are also against the use of the ‘Head to Back’
system as it would reduce their proﬁts.
• The consequences of enforcing this system in replacement of the current system
would mean that food prices would increase.
