The Evolution and Genetics of Herbicide Resistance in Weeds1 
MARIE JASIENIUK, ANITA L. BR~LE-BABEL, and IAN N. MORRISON' 
Abstract. The importance of various factors influencing the 
evolution of herbicide resistance in weeds is critically exam- 
ined using population genetic models. The factors include 
gene mutation, initial frequency of resistance alleles, inheri- 
tance, weed fitness in the presence and absence of herbicide, 
mating system, and gene flow. Where weed infestations are 
heavy, the probability of selecting for resistance can be high 
even when the rate of mutation is low. Subsequent to the 
occurrence of a resistant mutant, repeated treatments with 
herbicides having the same mode of action can lead to the 
rapid evolution of a predominantly resistant population. At 
a given herbicide selection intensity, the initial frequency of 
resistance alleles determines the number of generations re- 
quired to reach a specific frequency of resistant plants. The 
initial frequency of resistance alleles has a greater influence 
on the evolutionary process when herbicides impose weak 
selection, as opposed to very strong selection. Under selec- 
tion, dominant resistance alleles increase in frequency more 
rapidly than recessive alleles in random mating or highly 
outcrossing weed populations. In highly self-fertilizing spe- 
cies, dominant and recessive resistance alleles increase in 
frequency at approximately the same rate. Gene flow 
through pollen or seed movement from resistant weed popu- 
lations can provide a source of resistance alleles in previously 
susceptible populations. Because rates of gene flow are gen- 
erally higher than rates of mutation, the time required to 
reach a high level of resistance in such situations is greatly 
reduced. Contrary to common misconception, gene flow 
from a susceptible population to a population undergoing 
resistance evolution is unlikely to slow the evolutionary proc- 
ess significantly. Accurate measurements of many factors 
that influence resistance evolution are dimcult, if not impos- 
sible, to obtain experimentally. Thus, the use of models to 
predict times to resistance in specific situations is markedly 
limited. However, with appropriate assumptions, they can be 
invaluable in assessing the relative effectiveness of various 
management practices to avoid, or delay, the occurrence of 
herbicide resistance in weed populations. 
Additional index words. Gene mutation, initial frequency, se- 
lection, fitness, inheritance, mating system, gene flow, popu- 
lation genetic models.

Herbicide resistance in weeds has become a threat to conven-

resistance to at least 15 classes of herbicides has been reported 
in more than 100 weed species (65, 116). Moreover, the area of 
land infested with resistant weeds is increasing rapidly (65). 
The development of herbicide resistance in weeds is an evo- 
lutionary process. In response to repeated treatment with a par- 
ticular class or family of herbicides, weed populations change in 
genetic composition such that the frequency of resistance alleles 
and resistant individuals increases. In this way, weed populations 
become adapted to the intense selection imposed by herbicides. 
Numerous factors influence evolutionary processes in natural 
populations of plants and animals. Several of these have been 
included as parameters in mathematical models of herbicide 
resistance evolution (40, 41, 42, 43, 79, 85). The models have 
had limited application as predictive tools, however, because 
quantitative data on many parameters are lacking. As a result, 
various assumptions have been made concerning rates of muta- 
tion, initial frequencies of resistance, the inheritance of resis- 
tance, and the relative fitness of resistant and susceptible 
phenotypes. The impetus for this review arose out of a need to 
better understand and evaluate these assumptions since the theo- 
retical andlor empirical basis of many have not been explained 
fully in the literature. 
The objective of this paper is to critically review the theory 
and data on the major population genetic factors influencing the 
evolution of herbicide resistance in weed populations. The fac- 
tors discussed include mutation, selection, gene flow, mating 
pattern, and the inheritance of resistance. 
MUTATION TO RESISTANCE 
Genetic variation for resistance must be present in a suscep- 
tible weed population for the evolution of herbicide resistance to 
occur. The major source of genetic variation in an area where 
resistance has not been detected previously is likely to be gene 
mutation. In general, gene mutations conferring resistance to a 
specific herbicide class are not induced by application of the 
herbicide, but rather are believed to occur spontaneously. How- 
ever, it has been demonstrated that s-triazine herbicides can 
induce mutations at loci that do not confer herbicide resistance 
in a few non-weedy species (89). 
Spontaneous mutations at gene loci recur with characteristic 
frequency such that new mutations are continually being gener- 
ated in natural populations of plants, including weeds (19). 
Mutations at some loci, particularly those encoding herbicide 
sites of action, may confer resistance. Typical spontaneous mu- 
tation rates in biological organisms are often cited as 1 x low5or 
1 x lo4 gametes per locus per generation (84).These values have

INITIAL FREQUENCY OF RESISTANCE 
The frequency of mutant plants resistant to a particular herbi- 
cide prior to its initial use in a weed population has been esti- 
mated empirically in few studies. Matthews and Powles (76) 
obtained a frequency of 0.02 + 0.0092 plants resistant to diclo- 
fop-methyl in previously unsprayed farm populations and a 
frequency of < 0.002 resistant plants in non-farm populations of 
rigid ryegrass across southern Australia. Frequencies of triazine 
resistant mutants in seven populations of common lambsquarters 
(Chenopodium album L.) from private gardens in France ranged 
from 1 x lo4 to 3 x (22, 37). The values obtained for both 
species are higher than predicted based on expected rates of 
spontaneous mutation. Whether or not these estimates are typical 
of initial frequencies of herbicide resistant plants, in general, 
remains to be seen as additional data become available for other 
species and herbicide classes. 
Reliable empirical estimates of initial frequencies of resistant 
plants are difficult to obtain. Since frequencies are likely to be 
very low, accurate estimates require intensive sampling and the 
screening of enormous numbers of seeds or plants. For instance, 
if the frequency of resistant plants is 1.0 x lo4, an order of 
magnitude commonly assumed in models of herbicide resistance 
evolution (41, 79), a minimum of 3,000,000 seeds must be 
screened to be 95% confident of finding at least one resistant 
plant. 
Since data from field populations is lachng, and because of 
the inherent difficulty in obtaining reliable estimates of initial 
frequencies in the field, values are normally estimated theoreti- 
cally using population genetic equations. Several equations may 
be used, each with numerous underlying assumptions which may 
or may not be valid. At best, therefore, the estimates obtained are 
highly approximate. 
Using mutation-selection equilibrium theory, the frequency 
of resistance prior to herbicide exposure can be estimated by the 
equilibrium reached between the continual generation of resis- 
tance alleles by recurrent mutation (at mutation rate, y) and the 
selective disadvantage of the newly arisen resistant plant in the 
absence of herbicide (denoted by the selection coefficient, s) 
(48). The selection coefficient is defined such that the fitness of 
the most fit homozygote (the susceptible in this case) is one and 
the fitness of the least fit homozygote (resistant) is 1 -s. Its value 
is directly proportional to the intensity of selection against the 
least fit genotype. Thus, s = 0.01 if R plants are 99% as fit as S 
plants and s = 0.75 if R plants are 25% as fit as S plants.

instance, more than 500 generations are required for a population 
to reach equilibrium when a resistance mutation is recessive and

equal. It is unlikely, therefore, that weed populations are always 
in mutation-selection balance. When they are not, equilibrium 
frequencies may not closely approximate initial frequencies of 
resistance alleles. 
Thirdly, accurate estimates of s, the selective disadvantage of 
a newly arisen resistant plant in the absence of herbicide, are 
usually not available and require considerable effort to obtain. 
The initial frequency of resistance is of interest because it 
influences, to varying degrees, the number of generations for 
resistance to evolve in a weed population. Consider, for instance, 
a random mating population in which resistance to a particular 
herbicide is controlled by a single, dominant allele. A one hun- 
dred-fold difference in the initial frequency of resistance alleles 
is equivalent to a one generation difference in the time required 
for the frequency of resistant plants to reach levels between 0.02 
to 0.8 if a herbicide is 99% effective in killing susceptible weeds 
but has no effect on resistant plants, i.e. s = 0.99 (Figure 1A).

resistance. For example, when a herbicide confers moderately 
intense selection (s = 0.75), a one hundred-fold difference in the 
initial frequency of resistance alleles results in a three generation 
difference in the time required to reach a frequency of resistant 
plants ranging from 0.02 to 0.8 (Figure 1B).

INHERITANCE OF RESISTANCE 
Nuclear versus cytoplasmic inheritance. With one exception, 
resistance to all classes of herbicides examined to date is deter- 
mined by nuclear inheritance (Table 3). Transmission of resis- 
tance alleles thus occurs through pollen and ovules. In contrast, 
resistance to the triazine herbicides is inherited cytoplasmically 
in the vast majority of weed species. The gene conferring triazine 
resistance is located in the chloroplast genome (50). Transmis- 
sion of the chloroplast resistance gene largely occurs through the 
ovules of the maternal parent although a very low level of 
transmission by pollen has been observed in a few species (20, 
38). The sole exception to the cytoplasmic inheritance of triazine 
resistance is velvetleaf (Abutilon theophrasti Medicus). Resis- 
tance to the triazine herbicides in this species is controlled by a 
single, partially dominant, nuclear gene (4). 
The evolutionary significance of selection on a trait deter- 
mined by maternal cytoplasmic inheritance is the complete re- 
striction of genetic recombination and the ensuing rapid loss of 
allelic polymorphism in natural populations (17, 97). Since no 
segregation for resistance occurs in the seed offspring of a newly 
arisen resistant mutant, the application of triazine herbicides will 
quickly result in fixation of the resistance allele in a weed 
population. Moreover, immigration of susceptibility alleles from 
adjacent populations or the seedbank will not significantly delay 
the evolution of resistance, since genetic recombination among 
plants does not occur.

tance involves a single base substitution in the psbA chloroplast 
gene which codes for a Photosystem I1 membrane protein to 
which triazine herbicides bind (49,50). The expected frequency 
with which such a mutation occurs in the chloroplast genome and 
gives rise to gamete transmissible triazine resistance is very low. 
Amtzen and Duesing (6) and Duesing (27) derived an estimate 
of 1 x to 1 x 10-l2 mutations per gene locus. 
A nuclear gene, a plastome mutator, may be responsible for 
increasing the rate of spontaneous mutation of the chloroplast 
genome and the probability of triazine resistance mutations (6). 
Such a nuclear mutation-inducing gene has been reported in a 
few species of higher plants including triazine-resistant black 
nightshade (Solanurn nigrurn L.) (6,28, 114). In common lambs- 
quarters, a small percentage of plants, collected from populations 
that were never treated with herbicide, produced an unusually 
high number of offspring (between 1 and 12%) with the triazine 
resistance mutation. These may have possessed a nuclear mutator 
gene (9,22). 
Polygenic versus major gene inheritance. Most instances of 
adaptive evolution in plant and animal populations are achieved 
by the selection of phenotypes encoded by many genes each with 
small additive effect, i.e., polygenes (64). This is generally 
believed to be due to the fact that mutations with minor effects 
are present in populations at higher frequencies than those with 
major effects, which may be deleterious or pleiotropic (64, 122). 
In contrast, herbicide resistance is determined by major genes in 
the weeds examined to date. Moreover, in the majority of cases 
in which the number of genes has been determined, resistance is 
controlled by a single, major gene (Table 3). 
The predominance of major gene inheritance of herbicide 
resistance can be attributed to two factors. Firstly, most recently 
registered herbicides are highly target site specific and interfere 
with single enzymes in major metabolic pathways. Mutation of 
the gene encoding for the enzyme may alter a plant's sensitivity 
to the herbicide and result in resistance. Secondly, repeated 
application of these highly effective chemicals imposes strong 
selection, often causing 95-99% mortality, against susceptible 
phenotypes in weed populations. Adaptation is possible only if 
resistance genes are present in a population and have a suffi- 
ciently large phenotypic effect to allow the survival of a few 
individuals in a single generation (71). With polygenic inheri- 
tance, recombination among individuals for many generations is 
required to bring together a sufficient number of favorable alleles 
to produce a highly resistant phenotype. Polygenic inheritance 
of resistance is thus more likely under conditions of weak selec- 
tion as would occur with sublethal herbicide applications. 
Substantial continuous or quantitative variation in suscepti- 
bility to herbicides has been documented among, and within, 
populations of weeds (12, 24, 30, 31, 60, 91, 92, 106, 111). 
Estimates of heritability in a few of these studies (Table 4) 
indicate the potential for selection of increasing levels of resis- 
tance by repeated sublethal herbicide applications. Levels of 
resistance to ioxynil, MCPA, and simazine increased following 
artificial selection by herbicide in scentless mayweed [Tri-
pleurospermum inodorurn (L.)Schultz Bip.] (32). The presence 
of such heritable variation in response to low herbicide rates is 
sometimes erroneously interpreted as indicative of a high risk of 
a weed population evolving resistance to herbicides applied at 
field rates. This is not the case. As indicated above, few plants 
survive field rates of herbicides. The recombination and accumu- 
lation of favorable alleles increasing resistance to a herbicide 
among these plants, when such alleles are present at low frequen- 
cies, will not occur sufficiently rapidly to produce offspring that

Dominance. When nuclear inheritance is involved, herbicide 
resistance is conferred by an allele(s) that is at least partially 
dominant to the allele(s) for susceptibility in all but two species 
(Table 3). There are good theoretical reasons why resistance may 
largely be determined by dominant alleles. When a favored allele 
is rare, it will spread much faster in a population if it is at least 
partially dominant than if it is recessive since heterozygotes will 
manifest at least part of the phenotype of the homozygote under 
selection (15,61). Arare dominant resistance allele is thus much 
more likely to become established in a weed population, follow- 
ing herbicide treatment, than a recessive resistance allele. The 
significant role of dominant genes in adaptive evolution is not 
restricted to herbicide resistance. They appear to play an impor- 
tant role in a number of evolutionary processes, particularly if a 
species is forced to adapt rapidly to a new, strong selection 
pressure (84). 
A notable exception to the partially or completely dominant 
control of herbicide resistance is trifluralin resistance in green 
foxtail, which is determined by a single, recessive allele (62). 
The establishment and spread of a recessive resistance mutation 
in this species may be attributable to two features of its repro- 
ductive biology. Firstly, green foxtail is highly self-fertilized. 
Average selfing rates of plants from four field populations ex- 
ceeded 99% (unpublished data). Favorable recessive mutations 
are more likely to become established in a selfing population as 
opposed to an outcrossing population (15). Secondly, the prolific 
seed return of green foxtail, which can produce as many as 
12,000 seeds per plant (26), may have facilitated evolution of the 
recessive trait. 
Herbicide-induced resistance. Three biotypes of common 
lambsquarters differing in levels of resistance to atrazine (sus- 
ceptible [S], intermediate [I], and resistant [R]) are found in 
France. Whereas the R biotype is highly resistant to atrazine, the 
I biotype exhibits an intermediate response in terms of lethal dose 
and pattern of chlorophyll fluorescence following atrazine ab- 
sorption (23, 36, 37). After treatment with herbicide, however, 
type I plants give rise to fully resistant progeny that do not differ 
in response to atrazine from resistant plants collected in the field 
(9). 
The mechanism conferring full resistance to the progeny of 
type I plants is unknown. Type I plants have the same psbA gene 
mutation and their entire chloroplast populations are mutated as 
is characteristic of fully resistant plants (9). Moreover, type I 
plants do not differ from their herbicide-induced resistant [Ri] 
progeny in growth, reproduction, QAto QBelectron transfer, and 
thylakoid response to atrazine (9. 23). Yet I plants are not as 
resistant to atrazine as Ri plants and will produce fully resistant 
progenies only after herbicide treatment. 
The I to Ri transition may be due to nuclear control of 
chloroplast gene expression. Reciprocal crosses between S and 
I plants indicate that in the absence of herbicide, the unique 
characteristics of Type I mutants (intermediate lethal dose and 
intermediate fluorescence curve) are conferred by maternal cy- 
toplasmic inheritance (37). Similarly, crosses between S and Ri 
plants indicate that the Ri characteristics are inherited maternally 
and cytoplasmically (9). In contrast, reciprocal crosses between 
I and Ri phenotypes demonstrated maternal cytoplasmic inheri- 
tance in the F,, but the F2generation segregated I and R pheno- 
types in such a way that the maternal phenotype had the highest 
frequency. The results suggest incomplete cytoplasmic inheri- 
tance or nucleocytoplasmic interaction involving an unidentified 
nuclear component (9). Additional research is required to fully 
understand the genetic control of this unique expression of 
resistance. 
SELECTION BY HERBICIDES 
A unique feature of the evolution of herbicide resistance in 
weeds, in contrast to most evolutionary processes occurring in 
natural plant populations, is the intensity of selection imposed 
by herbicides. Most are applied at rates that result in the mortality 
of 90 to 99% of the susceptible weed seedlings in a field (41). If 
genetic variation for resistance is present due to mutation or gene 
flow, even at very low frequencies, repeated herbicide applica- 
tion will normally result in a rapid increase in the frequency of 
resistant individuals until they dominate the population. 
Because treatment with herbicides generally results in such a 
high mortality of susceptible plants, mathematical models have 
shown that selection pressure by herbicide is the most important 
factor determining the rate of enrichment of herbicide resistance 
in a weed population (40,41,42,43,79). The higher the intensity 
of selection against susceptible seedlings imposed by a particular 
herbicide, the faster the expected rate of evolution and spread of 
resistance (40,41). Several herbicide characteristics and patterns 
of use result in higher plant mortality than others, and thus 
impose a more intense selection pressure for the development of 
resistance. These include a single target site and highly specific

Selection pressure in the presence of herbicide treatment. 
Despite the importance of selection by herbicide on the rate of 
resistance evolution, accurate quantitative estimates of the inten- 
sity of selection by herbicide in terms of plant response are 
lacking. To date, most studies measure reductions in weed den- 
sity or biomass in response to varying herbicide dosages. Al- 
though the intensity of selection by herbicide in field populations 
can be approximated by indicators of plant mortality, the reduc- 
tion in seed return is a more accurate estimate of selection 
pressure. The reason for this is that seed production more closely 
reflects the change in relative numbers of resistant and suscepti- 
ble plants in a weed population in the next generation. Seed 
production assessed at the end of the growing season takes into 
account the influence of susceptible individuals that miss herbi- 
cide treatment and contribute progeny to the next generation. 
These individuals essentially reduce the effective selection pres- 
sure and slow the rate of resistance evolution (40). 
Gressel and Segel (41) have included seed production as a 
component of their estimate of selection pressure by measuring 
"effective kill." Effective lull is defined as the percentage reduc- 
tion in seed yield at the end of a growing season due to herbicide 
treatment. Values are obtained for both resistant and susceptible 
plants, and the selection pressure of a herbicide is calculated as 
the ratio of the fraction of resistant plants that survive herbicide 
application to the corresponding fraction of susceptible plants 
(41, 42, 43). Thus Gressel and Segel (41) define the selection 
pressure of a herbicide on a particular weed species as (1 -
effective kill of resistant plants) divided by (1 -effective kill of 
susceptible plants).

in a single study. Beckie and Morrison (8) measured the effective 
lull of green foxtail by trifluralin, applied preplant incorporated 
(PPI) in rapeseed and preemergence incorporated (PEI) in wheat. 
In addition to the measurement of effective kill, the authors also 
measured reductions in plant density and biomass due to herbi-

By convention, population geneticists measure selection pres- 
sure by the relative fitnesses of genotypes in the presence of a 
selective factor such as herbicide treatment (Table 5). Fitness is 
a measure of the number of gametes or progeny contributed by 
a genotype to the next generation. The relative fitness of geno- 
types is calculated by assigning a value of 1 to the genotype with 
the highest gametic or progeny contribution and values corre- 
sponding to their relative contributions to the remaining geno- 
types. The difference between the relative fitness of the most fit 
genotype and that of the least fit genotype is termed the selection 
coefficient (s) and is directly proportional to the intensity of 
selection against the least fit genotype. In the context of herbicide 
resistance evolution in weed populations, a relative fitness value 
of 1 would be assigned to fully resistant genotypes in the pres- 
ence of herbicide. Values corresponding to their relative repro- 
ductive success would then be assigned to the partially resistant 
and susceptible genotypes. The magnitude of the selection coef- 
ficient would then indicate the intensity of selection favoring the 
resistant genotype. This approach to estimating selection pres-

The intensity of selection for resistance imposed by a particu- 
lar herbicide, and thus the selection coefficient and relative 
fitness of resistant and susceptible genotypes, may vary due to 
the environment. Estimates are thus specific to the conditions, 
such as climate, weather, and soil type at the time of herbicide 
application. 
Density- and frequency-dependent effects commonly alter 
the selective environment of natural populations of plants. For 
example, the relative fitness of resistant and susceptible bio- 
types of blackgrass (Alopecurus myosuroides Huds.) grown in 
winter wheat was dependent on both the density and frequency 
of resistant and susceptible plants treated with chlorotoluron 
(85). At half and full recommended rates, reductions in seed 
production of the susceptible biotype due to density were greater 
than corresponding reductions in the resistant biotype. The in- 
tensity of selection against the chlorotoluron-susceptible biotype 
as estimated by the coefficient, S,, (85) thus increased towards 
1.0 with increasing density of the susceptible biotype but de- 
creased away from 1.0 with increasing density of the resistant 
biotype. 
Herbicide rotations. When herbicides are used in rotation, 
selection for resistance to a particular herbicide is generally 
restricted to the growing seasons during which the herbicide is 
applied. With the exception of those herbicides having long 
residual activity, the frequency of resistant plants is unlikely to 
increase more rapidly than the frequency of susceptible plants 
during the "herbicide-off' periods, and may even decline. The 
time to reach a given frequency of plants resistant to a specific 
herbicide will be extended by rotating the herbicide with various 
cultural practices or with herbicides differing in mode of action 
(42,43). 
Early models (40,41) predicted that resistance would evolve 
at a rate that was a function of the number of generations or 
seasons a weed was treated with a particular herbicide. Thus, if 
it took 8 yr for resistance to evolve in monoculture with annual 
applications of a particular herbicide, it would take 24 years in a 
rotation where the herbicide was used once in 3 yr. More recent 
models consider that resistant plants may have reduced fitness 
relative to susceptible plants in the absence of herbicide applica- 
tion, i.e., during the "off' seasons of a rotation (42, 43). The 
reduced fitness of resistant plants would further slow the evolu- 
tion of resistance.

frequency of resistant plants over time depends on the intensity 
of selection by a herbicide during the "herbicide-on" periods 
(which is equivalent to the fitness differential between resistant 
and susceptible plants during the "on" periods), the number of 
"herbicide-off' periods in a rotation, and the fitness differential 
between resistant and susceptible phenotypes during the "off' 
periods.

At a given selection intensity, the time to reach a specific 
frequency of resistant plants increases as the number of "off' 
years increases. For example, when s = 0.9 and resistant and 
susceptible phenotypes are equally fit, a weed population will 
become 80% resistant in approximately 7 yr if a herbicide group 
is applied repeatedly without rotation. However, to reach the 
same level of resistance in the population it will take 10 yr if the 
herbicide group is used 2 out of every 3 yr, and 20 yr if the 
herbicide group is used 1 out of every 3 yr. Thus herbicide 
rotation can be very effective in delaying the evolution of resis- 
tance. The effect of the number of "off' years is particularly 
evident when the herbicide applied during the "on" years im- 
poses relatively weak selection (s = 0.75 versus s = 0.99). If a 
herbicide exerts very strong selection during the "herbicide-on" 
seasons (s =0.99), a larger number of "off' periods in the rotation 
will be required to delay resistance. 
A fitness differential between resistant and susceptible plants 
during the "herbicide-off' periods will also affect the evolution 
of resistance (Figures 2A, B, C versus Figures 2D, E, and F). 
Resistant and susceptible plants are assumed to have equal fitness 
in Figures 2A, B, and C. The frequency of resistant plants neither 
increases nor decreases but remains constant in the absence of 
herbicide treatment. In contrast, resistant plants are assumed to 
be only 75% as fit as susceptible plants during the "herbicide-off' 
periods in Figures 2D, E, and F. The reduced fitness of resistant 
plants causes a decrease in the frequency of resistant plants in 
the absence of herbicide treatment. As a result, herbicide rota- 
tions are more effective in slowing the evolution of resistance in 
weed populations when a resistance mutation is associated with 
a reduction in plant fitness in the absence of herbicide. The 
magnitude of the fitness reduction also affects the rate at which 
a population returns to a predominantly susceptible state after 
use of a particular herbicide group is stopped (79).The larger the 
fitness cost, the more rapidly will the susceptible type replace the 
resistant type. 
Fitness in the absence of herbicide treatment. The assumption 
that resistant plants are less fit than susceptible plants in the 
absence of herbicide application is based on early studies of 
triazine resistance. These studies indicated a marked reduction 
in the reproductive success of s-triazine resistant biotypes rela- 
tive to susceptible biotypes of most species in the absence of 
triazine application (reviewed in 42). In addition, since many 
novel mutations in biological organisms have deleterious, pleio- 
tropic effects on an organism's likelihood of survival andlor 
reproduction (64), it generally is assumed that herbicide resis- 
tance mutations will be associated with an initial cost to a plant's 
fitness (53). 
Accurate quantitative estimates of the relative fitness of resis- 
tant and susceptible plants in the absence of herbicide are diffi- 
cult to obtain. Three major problems exist: 1) the choice of 
material for comparison, 2) the experimental conditions under 
which the fitnesses of resistant and susceptible types are com- 
pared, and 3) the characteristics used to measure fitness. 
In order to show causal effect of a resistance mutation on 
fitness reduction in an organism, isogenic resistant and suscep- 
tible lines must be compared. Only a few studies have made 
comparisons using isogenic lines (1 1, 39, 59, 81, 82, 109). All 
involved triazine resistant species and indicated that the triazine 
resistance mutation was associated with a cost in fitness in the 
species examined (Table 6). Fitness differences between nearly 
isogenic resistant and susceptible lines of spring rapeseed (Bras-
sica napus L.) were statistically significant when lines were 
grown together (in mixture), but were not significant when 
grown separately (in monoculture). 
Interactions between the resistance allele and the genetic 
background (the nuclear and chloroplast genome) have been 
examined in a single study. McCloskey and Holt (82) showed 
that there was a large differential temperature effect on the 
amount of biomass produced by common groundsel containing 
the same chloroplast genome but different nuclear genomes 
(triazine-resistant or -susceptible). Plants with the resistant nu- 
clear genome were better adapted to cooler growth temperatures 
while plants with the susceptible nuclear genome were better 
adapted to warmer growth conditions. In contrast, there was no 
differential temperature effect on biomass production of isonu- 
clear plants possessing resistant or susceptible chloroplast 
genomes. However, plants containing the susceptible chloroplast 
genome produced more biomass and had higher photosynthetic 
rates than those with the resistant chloroplast genome. Further 
research in other species is required to determine whether the 
cost of resistance varies in different genetic backgrounds and 
environments. 
The most appropriate fitness comparison to be used to esti- 
mate the delay in resistance evolution due to "herbicide-off' 
periods of a rotation is a comparison of the average fitness of 
resistant plants in the weed population with the average fitness 
of susceptible plants from the same population. Since in most 
circumstances resistant and susceptible plants from the same 
population cannot be distinguished in the absence of herbicide, 
studies have generally compared the fitness of resistant and 
susceptible plants collected from different populations (Table 6). 
A major drawback of these studies is that the fitness differential 
between resistant and susceptible plants may be confounded by 
differences in response to both abiotic and biotic environmental 
conditions among populations. If comparisons must be made 
using plants drawn from different populations, care must be 
taken to select populations from the same geographic area and 
growing conditions. Several resistant and susceptible popula- 
tions should be compared. A comparative study of one resistant 
and one susceptible population is unlikely to be truly repre- 
sentative. 
The experimental conditions under which the fitnesses of 
resistant and susceptible phenotypes are compared should reflect 
typical field conditions as closely as possible since relative 
fitness may vary due to the environment. The major drawback of 
most studies is that resistant and susceptible plants are grown 
neither in a crop nor in mixtures with one another (Table 6). 
Under normal field conditions, resistant and susceptible plants 
compete with each other and the crop. The term "productivity" 
has been used to describe the fitness of resistant and susceptible

plants when each type is grown in monoculture in contrast to 
"competitive fitness" which describes fitness under conditions 
of interbiotype competition (39). 
In addition to concerns regarding the genetic material and the 
experimental conditions used to compare the fitness of resistant 
and susceptible plants in the absence of herbicide, the variables 
measured to estimate fitness must reflect those that most directly 
result in changes in gene frequencies in weed populations. Most 
studies have measured plant biomass (typically, vegetative and 
reproductive dry weight) to estimate the relative fitness of resis- 
tant and susceptible phenotypes (Table 6). Assuming a phenotype 
has a higher fitness on the basis of dry matter production alone 
is likely to be subject to considerable error. In order to accurately 
predict rates of increase or decline in resistance due to herbicide 
rotation or the complete cessation of use of a particular herbicide 
group, measures of relative fitness must incorporate the differ- 
ential survival and reproduction (fecundity) of resistant and 
susceptible phenotypes. Survival and fecundity will be deter- 
mined by a number of factors such as seed viability, seedling 
vigour, competitive ability, and time to maturity. Maxwell et al. 
(79), for example, consider that the relative fitness of resistant 
and susceptible phenotypes is determined by the survival of 
seeds, seedlings, and mature plants, in addition to the fecundity 
(pollen and seed production) of each phenotype. 
Despite the difficulties associated with measuring fitness, 
some generalizations can be made with the data available to date. 
As stated previously, studies comparing isogenic lines indicate 
that triazine resistant phenotypes are either less fit, or as fit, as 
susceptible phenotypes (Table 6). Triazine resistant plants rarely 
exhibit greater fitness than susceptible plants for the characters 
measured to date. 
Environmental conditions influenced the relative fitnesses of 
triazine resistant and susceptible phenotypes to varying degrees. 
Resistant plants were less fit when grown in mixture with sus- 
ceptible plants than when grown in pure stands in some studies 
(1, 39, 120), but not in others (18, 109). Differences in light 
intensity also affected the relative fitness of resistant and suscep- 
tible phenotypes of common groundsel (55). Fitness was shown 
to vary among populations of a species when several resistant 
and susceptible populations were examined (74, 115). Thus, 
although triazine resistant phenotypes appear to be less fit than 
susceptible phenotypes, on average, the magnitude of the fitness 
reduction and, therefore, the effectiveness of rotations in slowing 
the evolution of triazine resistance in weed populations, is likely 
to vary with environmental conditions and among populations 
of a species. The relative fitness of triazine resistant and suscep- 
tible biotypes in the absence of triazine treatment is reviewed in 
greater detail by Holt (52), Radosevich and Holt (93), and 
Warwick (116). 
Few studies have compared the fitness of plants resistant and 
susceptible to the nontriazine herbicides (Table 6). It is thus 
difficult to draw any generalizations. In studies examining a large 
number of populations andlor fitness variables, differences be- 
tween resistant and susceptible phenotypes were found for some 
variables but not for others (2, 3, 75, 87). A single study (85) 
examined the fitness of resistant and susceptible phenotypes in 
the presence of crop competition. In the absence of herbicide, the 
per capita seed production of chlorotoluron resistant and suscep- 
tible biotypes of blackgrass was observed to be both density- and 
frequency-dependent when grown in mixtures in the presence of 
winter wheat. Susceptible plants produced more seed than resis- 
tant plants at low densities, whereas the reverse was true at high 
densities. 
WEED MATING SYSTEMS 
One of the underlying assumptions of most mathematical 
models of herbicide resistance evolution is random mating 
among plants in weed populations (40,41,42,43,85). However, 
many of the world's major weeds, including several that have 
developed resistance to herbicides, are highly self-fertilizing (1 3, 
14). Selfing in a plant population has the effect of greatly 
increasing the probability and rate of evolution of favorable 
recessive mutations (15). 
A newly arisen resistance mutation will initially be present in 
the heterozygous state. Thus, its probability of survival in a 
population will depend on its degree of dominance. If the muta- 
tion is dominant, it will survive a herbicide treatment. If, on the 
other hand, it is recessive, the plant carrying the mutation will be 
susceptible to herbicide treatment. Therefore, a resistant plant 
would only occur if the mutant escaped herbicide treatment and 
mated with another plant carrying the mutation. In a highly 
outcrossing weed species, the very few heterozygotes that may 
survive herbicide treatment will most likely mate with homozy- 
gous, dominant, susceptible plants because of the lack or extreme 
rarity of plants carrying the resistance allele in the population. 
Therefore, most of the progeny from heterozygotes would be 
susceptible to herbicide. A recessive resistance mutation is thus 
unlikely to spread in response to selection by herbicide in com- 
parison with a resistance gene with some degree of dominant 
expression. In general, natural selection strongly favors nonre- 
cessive favorable mutations in random mating or predominantly 
outcrossing species (15, 45, 83). Several examples of adaptive 
evolutionary change, such as insecticide resistance, industrial 
melanism (the evolution of dark-colored wings in peppered 
moths inhabiting industrial areas), and heavy metal tolerance in 
plants support this generalization (72). 
In contrast to outcrossing, selfing in a plant population greatly 
increases the probability and rate of evolution of a favorable 
recessive mutation (15). With selfing, 25% of the progeny from 
a heterozygous plant that escaped herbicide treatment will be 
resistant. By rapidly increasing the frequency of recessive ho- 
mozygotes, selfing reduces the chance of loss of a newly arisen 
recessive mutation. As a result, in highly self-fertilizing species, 
recessive resistance mutations will spread at approximately the 
same rate as dominant resistance mutations (Figure 3). One 
might expect, therefore, that recessive genes conferring herbi- 
cide resistance would have a good chance of establishment in 
populations of highly selfing weed species.

genetic basis of herbicide resistance differs among weed species

of trifluralin resistance in green foxtail, a species with a selfing 
rate that exceeds 9996, supports the hypothesis that recessive 
control of herbicide resistance, if observed, will occur in pre- 
dominantly self-fertilizing weeds (62). Nuclear inheritance of 
herbicide resistance in the remaining species in which genetic 
studies have been performed, including those that are predomi- 
nantly self-fertilizing, appears to be determined by dominant 
genes (Table 3). An alternative hypothesis for the frequent estab- 
lishment of dominant resistance mutations is that they involve 
gain-of-function or altered-function changes whereas recessive 
mutations involve loss-of-function changes. Additional data on 
the genetics and mechanisms of herbicide resistance, and the 
mating systems of weed species, are required to rule out one or 
the other explanation.

INTERPOPULATION GENE FLOW 
Spread of resistance among weed populations. Gene flow, via 
pollen or seed, from a field with herbicide resistant plants to an 
adjacent or nearby field with susceptible plants may provide an 
initial source of resistance genes, similar to mutation, for the 
evolution of herbicide resistance. Rates of gene flow are gener- 
ally believed to be higher than rates of mutation and therefore 
would result in a higher frequency of plants resistant to a particu- 
lar herbicide prior to its initial application. Therefore, gene flow 
and the resultant increase in initial frequency of resistance genes 
would reduce the time required to reach a specific level of 
resistance within a field once the herbicide is applied (Figure 1). 
The relative importance of mutation versus gene flow as a 
source of resistance genes in susceptible weed populations is 
unknown. Mutation is likely to result in the initial appearance of 
herbicide resistance in a particular geographic area whereas gene 
flow is more likely to bring about its spread among populations 
in a region. Afew studies of isozyme variation in triazine resistant 
and susceptible populations of common lambsquarters support 
this hypothesis. Distinct isozyme phenotypes were observed in 
triazine resistant populations from different regions of France 
(36). The same isozyme phenotype was found in populations 
from a geographical location, however. Similar results were 
obtained for triazine resistant populations in Canada (I 19). The 
data suggest that mutation for resistance in populations of com- 
mon lambsquarters occurred independently in the separate geo- 
graphic regions but that spread of the resistant type within a 
region was likely due to gene flow. 
Until recently, the role of gene flow in the spread of herbicide 
resistance among weed populations has largely been ignored. 
One reason may be that rates of gene flow between plant popu- 
lations in natural environments are generally believed to be too 
low to have much evolutionary significance (29,45,67,69,70). 
On average, levels of gene flow are estimated to be less than 1 % 
among populations separated by a few hundred meters and at 
least two orders of magnitude less between populations 1.5 km 
or more apart for most plant species (68, 70). In many highly 
self-fertilizing species, effective gene flow between populations 
is expected to be so low as to approach rates of mutation (46). 
More recent studies, however, challenge the widely held view 
that interpopulation gene flow is highly limited. Migration rates 
ranging from 0.5 to 5.5 individuals per generation have been 
inferred from statistical analyses of allele distributions among

plant populations (102, 103, 104). In addition, studies based on 
paternity-exclusion analysis (25) have shown that a significant 
proportion of a population's seed crop can be fathered by plants 
from outside of the population. For instance, 6.3 to 17.9% of the 
seeds assayed from five small populations of wild radish 
(Raphanus sativus L.), a self-incompatible, insect-pollinated 
weed, were estimated to be fathered by foreign pollen from 
populations at least 100 m away (25,33). Such rates of gene flow 
could lead to the rapid spread of a herbicide resistance gene 
among weed populations in a particular area. 
Empirical data on levels of gene flow between weed popula- 
tions of herbicide resistant species are lacking. Pollen movement 
of resistance genes within populations, however, has been meas- 
ured in two species: sulfonylurea resistant kochia [Kochia sco- 
paria (L.) Schrad.] and diclofop-methyl resistant Italian ryegrass 
(Lolium multijlorum Lam.). Outcrossing of resistant pollen to 
susceptible plants was 1.4% at a distance of 28.9 m in kochia and 
1%at > 6.84 m in Italian ryegrass (77, 108). In a separate study, 
kochia pollen was recovered as far as 50 m from its source 
indicating that pollen dispersal could lead to long distance spread 
of herbicide resistance genes (86). Thus, the establishment of 
new sulfonylurea resistant kochia populations in previously sus- 
ceptible fields through gene flow is possible. 
Although pollen dispersal has generally been assumed to be 
the major mechanism of interpopulation gene flow in plants, seed 
dispersal may play a far greater role in weed populations. Many 
weed species are highly self-fertilizing and pollen flow is prob- 
ably minimal. Indirect evidence of the importance of seed move- 
ment in determining the spread of resistant weeds can be obtained 
from information on the introduction and spread of weeds in a 
region. Weed seed movement due to agricultural implements can 
be considerable (78,80). Grain harvesting equipment, in particu- 
lar, may be an important mechanism of weed seed dispersal 
among fields. For instance, roughly 3% of the seed of the 
black-seeded biotype of wild-proso millet (Panicurn miliaceum 
L.) in one field was moved to a second field by combine harvest- 
ers (80). Movement of harvesting equipment from one field to 
another may similarly disperse herbicide resistant seed into 
susceptible populations. 
Immigration of susceptibility genes. The immigration of genes 
for susceptibility to a particular herbicide into a weed population 
undergoing selection by that herbicide can slow the evolution of 
resistance. Immigration of susceptibility genes can occur by 
pollen or seed dispersal from nearby susceptible weed popula- 
tions or as a result of the germination of susceptible seed from 
the seed bank. The effectiveness of gene flow in opposing 
selection for resistance will depend on the intensity of selection 
by herbicide, the inheritance of resistance, and the rate of immi- 
gration of genes for susceptibility into the weed population (5). 
It has been suggested that immigration of susceptibility genes 
into a weed population that has evolved resistance can lead to its 
"recovery" or return to susceptibility. For instance, Maxwell's 
RSIM (Resistance Simulation) model shows that 2 yr after 
abandoning herbicide use, the proportion of resistant plants in a

CONCLUSIONS 
Many factors have been shown to be important in determining 
the evolution of herbicide resistance. The source of genetic 
variation for resistance (mutation or immigration), the initial 
frequency of resistance alleles, fitness in the presence and/or 
absence of herbicide, a weed's mating system, and the inheri- 
tance of resistance are among those discussed in this review. 
Relative fitness of resistant and susceptible plants in the 
presence of herbicide is the single most important factor in the

determination of the rate of evolution of herbicide resistance. At 
any given selection pressure, the initial frequency of the resistant 
plants will determine the time required to reach a specific fre- 
quency of resistant plants. Accurate measures of initial frequency 
are difficult to obtain for a given population and will depend on 
mutation rate, gene flow, mating system, and inheritance of 
resistance. Mating system and nature of inheritance will further 
influence the rate of evolution of resistance. 
Rotating a herbicide with various cultural practices or with 
herbicides differing in mode of action will have the largest 
impact on delaying the time to reach a high frequency of plants 
resistant to the herbicide. The larger the number of "herbicide- 
off' years, the longer the delay. Differences in fitness between 
resistant and susceptible plants during the "herbicide-off' peri- 
ods will significantly delay resistance only if the susceptible 
plants are substantially more fit than the resistant plants. Simi- 
larly, maintaining a source of susceptible plants to dilute the 
frequency of resistant plants is not likely to be effective or 
desirable. 
Mathematical models can assist in assessing different strate- 
gies to delay the evolution of resistance. Unfortunately, it is 
unlikely that models will be able to accurately predict a specific 
time to resistance for any given population because of the inher- 
ent difficulties in obtaining accurate measures of many important 
factors. Models should continue to be used to assess the effec- 
tiveness of strategies to delay resistance evolution, but the lirni- 
tations in their ability to predict specific outcomes must be 
recognized.
