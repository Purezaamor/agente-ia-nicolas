Pivotal periods for pregnancy loss during the ﬁrst trimester
of gestation in lactating dairy cows

abstract
Loss of pregnancy can occur at many different stages of gestation and for a variety of causes
but clearly produces a negative impact for reproductive and economic performances of
dairy herds. This review describes four pivotal periods for pregnancy loss during the ﬁrst
trimester of gestation and discusses possible causes for pregnancy failure during these
periods. The ﬁrst period occurs during the ﬁrst week after breeding with lack of fertil-
ization and death of the early embryo producing major losses in pregnancy, particularly
under speciﬁc environmental and hormonal conditions. In general, 20%–50% of high-
producing lactating dairy cows have already experienced pregnancy loss during the ﬁrst
week of gestation with methods to decrease pregnancy loss during this period targeting
improved oocyte quality by alleviating heat stress, inﬂammatory diseases, and body con-
dition loss, and by increasing progesterone concentrations during preovulatory follicle
development. The second pivotal period, from Days 8 to 27, encompasses embryo elon-
gation and the classical “maternal recognition of pregnancy” period with losses averaging
w30% but with surprising variation between farms (25%–41%). Maintenance of the CL of
pregnancy is produced by the embryonic signal interferon-tau and alteration in uterine
secretory patterns of prostaglandins F2a, E1, and E2. Failures or delays in trophoblast
elongation and/or embryonic development result in loss of pregnancy during the second
pivotal period possibly due to suboptimal histotroph. The third pivotal period is during the
second month of pregnancy, Days 28 to 60, with losses of w12% based on a summary of
published results from more than 20,000 pregnancies in high-producing dairy cows. De-
lays or defects in development of the chorioallantoic placentomes or embryo result in CL
regression or embryo death during this pivotal period. Finally, a fourth period during the
third month of pregnancy has reduced pregnancy losses (w2%), compared with the ﬁrst
three periods but can be elevated in some cows, particularly in those carrying twins in the
same uterine horn. Thus, there are varied causes for pregnancy losses during each pivotal
period that correspond to key physiological changes in the embryo, uterine environment,
and ovary. Similarly, strategies to reduce these losses are likely to require a multifaceted
approach using rational methods that target the critical physiology in each pivotal period.

1. Introduction
1.1. Deﬁning the pivotal periods during the ﬁrst trimester of
bovine pregnancy
Reproductive efﬁciency is suboptimal in lactating dairy
cows. Improvements in reproduction can improve proﬁt-
ability of dairy farms. One of the factors that reduces
reproductive performance in some dairy herds is a high
rate of pregnancy loss. In many studies, only 30% or less of
high-producing lactating cows calve after a single AI.
However, this rate can vary substantially on the basis of
environmental, genetic, and management conditions.
Conceptus is the embryo plus the associated extraembry-
onic tissues (combination of embryonic and trophectoderm
cells early on that differentiate into trophoblast and then
placenta). Pregnancy loss per day generally decreases as
pregnancy progresses and is much lower after Day 60 of
pregnancy [1–5]. However, pregnancy loss during later
stages of gestation can be increased due to speciﬁc genetic
abnormalities [5] and speciﬁc bacterial, protozoal, or viral
infections [6–10], such as BVDV, BHV-1, Tritrichomonas
foetus, Neospora caninum, Brucella abortus, Leptospira, and
Campylobacter foetus. In this review, we will consider a few
speciﬁc concepts related to loss of pregnancy that are not
related to a major genetic abnormality or infectious disease
during the ﬁrst trimester of gestation in high-producing
lactating dairy cows. A central idea of this review is that
there are two fundamental goals during early pregnancy in
ruminants. First, initiate, establish, and maintain a healthy
pregnancy. Second, in the absence of success in the ﬁrst
process, reinitiate the process to provide another oppor-
tunity for pregnancy at the earliest possible time. From
these two goals originate the idea of pivotal or crucial
periods, when the embryo encounters a physiological
gateway and the uterus/dam makes a “decision” about the
“health” of the pregnancy and eventually either continues
the current pregnancy or resets the whole process by
eliminating the “substandard” conceptus and the source of
progesterone (P4), the CL. Finally, there is reinitiation of the
estrous cycle and reorganization of the uterine environ-
ment to be compatible with gamete transport and a return
to the initial stages of pregnancy.
Our objective is to ﬁrst deﬁne the normal stages of
pregnancy and the embryonic requirements to proceed
through each pivotal period. Second, to chronicle the
amount of pregnancy loss that occurs in lactating dairy
cattle during each pivotal period. Finally, to speculate
about why pregnancy losses are occurring during each
pivotal period. Thus, the ultimate goal for this review is to
not only provide some chronology and potential mecha-
nisms that underlie pregnancy loss but to also aid in the
rational development of management programs and
treatments to reduce pregnancy loss in lactating dairy
cattle. This article will not evaluate a number of important
topics that have been the subject of recent reviews
including:roleofspeciﬁc diseases in pregnancy loss
[11,12],speciﬁc haplotypes or genotypes that decrease
fertility or increase incidence of pregnancy loss in
different breeds of dairy heifers or cattle [5,13–18],and
epigenetic effects on pregnancy loss [19,20].
2. Pivotal period 1, ﬁrst week of pregnancy:
fertilization, embryonic genome activation, and
blastocyst development
The ﬁrst pivotal period of pregnancy is deﬁned as the
ﬁrst week after breeding in ruminants, with failure during
this period resulting in either lack of fertilization or death
of the early embryo. Failure of pregnancy during this
pivotal period results in reinitiation of the estrous cycle as if
pregnancy had not been initiated. Although there is
communication between the ruminant embryo and
oviduct/uterus during this early period, these effects are
likely to be localized. This is illustrated by the lack of a
detectable change in oviductal gene expression in response
to a single embryo, although clear transcriptional alter-
ations were detected after transfer of 50 embryos to the
oviduct [21].
Although technically not embryonic death, fertilization
failure or failure of ﬁrst cleavage can be an important factor
determining the reproductive efﬁciency of dairy cows.
As shown in Table 1, fertilization failure occurs in 10%–20%
of the cows that are inseminated with high-fertility sperm
in cool weather conditions [3,24,25,27,28]. Nevertheless,
speciﬁc conditions have been reported to reduce fertiliza-
tion [23,27,29,30]. Many of the factors that lead to fertil-
ization failure are likely related to lack of proper gamete
transport preventing contact of the sperm and oocyte
such as: improper AI technique [31], presence of poly-
morphonuclear cells in the uterus [32], and small eleva-
tions in circulating P4 near the time of AI due to lack of
complete regression of the CL, particularly in cows bred to
timed AI [33,34]. In addition, poor-quality sperm or oocyte
could increase fertilization failure. For example, heat stress
increased fertilization failure (Table 1) in normally ovulat-
ing dairy cattle in most [23,26,27] but not all [22] studies.
This appears to be due to an oocyte problem because
accessory sperm are present on unfertilized oocytes [23],
and this effect can be mimicked in oocytes that were
exposed to heat stress in vitro [35]. In addition, increased
circulating insulin during the ﬁnal week of follicle growth
reduced percentage of oocytes that were fertilized in
superovulated cows but through unknown mechanisms
[29]. Finally, inﬂammatory diseases in the ﬁrst weeks of
lactation reduced fertilization in single-ovulating dairy

Fertilization and the ﬁrst 3–5 days of embryonic
development occur in the oviduct, which provides a unique
environment, in terms of amino acids, energy substrates,
and ion concentrations, as compared with the uterine horn
[36–38]. Although the oviduct, as the name implies, is
clearly the passageway for the ova and embryo to reach the
uterus, there also are important physiological actions of
this tissue. The oviductal isthmus cells clearly bind, store,
and optimally capacitate spermatozoa, thus augmenting
efﬁciency of the fertilization process and reducing the
likelihood of polyspermy [39–41]. In addition, culturing
embryos in the presence of oviductal cells optimizes
embryonic development [39,42,43]. There is also a distinct
temporal and spatial pattern of transcriptional changes in
the oviduct that are regulated by circulating concentrations
of P4 and estradiol (E2). Endoscopic transfer of in vitro-
produced (IVP) embryos into the oviduct on Day 2, and
ﬂushing of embryos from the uterus on Day 7 has provided
evidence that the oviduct of a lactating cow is compro-
mised with fewer embryos developing to the blastocyst
stage, as compared with nonlactating cows [44]. In addi-
tion, similar studies compared embryo development in
heifers with normal or elevated circulating P4 and reported
no differences in percentage of embryos developing to
blastocyst, but distinct differences in embryonic gene
expression because of elevated P4 [45]. Thus, accumulating
evidence is consistent with a critical physiological role of
the oviduct in fertilization and optimal early embryo
development; however, further research is still needed to
deﬁne the precise oviductal mechanisms and practical
interventions that could reduce pregnancy loss [39].
Early embryo development entails a series of coordinated
and appropriately timed cell divisions occurring within the
zona pellucida that result in decreasing cellular size with
each subsequent cell division. The ﬁrst stages of embryo
development depend on maternal mRNAs and proteins that
are stored in the oocyte [46]. A key part of the differentiation
process is degradation of these maternal mRNA and proteins
and activation of transcription of the embryonic genome,
which is termed the maternal-to-embryonic transition
[47–49]. In bovine, inhibition of embryonic transcription, by
a-amanitin treatment, does not inhibit initial cleavage or
development of the embryo to the 8- to 16-cell stage,
consistent with embryonic genome activation being near
this time [50]. In addition, labeling with [3H] uridine is
generally consistent with the activation of the embryonic
genome near the 16-cell stage, although some embryonic
proteins begin to be transcribed at the 4-cell (8 proteins) and
8-cell (23 proteins) stage [50,51]. A complete characteriza-
tion of approximately 7400 genes of early embryonic tran-
scriptome activation [51] indicated that genes activated at
the 4-cell stage or before were functionally related to RNA
processing and transport and mRNA translation. These initial
proteins allow for successful embryonic genome activation
with transcription of a broad range of genes at the 8-cell
stage. At the 16-cell stage, transcription of additional genes
involved in transcription and translation were induced. By
the blastocyst stage, many distinct genes had been activated
including genes that were regulators of cellular lineage dif-
ferentiation. Thus, a decisive point in early embryonic
development is activation of the embryonic genome and
without this activation; development does not proceed
beyond the 16-cell stage.
During expression of the maternal mRNA, cells maintain
totipotency, however, with degradation of the maternal
mRNA and protein and activation of the embryonic
genome, the embryonic cells acquire the ability to differ-
entiate into distinct cell lineages. By the blastocyst stage,
the conceptus is characterized by a group of cells near one
pole of the embryo known as the inner cell mass, a ﬂuid-
ﬁlled blastocoele cavity, and an outer epithelial-like layer
termed the trophectoderm that will give rise to the
placenta. The inner cell mass has two distinct cell lineages
with the primitive endoderm near the ﬂuid-ﬁlled blasto-
coele cavity eventually forming the amniotic sac and the
inner-most, pluripotent cells of the epiblast eventually
differentiating into the embryo proper [52]. Thus, normal
development from the 16-cell stage to blastocyst, not only
requires rapid cell division but also accurate differentiation
of these distinct cellular lineages from the totipotent cells
of the very early embryo.
One major purpose for this review is to develop a
rational for the physiological basis for pregnancy loss,
including death of the embryo during the ﬁrst week of
pregnancy. Initial evidence was provided by early obser-
vations of large follicles and low fertility in cows synchro-
nized by extended progestin treatments, as previously
reviewed [53]. A model system was developed in which a
persistent follicle developed by induction of extended
follicular growth in a reduced P4/progestin environment.
Cows with persistent follicles had lesser fertility than cows
that ovulated younger follicles [54–56]. A study of persis-
tent follicles in beef cattle [57], recovered oocytes/embryos
on Day 6 after breeding and found similar fertilization rate
(97%) but a greatly reduced (P < 0.001) percentage of
embryos developed beyond the 16-cell stage in cows with
persistent follicle (14%) compared with cows that ovulated
growing follicles (86%). Thus, at least in the persistent fol-
licle model, inhibition of early embryo development
occurred near the time of embryonic genome activation.
This appears to be related to ovulation of an excessively
large, overly stimulated follicle [53,57] with oocytes that
may have premature germinal vesicle breakdown [58].In
addition, growth and ovulation of a persistent dominant
follicle produces changes in the oviductal secretory pro-
teins [59] that also could alter early embryo development.
Consistent with this alternative or additional mechanism
for altered fertility in cows ovulating larger or persistent
follicles, size of the ovulatory follicle in beef cattle produced
differences in spatial patterns of oviductal gene expression
[60].
In lactating dairy cows, a similar problem as observed in
the persistent follicle model may exist. Lactating dairy
cattle ovulate larger follicles than heifers or than similar
age/size nonlactating cows [61–63]. In addition, size of the
ovulatory follicle is related to the level of milk production
of the cow [64]. Recovery of oocytes/embryos during the

ﬁrst week after breeding (Tables 1 and 2) [65] shows that,
in the absence of heat stress, generally fertilization rate is
high but that only about 50% of the embryos are viable by
Day 7 after breeding to an estrus (Table 2). In addition, the
duration of follicle persistence in lactating dairy cows did
not alter fertilization rate, but dramatically increased per-
centage of embryos classiﬁed as degenerate [28]. Likewise,
lactating dairy cows that ovulated a persistent follicle at a
timed-AI protocol, had less (P ¼ 0.06) pregnancy per AI at
Day 31 than cows ovulating younger follicles (20.6% vs.
43.3%; [34]). Furthermore, follicles of similar age but
exposed to lower P4 during preovulatory follicle develop-
ment had no change in fertilization rate but an increased
percentage of degenerate embryos [33]. Similarly,
increased P4 concentrations during superstimulation with
FSH increased the subsequent quality of the embryos that
were recovered on Day 7 after ovulation [66]. Thus,
lactating dairy cows have increased degeneration of
embryos during the ﬁrst week of pregnancy related to
either ovulation of a larger, possibly persistent follicle and
potentially related to inadequate circulating P4 during the
ﬁnal stages of preovulatory follicle growth. The precise
mechanisms that produce this increase in early embryo
degeneration could be related to overstimulation by LH
pulses which occur in response to reduced circulating P4.
In addition, increased duration of follicle growth could
also expose the follicle/oocyte to more LH pulses. Moreover,
the oviduct of lactating dairy cows ovulating a larger follicle
appears to be suboptimal for embryo development
compared with nonlactating cows [44].
Another intriguing result that is consistent with the
concept that reduced circulating P4 during preovulatory
follicle development is a major factor in the increased
pregnancy loss during the ﬁrst week of pregnancy comes
from studies that have eliminated these problems using
optimized hormonal treatments of lactating cows. Ovsynch
is a hormonal synchronization program that allows timed AI
of lactating dairy cows with pregnancies per AI similar to
thoseobservedincowsinestrus[67–70]. Initiation of
Ovsynch on Day 6 or 7 of the estrous cycle increases fertility,
generally above what is achieved with Ovsynch or AI to
estrus [69]. Giving the ﬁrst GnRH on Day 6 or 7 allows
ovulation of the dominant follicle of the ﬁrst follicular wave,
thus increasing circulating P4 during preovulatory follicle
development because of the presence of two CL. At the end
of an Ovsynch protocol initiated on Day 6 or 7, the ﬁnal
GnRH allows ovulation of a smaller ovulatory follicle that is
similar to the size that is ovulated by nulliparous cows,
approximately 15–16 mm [71–73]. One study [28] evaluated
embryo quality in lactating dairy cows that were ﬂushed
from cows on Day 7 of pregnancy after initiation of Ovsynch
on Day 6 of the estrous cycle. Overall, 73 fertilized ova
were collected from cows that ovulated to the ﬁrst GnRH
of Ovsynch and only six of these structures were degenerate
embryos (8.2%) with 82.1% of the embryos being graded
as excellent or good quality. This contrasts with cows
that ovulated follicles of longer follicular dominance
(8.5–11.5 days) in which more than 50% of the embryos were
degenerate or of poor quality [28]. Our laboratory has
recently completed a study [74] evaluating Day 7 embryo
quality in single-ovulating cows with preovulatory follicle
development occurring in low compared with high-P4
concentrations, with both treatments having the same
duration of follicular dominance. We found a greater
(P ¼ 0.02) percentage of cows with grades one and two
embryos in those receiving the high compared with the
low-P4 treatment (86.5% [32/37] vs. 61.5% [16/26]). Thus, in
high-producing lactating dairy cows when circulating P4
during preovulatory follicle development are increased
and cows ovulate a smaller follicle, embryo quality on Day
7 is greatly improved and is similar to our results on embryo
quality in heifers [23]. All of these results and other results
[33] are consistent with the idea that one major reason for
high-pregnancy loss during the ﬁrst week of pregnancy in
lactating dairy cows is the ovulation of an oocyte from an
overstimulated follicle due to reduced circulating P4 during
preovulatory follicle development, increased duration of
follicle dominance, and/or ovulation of a larger follicle.

One other intriguing idea was ﬁrst introduced by Britt
[75], who postulated that energy status during the early
postpartum period could alter follicular/oocyte quality
resulting in negative effects on subsequent fertility in
lactating dairy cows. This idea was supported by results
showing that the amount of body condition score (BCS) loss
between parturition and ﬁrst AI was related to the fertility
of lactating dairy cows on commercial dairies [2,76,77].We
evaluated the Britt hypothesis in superstimulated lactating
dairy cows by evaluating the effect of early postpartum
body weight loss on embryo quality from cows in multiple
ovulation treatment [77]. There was no effect of body
weight loss on number of ovulations, total embryos
collected, or percentage of oocytes that were fertilized;
however, the percentage of fertilized oocytes that were
transferable embryos was less for cows that had the largest
amount of postpartum body weight loss. In addition, the
percentage of degenerate embryos was the greatest for
cows with the greatest body weight loss during the post-
partum period [77]. Thus, the impact of changes in BCS
during the early postpartum period on subsequent fertility
at ﬁrst AI could be partially explained by the reduction in
embryo quality and increase in degenerate embryos by Day
7 after AI in cows that lose excessive amounts of weight
postpartum, consistent with the hypothesis introduced by
Britt [75]. Other potential effects of nutrition during pre-
ovulatory follicle growth on subsequent early embryo
development in lactating cows, including the effects of
high-energy diets, increased insulin, or altered circulating
amino acids, have been previously reviewed [29,78].In
addition, the effect of lactation on early embryo develop-
ment could be mediated by an inadequate oviduct/uterine
environment to support early embryo development
because fewer IVP embryos developed to the blastocyst
stage in lactating compared with nonlactating cows [44].
Finally, recent work by our group indicates that disease in
early lactation not only reduces fertilization but also com-
promises embryo quality in lactating dairy cows [30].
Obviously, disease and weight loss are associated, such that
cows that develop illnesses in early lactation also lose more
body weight and condition score.
Thus, between 10% and greater than 50% of potential
pregnancies are lost during the ﬁrst week of pregnancy. It
may be possible to reduce early pregnancy losses by opti-
mizing circulating P4 concentrations during preovulatory
follicle growth, reducing size of ovulatory follicle, reducing
heat stress, and improving metabolic state of the lactating
dairy cows.
3. Pivotal period 2, Days 8–27: elongation, interferon-
tau (IFNT), and maintenance of the CL
The second pivotal period encompasses the time of
normal luteolysis or “maternal recognition of pregnancy”
that occurs from Days 16–25 in cattle [79–83]. Our ratio-
nale for choosing this extended period that continues
beyond the normal time of luteolysis is on the basis of
the practical consideration of being able to deﬁnitively
diagnose pregnancy on Day 28, using either ultrasound or
pregnancy-associated glycoproteins (PAGs). During this
pivotal period, the conceptus dramatically increases in
size through the process of elongation, particularly the
trophoblast cells, ultimately leading to CL maintenance
during maternal recognition of pregnancy. Alternatively,
inadequate communication between the conceptus, uterus,
and ovary produces failure of CL maintenance [84] and
redirection of the physiology toward luteolysis and a
decline in circulating P4 resulting in loss of any conceptus.
The loss of pregnancy during this time period may produce
no change in time of luteolysis or a delay of luteolysis until
Day 25–30.
During this pivotal period, the embryo emerges from
the zona pellucida and commences a free-ﬂoating state of
almost 2 weeks with dramatic trophoblastic elongation,
concluding with distinct development of the embryo and
placenta. Concurrently, three critical events occur: (1) The
trophoblast/embryo grows more than 200 fold from a
blastocyst of w0.15-mm diameter to an elongated struc-
ture of more than 40 cm in length with trophoblastic tis-
sues reaching through both uterine horns [85].This
growth and cellular differentiation is fueled entirely by
transport of nutrients from the histotroph to the cells of
the embryo/trophoblast. There is subsequent transition to
a choriovitelline type of nutrition with development of the
amnion, yolk sac, and circulatory system of the embryo
near the end of this period. (2) The embryo must signal its
presence to the maternal system to maintain the CL and
assure continuation of elevated P4 concentrations that are
essential for preserving pregnancy. (3) The embryo must
fend off the maternal immune system that would other-
wise trigger an immune response to the paternally
inherited polymorphic proteins that can be recognized as
alloantigens by the maternal immune system and could
potentially terminate the pregnancy.
Optimal elongation of the embryo involves a complex
cross-talk between the conceptus and the maternal system
[86]. The conceptus secretes local regulatory factors, most
notably IFNT, that regulate the uterine endometrium,
stimulating the uterine cells to produce and/or transport
growth factors, and nutrients that optimize the histotroph
for embryonic nutrition and elongation [87]. From the
perspective of the maternal system, a key regulator of
uterine function and histotroph involves the circulating P4
concentrations. Suboptimal patterns of circulating P4 dur-
ing the early luteal phase can lead to altered gene expres-
sion in uterine endometrial cells, suboptimal growth of the
embryo, and reduced pregnancy success [88,89].For
example, the uterine proteome on Days 7 and 15 were
associated with the circulating P4 from Days 3 to 7 [90], and
embryo size on Day 14 was altered by increasing circulating
P4 before Day 7 [91]. Proteomic analysis of uterine histo-
troph identiﬁed 85 unique proteins that also had corre-
sponding mRNA transcripts that were expressed by Day 16
of pregnancy in either endometrial or conceptus tissues
[84], and these proteins could be interesting targets for
improving embryonic survival during this period.
The nutrients present in the histotroph change
dramatically near the time of embryo elongation [92–95].
For example, amino acid concentrations in the histotroph of
nonpregnant cows [36] are altered with most amino acids
being increased in the oviduct and uterus with particularly
dramatic alterations (uterus/plasma, %) in asparagine

(357%), aspartic acid (2059%), and glutamic acid (341%).
However, even more impressive are the dramatic changes
in amino acid concentrations in the histotroph of pregnant
compared with nonpregnant animals near the time of
embryo elongation (summarized in [29]). Three studies
have provided amino acid concentrations near the time of
embryo elongation; two in sheep [95] and one in cattle
[96]. Although there seems to be very little change in amino
acid concentrations between Day 10 and 16 in nonpregnant
sheep, there are dramatic increases from three to 23 fold in
speciﬁc amino acids in the uterine lumen of pregnant sheep
[95]. Of particular interest for dairy cattle, the three amino
acids that are considered rate limiting for milk production
under typical lactating rations, lysine, methionine, and
histidine, are those with the greatest increase in concen-
trations in the uterine lumen during embryo elongation
(>10-fold increase on average from these three studies).
Arginine is another amino acid that has been studied
extensively in relation to reproduction [97–99], and it is
also highly concentrated in the pregnant compared with
the nonpregnant uterine histotroph of ruminants (7.6 fold).
The increase in speciﬁc amino acids in the uterus near the
time of embryo elongation appears to be due to an induc-
tion of speciﬁc amino acid transporters in the uterine
endometrial cells [92–96,100]. The induction of these
amino acid transporters is most likely induced by IFNT,
secreted by the elongating embryo. For example, IFNT
treatment dramatically increased one speciﬁc amino acid
transporter, solute carrier family 15, A3, in both glandular
epithelial (36 fold) and stromal epithelial (177 fold) uterine
cells [96]. The indispensable role of the arginine trans-
porter, solute carrier family 7 (SLC7A1), in ovine, tro-
phectoderm was reported by knockdown using antisense
oligonucleotides [101]. Knockdown of SLC7A1 resulted in
reduced arginine transport and retarded conceptus devel-
opment. Induction of the 10-fold increase in amino acids in
the uterine lumen during embryo elongation highlights the
importance of elevated amino acids and amino acid
transporters for optimal embryo elongation. Elevated pro-
vision of other key nutrients and proteins in the histotroph
during this critical period are also likely to be critical for
conceptus elongation. Finally, it seems clear that IFNT has
a central role in regulation of uterine histotroph and
embryonic elongation.
Maternal recognition of pregnancy, maintenance of the
CL, and initial establishment of pregnancy are the terms
that correspond to the second critical process that hap-
pens during this pivotal period with prevention of normal
luteolysis in pregnant cows. The loss of pregnancy by this
time has generally been measured by the decreases in
circulating P4 during luteolysis, although decreases in
luteal volume and recently the use of Doppler ultraso-
nography to measure luteal blood ﬂow during this critical
period has become an intriguing alternative method for
detecting CL regression and pregnancy loss [102–104].In
the cow, most data are consistent with the decisive
trophoblastic signal being IFNT producing changes in the
endometrial cells and ultimately in the CL that results
in continuation of the elevated circulating P4 concentra-
tions that are required for pregnancy. Most data are
also consistent with the “critical period for maternal
recognition of pregnancy” being at about Day 16 after
estrus in cattle. For example, embryos transferred on Day
16 of the estrous cycle were able to maintain the preg-
nancy, but no pregnancies were maintained when em-
bryos were transferred to recipients on or after Day 17
[105]. Alternatively, removal of the embryo by uterine
ﬂushing before Day 16 allowed a normal estrous cycle
length, whereas, cows that had the embryo ﬂushed from
the uterus on Day 16 or later had an extended luteal phase
[106]. Thus, although luteolysis does not begin until Day
18 or 19 in cattle, signals from the embryo must be present
by Day 16 to change the programming in the uterine
endometrial cells to induce an extended cycle in pregnant
cows. Similar observations have been made in sheep,
reporting that surgical removal of embryos before the
critical period does not extend the length of the cycle,
whereas removal of the embryo after this critical period
extends the lifespan of the CL [107]. Infusion of IFNT into
the uterine lumen or into the systemic circulation during
this critical period will produce a similar extension of the
CL lifespan. Nevertheless, a viable embryo proper is not
the critical signal for maintenance of the CL during this
pivotal period because trophoblastic vesicles [108] or
parthenogenetic embryos [109], even without the embryo
proper, can secrete IFNT and lead to an extension of the CL
lifespan. Thus, timely elongation of the embryonic
trophoblast throughout the uterine horn and exposure of
endometrial cells to IFNT seems to be crucial for contin-
uation of the pregnancy during this pivotal period.
Therefore, IFNT is not just a key regulator of uterine
histotroph and embryo elongation but also is the decisive
signal for maternal recognition of pregnancy; however, the
precise mechanisms for this process are still a matter of
some debate and active research. Three physiological
models have been advocated. The ﬁrst model is that the
presence of an elongating conceptus produces IFNT which
alters expression of speciﬁc proteins within the uterine
endometrial cells, such as E2 and oxytocin receptors, and
this produces an inhibition of prostaglandin F2a (PGF)
pulses from the uterus in response to oxytocin pulses
resulting in lack of luteolytic signals [110–114 ]. The second
model postulates that increased Prostaglandin (PG) E1 and
PGE2 production by the uterine endometrial cells blocks
PGF action at the level of the large luteal cells and, this leads
to continued function of the CL during this pivotal period
[115–117 ] . Finally, a third model promotes the role of sys-
temic IFNT, which escapes from the uterine lumen and acts
directly on the CL [118–120]. This review will not attempt to
resolve this scientiﬁc discussion nor provide all of the data
that are consistent or inconsistent with each physiological
model. Sufﬁce it to say, that there are results that support
each of these models although, in our opinion, support for
one model does not necessarily exclude a physiological role
for the other two models. Of particular importance to this
review, the mechanisms for continuation of the CL must be
in place by around Day 16 to have the embryo by-pass this
pivotal period and avoid pregnancy loss.
By Day 17, the ﬁrst morphologically identiﬁable binu-
cleate giant cells appear, although infrequently, near the
interphase of the undifferentiated trophoblast and endo-
metrial cells with whole regions of proliferating giant cells
M.C. Wiltbank et al. / Theriogenology 86 (2016) 239–253244
visible by Day 22 [121]. From an immunological perspec-
tive, these cells, and the newly generated conceptus, are
intruders within the uterus and speciﬁc mechanisms are
invoked to restrain the normal immunological response.
The normal immune response uses major histocompati-
bility complex (MHC) molecules to present foreign anti-
genic peptides to cytotoxic T lymphocytes that then kill the
foreign cells [122]. The trophoblast protects the embryo
from rejection by downregulating the expression of MHC
class I molecules at critical stages of development [123].It
is also known that expression of MHC is regulated by
interferons [124], and presence of regulatory T cells in the
uterus regulates the maternal immune response to the
pregnancy by signaling other maternal immune cells to
induce a state of immune tolerance to the paternally
inherited proteins of the conceptus/fetus [125].
However, expression of endometrial MHC is limited to
the glandular epithelium and endometrial stroma but
absent in the luminal epithelium adjacent to the tropho-
blast [122]. Thus, an interaction among MHC expression
and the actions of IFNT, P4, maternal T regulatory cells, and
cortisol, which are highly expressed in the embryo and
endometrium during this elongation period [126,127], may
be partially responsible for immunological protection of
the embryo and placenta in pregnant cows. In addition,
proteins secreted by the uterine endometrium, in particular
SerpinA14, have been postulated as immune regulators
that mediate the actions of P4 to protect the embryonic/
fetal allograft [128].
On the other hand, immunological diversity appears to
be positive for reproductive success. Early studies by Mares
et al [129] found that lactating cows and embryos with the
least genetic and immunological differences, on the basis of
inbreeding for dam and embryo, had the lowest fertility
compared with when embryos and dams were outbred
(50.3 vs. 69.3% for heifers; 39.6 vs. 67.1% for lactating cows).
Thus, immunological differences do not have a negative
effect but seem to be positive for fertility, possibly due to
positive interactions among IFNT, MHC expression, and
immunological responses.
Therefore, IFNT action is essential to all three of the
critical processes that occur during the second pivotal
period of pregnancy: elongation, maintenance of the CL,
and immunological protection of embryo. It is possible to
measure the actions of IFNT during early pregnancy
because small amounts of embryonic IFNT escape the
uterus and can be evaluated by measuring interferon-
stimulated genes (ISGs) in peripheral blood mononuclear
cells [130]. Some studies have evaluated the measurement
of peripheral ISGs for pregnancy diagnosis [130–135], but
few have allowed estimation of pregnancy loss during this
pivotal period. In one study [130], cows were bred by AI or
received an embryo and ISG-15 was evaluated on Days 18,
21, and 25. Although all cows that were pregnant had an
elevation in ISG-15 on Days 18–21, cows that were not
pregnant after AI did not have an elevation in ISG-15, even
in cows with an extended estrous cycle interval (9/28 ¼ 32%
of nonpregnant cows). In contrast, in cows that received
embryo there was an elevation in ISG-15 in cows with an
extended interestrous interval (11/28 ¼ 39.3%). Thus, in
that study, approximately 32% of cows that received AI had
pregnancy loss in pivotal period 2, on the basis of extended
interestrous interval, but none had pregnancy loss based on
ISG-15.
To evaluate the pregnancy loss during this pivotal
period (see Table 3), based on ISG-15, we have analyzed
data from one published study from another laboratory
[131], and data from our own laboratories from two
unpublished and one published experiment [136]. Overall,
monitoring of Day 19 ISG-15 expression in blood mono-
nuclear cells has pitfalls, as reported by the false negative
rate of almost 14%. In other words, 14% of the cows did not
have elevated ISG-15 mRNA expression on Day 19, although
those cows were later diagnosed pregnant. This could be
due to the numerous technical issues associated with a
PCR-based assay but may also represent biological variation
in the amount of IFNT activity in the circulation of a speciﬁc
pregnant cow to stimulate leukocyte genes sensitive to
interferon. Overall, there were about 70% of the cows that
were estimated to be pregnant on Day 18/19 after AI. There
were a greater percentage of cows pregnant on Day 19 in
those synchronized with Double-Ovsynch (76.1%; 335/440)
than Ovsynch (51.5%) or Presych-Ovsynch (65.3%). Double-
Ovsynch is designed to provide increased synchronization
of cows, greater circulating P4 concentrations during pre-
ovulatory follicle development, and ovulation of a smaller
and more consistent diameter follicle than other programs
[72,137], and this result is consistent with the better
embryo quality on Day 7 after programs like Double-
Ovsynch. Nevertheless, by the end of this period, on Days
28 or 32, between 19% and 41% of the pregnancies have
been lost, arguing that this period is particularly prob-
lematic and variable for pregnancy loss in lactating dairy
cows.
It is currently unclear, why elongated embryos that are
producing IFNT, as detected by peripheral expression of
ISG-15, would be lost before Day 32. Only 15.8% of the
pregnant cows on Day 19 after AI, based on ISG-15, and that
were not pregnant on Day 32 had reduced circulating P4 on
Day 19, suggesting that most elongated trophoblasts pro-
duced an extended luteal phase. In a study of IVP embryos
transferred to either lactating cows or heifers [138],
embryos were much smaller in cows than heifers on either
Day 13 (0.30 vs. 1.15 mm; P < 0.0001) or Day 14 (3.20 vs.
11.16 mm; P ¼ 0.01) suggesting that the uterine environ-
ment of lactating cows may be suboptimal for embryo
elongation. A previous study indicated that ISGs were
consistently elevated on Day 18 in heifers, but that lactating
cows had a more variable elevation in ISGs [134]. This result
indicates that timely IFNT secretion, speciﬁcally by the
critical Day 16 window, may be a limiting factor in preg-
nancy maintenance during this period, particularly in
lactating dairy cows. In addition, a lack of synchrony
between embryonic gastrulation and trophoblastic elon-
gation has been suggested as a potential developmental
defect that may produce pregnancy loss during this period
[139]. Research is needed to deﬁne the mechanisms, such
as previous programming of the embryo, that produce
suboptimal patterns of IFNT secretion to determine
whether the temporal and spatial dynamics of IFNT secre-
tion in individual animals is critical for optimal mainte-
nance of the CL. Optimization of circulating P4 during the
M.C. Wiltbank et al. / Theriogenology 86 (2016) 239–253 245
ﬁrst week after AI or other treatments that may optimize
embryo elongation may result in a high percentage of cows
pregnant at Day 19, but this may not optimize percentage of
cows that are pregnant at the end of this pivotal period
(approximately Day 27).
4. Pivotal period 3, days 28–60: placentome
development, embryo growth, and unexplained
pregnancy loss
The third pivotal period is during the second month of
gestation, between Days 28 and 60. Early in this period,
embryonic allantoic tissue apposition leads to adherence,
and eventually attachment of the maternal and allantoic
membranes with full development of placentomes and
chorioallantoic nutrition of the embryo by the end of this
period. During this period, embryonic nutrition makes a
pivotal switch from uterine histotroph through a yolk-sac
placenta or choriovitelline nutrition, and ﬁnally to the
chorioallantoic placental nourishment that will continue
throughout the remainder of pregnancy. Uterine blood ﬂow
increases as the conceptus undergoes rapid development
and any deﬁciencies in placental development will lead to
inadequate embryo development and these “unhealthy”
embryos seem to be detected and eliminated.
Successful passage through the third pivotal period in-
volves substantial growth and differentiation of embryonic
and trophoblastic cells, and substantial cross-talk between
the uterine and the embryonic tissues. The embryo with
the surrounding amnion is only w5 grams with no coty-
ledons on Days 20–25of gestation but increases almost 6
fold to w30 g in the next 5 days (Day 30) with the ﬁrst
morphologically distinguishable cotyledons [140]. Just
before Day 30 of gestation, the maternal and fetal mem-
branes begin the interaction that results in development of
placentomes, starting with apposition between trophoblast
and uterine epithelial microvilli on Days 18–19 [141], fol-
lowed by adhesion of the membranes 2 days later, and
subsequently weak attachments linking the maternal and
fetal tissues in the next week. Morphological studies have
shown clear evidence of interdigitation between maternal
and trophoblastic tissues by Day 30 of pregnancy, although
the connections were tenuous and easily disrupted
[142,143]. Cuboidal epithelial cells were present
throughout the process of attachment with increased
number of binucleate giant cells as placentome formation
progressed [143]. In contrast, the yolk sac develops from
Days 18–23 and is largest at wDays 25 of gestation
(approximately 10 cm in length) with decreasing length
throughout the second month of pregnancy until essen-
tially complete disappearance occurs by Day 60 of preg-
nancy [85]. During this period, embryonic nutrition occurs
through the choriovitelline placenta by way of the yolk sac
(Day 25), which is functionally replaced by development of
the chorioallantoic placentomes that allow greater diffu-
sion of nutrients and gases directly between the maternal
and fetal circulatory systems. Thus, the bovine develops a
cotyledonary, epitheliochorial, allantoic placenta between
30 and 60 days of gestation with development by 60 days of
a fetus with ultrasonically distinguishable adult organ
systems [144], a complete complement of 70 to 100

Deﬁciencies in development can occur during this
pivotal period resulting in pregnancy loss. Some of the key
developmental problems are: inadequate placentation
[145,146], difﬁculty in the transition from amniotic to
allantoic nutrition [147,148], alterations in vascularization
of placenta and other systems [149,150], and underdevel-
opment of the embryo/fetus [151]. Some of the most dra-
matic and well-described deﬁciencies in development
during this period occur in IVP embryos [149,151] or clones
using somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) [145–148]. These
types of embryos have increased pregnancy wastage during
this pivotal period. For example, 25% of the pregnancies
from IVP embryos are lost during this period, whereas, this
number can be as high as 80% in clones [152] (our unpub-
lished results). In the case of embryos derived from nuclear
transfer, it is clear that abnormalities of placental devel-
opment seem to underlie the high-pregnancy loss during
this period.
The pregnancy losses that occur during the second
month of gestation, from Day 28–60 in lactating dairy cows
have also been calculated in many studies and found to be
of substantial importance in determining reproductive
efﬁciency of dairy herds [1,3,53,153]. For example, in one of
our early studies, we evaluated approximately 1600
lactating cows during pregnancy using ultrasound [154].Of
468 lactating cows diagnosed pregnant on Day 28 after AI,
49 lost their pregnancies by Day 42 (10.5%) and another 26
were lost by Day 56, resulting in a total loss of 16.0% during
pivotal period 3. Similarly, losses during this period or some
part of this period have been quantiﬁed in numerous
studies. Losses were estimated at 12.5% between Day 31
and 45 days for 1465 pregnancies [153] and 17.5% from Day
27 to 41 for 251 pregnancies [155]. In a summary of 14
different studies and a total of 4870 pregnancies, there was
a calculated 12.8% pregnancy loss during about a 15-day
time period from w28 days to w42 days of pregnancy
or about 0.85% loss/day. We have recently summarized
the pregnancy loss data from 46 recent studies with
132 different treatment groups (Toledo, Monteiro, and
Wiltbank, unpublished). In most studies, the ﬁrst preg-
nancy diagnosis was on Day 32 after AI, but it ranged from
27 to 40 days with a second pregnancy diagnosis at
w60 days, range from 56 to 90 days. There were 24,391
pregnancies at the ﬁrst diagnosis and 11.95% of these
pregnancies were lost before the second pregnancy diag-
nosis (Toledo, Monteiro, and Wiltbank, unpublished). Thus,
w12% pregnancy loss during pivotal period 3 is typical for
lactating dairy cows but with substantial variation between
the farms, ranging from 3.5% to 26.3%. Although some
variation is probably due to random chance, some is likely
to be caused by real differences between farms possibly
due to synchronization protocols, health status, manage-
ment practices, or environmental conditions. These losses
result in substantial economic impact for dairy producers
because of the delays in time to pregnancy [156–158].
There are numerous factors that have been found to
increase risk for pregnancy loss during the second month of
gestation including anovulation at the end of the voluntary
waiting period (2-fold increase; [1]), reduced circulating
concentrations of P4 during growth of the dominant follicle
[33], parity (multiparous 17.2% vs. primiparous 8.7% from
Day 30 to 58 of gestation; [2]), farm [2], change in BCS
(calving to AI; /C211 ¼ 20.5% [27/132]; <1 ¼ 14.5% [174/1197];
no change ¼ 10.7% [108/1008]; [2]), uterine diseases
[159,160], and nonuterine diseases such as mastitis [161].
One unexpected ﬁnding that we observed in a recent study
[162] was that in herds that were tocopherol deﬁcient
during the dry period, treatment with injectable vitamin E
of 1000 IU each week for the last 3 weeks of gestation not
only reduced retained placenta and stillbirth but also
decreased pregnancy loss (20.5% vs. 12.5%; P < 0.01). One
other surprising risk factor for pregnancy loss during AI
programs was lack of expression of estrus during the pre-
ovulatory period [163] with cows that expressed estrus
having 14.4% loss (255/1785), whereas, cows that did not
express estrus had 20.1% pregnancy loss (43/222). Inter-
estingly, this effect of estrus was also observed in lactating
cows that received embryo transfer, indicating that the
factors inhibiting estrus during the preovulatory period,
probably circulating E2 concentrations, leads to pregnancy
loss between Day 32 and 60 of pregnancy, probably due to
lingering effects on the uterine environment [163].
Regardless of risk factor, one important, but unresolved
issue is whether pregnancy loss during this period is due to
death of the embryo and subsequent regression of the CL
or, alternatively, regression of the CL leads to subsequent
pregnancy loss [164]. A study evaluating this topic reported
that regression of the CL by using exogenous PGF treatment
on Day 39.5 lead to a rapid decrease in circulating P4,
cessation of embryonic heartbeat by 36 hours, and expul-
sion of the conceptus from the uterus by 1.9 days; whereas,
death of the embryo by intrauterine infusion of hypertonic
saline lead to immediate cessation of the heartbeat, but no
changes in circulating P4 for almost 1 week, and expulsion
of the dead conceptus only at 7.1 days after the death of the
embryo [164]. Circulating PAGs were similar for the two
groups and required about 1 week to decrease to basal
concentrations. Thus, use of PAGs, P4, and evaluation of the
embryonic heartbeat should allow determination of the
timing of embryonic death and CL regression during nat-
ural pregnancy loss in this period. Some studies have also
indicated that induction of an accessory CL can reduce the
risk of pregnancy loss [165,166], suggesting that inadequate
P4 may be partially responsible for increased risk of preg-
nancy loss in lactating dairy cows. Thus, a wide variety of
factors ranging from disease, to metabolic and nutritional
conditions, and hormonal environment are related to losses
during the third pivotal period. Successful passage through
this second month of pregnancy indicates that the embryo/
fetus has developed the proper placental vascularization,
nutritional status, and healthy maternal environment to be
likely to complete a successful pregnancy. Alternatively,
loss of the pregnancy during this pivotal period may be a
tactic to eliminate a pregnancy that has developed insuf-
ﬁcient placental function, or is deemed insufﬁciently
“healthy”, thereby avoiding misallocation of maternal
resources and further delays in initiation of a new healthier
pregnancy. Thus, placental insufﬁciency or embryonic
M.C. Wiltbank et al. / Theriogenology 86 (2016) 239–253 247
deﬁciencies could be the cause of losses during this period,
with no study clearly distinguishing between these two
alternatives. In conclusion, the occurrence and quantity of
pregnancy loss during this third pivotal period has been
well described to average w12%; however, the mechanisms
that produce pregnancy loss during this period still need to
be deﬁned in future research.
5. Pivotal period 4, days 60–90: embryo and
placentome growth, crowding of twins
The fourth pivotal period continues the growth of the
fetus, increasing volume of the chorioallantois, and
amnion. There is continuous growth of the fetus from
w10 g on Days 60, to w32 g on Days 70, to w166 g on Day
90, almost a 17-fold increase in fetal mass during a 30-day
period [167]. In this time period, there is also an increase in
total membrane weight from w70 g on Day 60 to w240 g
by Day 90 with moderate increases in the chorioallantois
and a more dramatic increase in the amnion [140,167]. The
cotyledonary attachments become more robust with efﬁ-
cient diffusion of nutrients from the maternal to fetal cir-
culation. The number of placentomes has been reported to
increase between Days 60 and 70 of gestation from 40 to 80
[85], but is relatively constant after Day 70 of gestation
[168]. The increasing nutritional demands of the growing
fetus appear to be met not by changes in number of pla-
centomes but by a dramatic increase in placentome length
and mass [168]. Active growth of the placentome is parallel
with the vascular development during this period, which
will not be completed until w190 days of gestation [169]
with placentome size reaching a plateau at about Day 190
[168]. The increase in placentome volume and vasculature
during this ﬁnal month of the ﬁrst trimester of pregnancy
are probably critical to support the increases in blood ﬂow,
nutrient uptake, and fetal growth that will occur during the
second and third trimesters of gestation [167,170].In
addition, the endocrine output of the placenta changes
during this third month of gestation. Concentrations of
circulating estrogens are only 1 to 6 pg/mL before Day 50 of
gestation but increase dramatically thereafter with estrone
sulfate concentrations reaching 30 pg/mL in singleton
pregnancies by Day 90 [171]. Numerous other growth fac-
tors and circulating hormones also begin to be secreted
from the placenta during this stage of pregnancy, with
dramatic effects of maternal physiology and potential
implications for improved embryo survival and health
[172,173].
Estimates of pregnancy loss during this pivotal period
are not commonly reported in the literature, although we
found losses ranging from 1 to 3% (Table 4). Factors asso-
ciated with increased pregnancy loss in this period have
not been studied in detail; however, twinning was identi-
ﬁed in one study as a major contributor to the pregnancy
loss during this period [176]. In this study, 75% of the
pregnancy loss between 45 and 90 days in cows bearing
twins occurred between 68 and 90 days, whereas for cows
bearing singletons 100% of the losses were identiﬁed
between 45 and 61 days. In addition, pregnancy loss during
this period in cows bearing twins was 5.4 times greater in
cows bred during the warm season. In another study [179],
ipsilateral twin pregnancies were 3.45 times more likely to
undergo pregnancy loss, based on odds ratio, than bilateral
twin pregnancies, indicating that crowding, particularly
two fetuses in a single uterine horn may be a contributor to
pregnancy loss. Similarly, in beef cattle selected for twin
ovulations [180] there were also greater pregnancy losses
for twins in the same uterine horn (5.8-fold increased risk
compared with loss of pregnancy in singletons) compared
with twins in opposite horns (2.1-fold increase in risk of
pregnancy loss relative to singletons). Thus, some of the
loss of pregnancies during this period may be attributable
to twin pregnancies in the same uterine horn.
The development of advanced assisted reproductive
technologies such as IVP embryos and SCNT has brought
numerous interesting insights into conceptus development
during this period. Placentas of Day 70 fetuses from IVP
embryos produced using synthetic media had fewer pla-
centomes with lower density of blood vessels, lower
placental efﬁciency (fetal weight/placental weight), and
decreased vascular-endothelia growth factor mRNA
compared with embryos produced in vivo or in vitro using
serum supplementation with cumulus cell coculture [181].
Similar abnormalities have been observed in SCNT preg-
nancies with reduced number of caruncles at 100 days but
increased individual and total weight of caruncles as
compared with AI and IVP pregnancies [182]. In addition,
there is a substantial variation in placental development in
pregnancies derived from SCNT with placental abnormal-
ities and fetal abnormalities, a likely cause of pregnancy
losses during this time [145].
6. Conclusions: reducing overall pregnancy loss by
focus on each pivotal period
As mentioned early in this review, a fundamental goal of
reproduction is to establish and maintain a healthy preg-
nancy; however, in the absence of successful completion of
this goal, it is most reproductively efﬁcient for the animal to
promptly reinitiate a new potentially fertile cycle. Many
factors impact the reproductive process and it is clear that,
in the dairy cow, pregnancy loss is one of, if not the most
important factor that depresses reproductive performance.

misuse on inferior offspring. However, many of the
contributing factors that increase the risk of pregnancy loss
can be managed, and aspects related to the hormonal
environment during the preovulatory and periovulatory
periods, disease and metabolic states, and environmental
conditions can likely be optimized by management prac-
tices. In addition, other issues such as genetic and devel-
opmental anomalies will occur naturally and pregnancies
with these problems seem to be detected in most cases
during the normal pregnancy process and are eliminated in
a timely manner to provide another opportunity for a
better pregnancy.
The objective of this review is to provide veterinarians,
reproductive managers, and scientists with potential issues
to consider in each pivotal period to reduce pregnancy loss
in dairy cattle. In many situations, pregnancies are not
viable by Day 7 after AI either due to lack of fertilization or
gamete issues, such as ovulation of an overstimulated
oocyte. As shown in Table 3, use of improved breeding
programs, such as Double-Ovsynch, can resolve some of the
hormonal issues, such as low-circulating P4 in lactating
dairy cows, and produce 70% or more pregnant cows on
Day 19, based on ISG-15. However, loss after this time may
still occur as shown by the signiﬁcant differences in preg-
nancy loss between Days 19 and 32 (25 vs. 41%; Table 3)or
Days 19 and 62 (30 vs. 45%) in the two studies that both
used Double-Ovsynch. It is unclear what is producing these
differences in pregnancy loss, but the result is one herd that
had more than 50% pregnant per ﬁrst AI vs. another herd
that was only in the 43% range by Day 60 of pregnancy. It
seems likely that optimizing circulating P4 concentrations
probably is an important key to reducing pregnancy loss.
For example, increased circulating P4 before AI can improve
oocyte quality and development during the ﬁrst pivotal
period, decreased P4 near AI could increase fertilization,
and increased P4 after AI may improve elongation during
period 2 and may reduce pregnancy losses during period 3
[33].
After completing this review, we are struck by the dra-
matic changes in physiology of the embryo and dam during
each period of pregnancy loss. Although most authors have
spoken of the problem of pregnancy loss, it seems clear that
the ruminant organism has developed speciﬁc monitors of
the competence of the embryo to complete each speciﬁc
physiological process, such as timely elongation and
adequate placentome development, as gateways to
continued pregnancy. We speculate that it is more repro-
ductively efﬁcient to reinitiate the reproductive process
with potentially a higher quality pregnancy than to
continue a ﬂawed pregnancy. Thus, passage through each
pivotal period assures the competence and health of the
embryo, related to speciﬁc processes, and thereby increases
the likelihood of a healthy offspring at parturition. Future
focus on the hormonal, metabolic, genetic, and manage-
ment factors that underlie pregnancy failure during each
pivotal period will allow us to not only understand the
intriguing biology of each process but should also allow for
the rational development of treatments and methods to
overcome each type of pregnancy failure.
