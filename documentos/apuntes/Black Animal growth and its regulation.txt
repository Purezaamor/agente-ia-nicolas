ANIMAL GROWTH AND ITS REGULATION 
J. L. Black 
CSIRO1, Blacktown, 2148, Australia 
ABSTRACT 
There are large variations both within and among animal species in size, growth rate, body 
composition and metabolism. If the reasons for these differences are understood, the poten­
tial for improving the efficiency of animal production could be markedly enhanced. This 
paper has two sections. First, a computer simulation model that integrates current descrip­
tive information on the rates of protein and energy deposition in domestic animals is used to 
illustrate the consequences of genotype and administration of exogenous growth hormone 
on growth rate, meat quality and whole enterprise profitability for pigs. Second, the deter­
minants of animal growth and its regulation are discussed. Organ size is primarily determined 
by cell number when defined as the amount of DNA per organ, whereas the rate of tissue 
accretion is influenced by the rate of mitogenesis and the relative rates of protein synthesis 
and degradation and of lipogenesis and lipolysis. Although the fundamental biochemical 
reactions and methods of their control are similar for all species, large differences result from 
minor variations in the regulation of these basic processes. The regulation results from an 
intricate interaction between the genome and many mediators of metabolism. The peptide 
growth factors, prostaglandins and cyclic-AMP, play particularly important roles in this 
regulation. Aspects of the control of mitosis, protein turnover and cell differentiation are 
discussed. 
Introduction 
There are large variations both within and 
between animal species in mature body weight, 
size of individual organs, rate of tissue accretion 
and whole body composition. For example, 
within the canine species, mature body weight 
ranges from around 1 kg in the small breeds to 
over 70 kg in the large breeds. Although most 
mammals and birds have a finite body size that 
is determined primarily by the potential num­
ber of cells in each organ (Munro and Gray, 
1969), many aquatic species of animals, in­
cluding some cetaceans, appear to have an un­
limited capacity for growth (Goss, 1976). The 
continued enlargement of these aquatic species 
is known to be associated with a progressive 
increase in cell number and, in contrast to the 
terrestrial mammals, also with an increase in 
functional units such as kidney nephrons, teeth 
and myocardial muscle fibers in the heart; there 
is no epiphyseal closure in the long bones

(Goss, 1974). Furthermore, some animal 
species have the capacity to completely regen­
erate lost appendages, such as is seen following 
the removal of fins from fish or of tails from 
lizards (Goss, 1976). The salamanda will 
regenerate an entire limb following its amputa­
tion, yet it is unable to regenerate a muscle or 
bone that is taken from an otherwise intact 
limb. 
There are many examples of organs that 
grow to different sizes in different animal 
species because of evolutionary adaptations 
within each species to its particular environ­
ment. One example is the tail of the tree kanga­
roo (Dendrolagus bennettianus), which is 
almost twice the length of the rest of the body, 
whereas in another arboreal marsupial of simi­
lar weight, the koala (Phascolarctos cinereus), 
the tail is virtually absent (Strahan, 1983). An 
example of variation in the rate of growth of 
animal species is seen in comparison of man 
and sheep. Both have similar birth weight and 
final body weight; man takes 15 to 20 yr to 
reach mature size, whereas the sheep takes only 
1.5 to 2 yr. The efficiency of conversion of

If the reasons for these differences between 
animals were understood, the potential for im­
proving the efficiency of animal production 
could be markedly enhanced. Prior to the last 
decade, most research into animal growth and 
metabolism was concerned with describing 
changes in the organs and whole bodies of ani­
mals in relation to various animal, dietary and 
environmental phenomena. However, in recent 
years there has been a substantial increase in 
understanding of the determinants of animal 
growth and its regulation, and it is now possible 
to speculate on probable reasons for many of 
the observed differences outlined above. It is 
the role of animal scientists both to adopt 
appropriate ways of utilizing current knowledge 
and to conduct research for improving the effi­
ciency and profitability of livestock production 
now and in the future. The purpose of this 
paper is, therefore, twofold. First, it is to illu­
strate how current knowledge of the descrip­
tion of animal growth and its components can 
be integrated into computer simulation models 
and used to improve the efficiency and profita­
bility of meat production in domestic animals. 
Second, it will discuss the determinants of 
growth and its regulation with the aim of 
focusing on the research needs of the future. 
Description of Whole Animal Growth 
Domestic mammals used in animal produc­
tion such as cattle, sheep, goats and pigs show a 
sigmoidal pattern to live weight increase with 
time when reared under ideal dietary and en­
vironmental conditions. The rate of live weight 
gain increases during approximately the first 
third of growth, remains relatively constant 
during the middle third and decreases to reach a 
plateau in live weight at maturity (Taylor and

Figure 1 illustrates, for some organs in sheep,

represents the extreme, having reached about

Of the other major body components, the order 
of maturation is skeleton, muscle and fat, with 
only fat approaching its maximum weight at a 
faster rate than the whole body during the final 
stages of growth. The species, strain and sex of 
animal affect the pattern of organ development. 
For example, males have a greater postnatal 
development of bones and muscles, particularly 
of the head and neck region, than do females 
or castrates. 
The consequence of these differences in the 
rate of maturity between organs is that the 
pattern of protein and fat deposition varies 
both throughout the growth phase and between 
animals of different strain and sex (Searle et al., 
1972; Searle and Griffiths, 1976a,b). As an

strains (Searle and Griffiths, 1976b). When 
comparisons are made at the same weight, geno­
types that are heavier at maturity generally 
grow faster, contain less fat and more protein 
and bone in their bodies than do animals of 
smaller mature size (Black, 1983b). However, 
differences due to sex do not become apparent 
until around puberty.

amino acid adequacy of the diet and climatic

body composition and interactions between 
various factors have been reviewed for sheep by 
Black (1983b). Although there are some differ­
ences in degree among the domestic species, the 
effects of nutrition and climate on growth and

Prediction of Whole Animal Growth 
and Composition 
Many of the observed interrelationships 
described above can be incorporated into com­
puter programs that simulate animal growth and 
body composition. These programs can then be 
used to assess the likely consequences on 
performance of manipulations to the strain or 
sex of animal and to its diet and climatic en­
vironment. The procedures involved in develop­
ment of such programs were described for 
sheep by Black (1983b) and have been further 
refined in a simulation model developed for 
pigs (Black et al., 1986). The pig program pre­
dicts the potential rates of protein and energy 
deposition for different strains and sex of 
animals in relation to their live weight. It also 
predicts the energy requirements for mainte­
nance in relation to the protein content of the 
body and the rate of protein deposition. Endo­
genous amino acid losses through inevitable

Potential Rate of Energy and 
Protein Deposition 
It is assumed that when animals, growing 
from birth to maturity, are given free access to 
a high-quality diet in a situation in which feed 
intake is not limited by gut capacity, disease or 
the climatic or social environment, the rate of 
energy and protein deposition represents the 
maximum capacity of the animal to lay down 
body tissue. The rate of deposition of both 
energy and protein in pigs increases from birth 
to a maximum when the animal weighs from 60 
to 120 kg, depending on the genotype, and 
then falls to zero when maximum body size is 
reached. Unfortunately, there are few experi­
ments in which pigs have been grown to matu­
rity under ideal conditions. Results from Tullis 
(1982) for a British strain of pig and for Austra­
lian pigs grown to 100 kg (Hodge, 1974; 
Robards and Radcliffe, 1987) were originally 
used to estimate the parameters for predicting 
potential rates of energy and nitrogen retention 
in pigs of two strains using equations developed 
for sheep by Black (1983b). Predictions from 
these equations (Black et al, 1986) for entire 
male, female and barrows for a superior geno­
type and barrows of an inferior genotype are 
shown in Figure 3. Results from a recent ex­
periment (L. R. Giles and J. L. Black, unpu­
blished data) in which pigs at the Wollongbar 
Research Institute, New South Wales, Austra­
lia, were grown from weaning to 150 kg under 
ideal conditions indicate that the form of the 
original equations should be modified slightly 
to allow prediction of sufficiently high rates of 
deposition in light weight pigs and still main­
tain near maximal rates over the observed range 
of body weights in heavier pigs. The equations

Relationship between Energy Intake
and Protein Deposition
Many recent experiments (Campbell et al.,
1985a,b; Dunkin et al., 1986; Dunkin and
Black, 1987; Campbell and Taverner, 1988a)
indicate that protein deposition in the body of
pigs increases linearly with energy intake until
the potential for the particular genotype and
body weight is reached. Comparison of results
from entire male pigs of the one strain ranging
in weight from 6 to 90 kg (Dunkin and Black,
1987 and unpublished data) show that the
slope of the relationship between nitrogen
retention falls with increasing body weight. The
change in slope was greater as weight increased
for lighter than for heavier pigs and there was
little change for pigs weighing more than 50 kg.

Predicted Effects  of  Exogenous Growth 
Hormone Administration 
The daily administration  of  exogenous por­
cine growth hormone  has  resulted in a  substan­
tial increase  in  growth rate,  an  improvement  in 
feed conversion efficiency  and the  production 
of a  leaner carcass  in  pigs from  25 to 100 kg 
(Machlin, 1972;  Boyd  et al., 1986;  Etherton 
et al., 1986, 1987;  Campbell et al.,  1988a,b). 
Although there are normally large differences  in 
these characteristics between entire males, 
females and  barrows,  the  administration  of 
growth hormone eliminates them  and  reduces 
voluntary intake  in all  sexes below that nor­
mally recorded  for  entire males (Campbell  et 
al., 1988b). However,  it  does  not  eliminate dif­
ferences between strains (Campbell  and  Taver­
ner, 1988b).  In the two  experiments reported 
by Campbell  et al.  (1988a,b), 100  fxg/kg of  por­
cine growth hormone  was administered daily  to 
Duroc x  Yorkshire pigs growing from  25 to 55 
kg and  from  60 kg for 31 d. The  lighter pigs 
were fed at  three levels  of  intake including  ad 
libitum, whereas  the  heavier pigs were  fed ad 
libitum; comparisons were made with  un­
treated controls. Because live weight gain  and 
the rates  of fat and  protein deposition were 
measured, these experiments provide sufficient 
information to  allow  an  approximate estima­
tion of the  parameters outlined above  for de­
scribing genotype.  It can be  assumed that  the 
growth hormone-treated pigs behave  as if  they 
were another "genotype," because their poten­
tial to  deposit protein  and  energy have been 
altered. Parameters derived  for the  untreated 
and growth hormone-treated barrows  are in­
cluded in  Table  1. The  predicted and  observed 
performance of the  pigs  are  compared  in 
Table 3 and  show reasonable agreement.  It is 
interesting to  note that because  the  body pro­
tein content  and  rate  of  protein deposition 
were greater  in the  treated pigs, energy require­
ments for  maintenance were predicted  to be 
higher in  these pigs than  in the  controls.  The 
predicted differences  for the  pigs growing from 
25 to 55 kg  ranged from  .7 to 2.4 MJ/d  depend­
ing on  intake  and are  similar to the  difference 
of 1.3 MJ/d  observed  by  Campbell  et al. 
(1988a). Furthermore,  the  observed reduction 
in ad  libitum intake  in the  treated pigs  was 
predicted because  of  their reduced potential 
for energy deposition.

Cellular Factors Associated 
with the Size and Growth Rate of Organs 
The size of an animal is the result of the size 
of its individual organs, which depend on the 
number and size of cells they contain. Growth 
rate is determined by the rate of increase in cell 
contents in each organ and is primarily the 
result of the rate of mitogenesis and the relative 
rates of protein synthesis and degradation and 
of lipogenesis and lipolysis. Although the num­
ber of cells in some tissues such as mammalian 
muscle is fixed near birth, the number of nuclei 
in each muscle fiber cell continues to increase 
into postnatal life through transfer from satel­
lite cells outside the fiber (Allen et al., 1979). 
Cheek et al. (1971) suggested that each nucleus 
in a tissue has the capacity to support a certain 
amount of cell tissue. Because the DNA content 
of a diploid nuclei is virtually constant in ani­
mals (Vendrely, 1955), cell number is often 
expressed as the amount of DNA in an organ 
and cell size as the mass of tissue/unit DNA. 
This avoids problems with polyploid tissues. 
Organ Size 
Although there are substantial differences in 
the size of cells between organs (Colebrook et 
al., 1988), considerable evidence both among 
(Munro and Gray, 1969) and within (Robinson 
and Bradford, 1969; Falconer et al., 1978a; 

12 BLACK 
Bernier et al., 1987; Colebrook et al., 1988) 
specie
s indicates that the difference in the size 
of a specific organ is due mainly to a difference 
in cell number, with only minor variation in 
cell size. For example, Munro and Gray (1969) 
observed that liver cells in the mouse, rat, 
rabbit, dog, bullock and horse were approxi­
mately the same size and that the difference in 
liver mass from 1.7 g in the mouse to 9.0 kg in 
the horse was due entirely to the number of 
cells. There was, however, a small variation be­
tween the species in the size of muscle cells 
when measured as mass tissue/DNA. Muscle 
mass was 23,000 times greater in the horse 
than in the mouse; there were 10,000 more 
cells in the horse muscle and they were 2.3 
times larger. Colebrook et al. (1988) examined 
the cellularity of 16 organs in six breeds of 
mature rams fed a high-quality diet ad libitum 
and found that cell number was the major 
determinant of organ mass for all organs except 
perirenal fat, for which cell number and cell 
size contributed approximately equally. Cell 
size contributed significantly, but to a minor 
extent, to the mass of only three other organs, 
spleen, liver and vastus lateralis muscle. Falco­
ner et al. (1978a) selected large and small 
strains of mice and found also that cell size as 
well as cell number contributed significantly 
to differences in the mass of spleen, liver, lung 
and kidney. Cell size also contributed more to 
the differences in organ mass between males 
and females than it did between the strains. 
Chimeras developed from the two strains of 
mice (Falconer et al., 1978b) showed a positive 
relationship between body weight and the pro­
portion of cells contributed from the large 
strain, further suggesting that cell size accounts 
for some of the difference in organ size be­
tween animal strains. 
Limited evidence indicates that the major 
effect of endocrine manipulation of growth is 
also through hyperplasia. Introduction of addi­
tional copies of the growth hormone gene into 
mice markedly increases growth rate and can 
result in a doubling of body size (Palmiter et 
al., 1982). However, the effect on indivdual 
organs differs (Hammer et al., 1985). The liver 
and spleen were 3.5-fold and 3.0-fold larger, 
respectively, in the treated animals than in the 
controls, with approximately 80% of the differ­
ence being due to hyperplasia. Kidney, heart, 
lung and testes were twice as large due solely 
to an increase in cell number, whereas the size 
and cellularity of brain was unaffected. 
The cessation of cellular hyperplasia in most 
organs is one feature of growth that distin­
guishes land-dwelling mammals and birds from 
many other forms of animal life. In mammals, 
organs attain their full complement of cells at 
different stages of the animal's development. 
For example, hyperplasia of the brain in sheep 
is completed soon after birth (Black, 1983b), 
whereas it occurs in the gastrocnemius muscle 
when the animal weighs about 35 kg (Johns and 
Bergen, 1976) and in adipose tissue at about 
45 kg live weight (Hood and Thornton, 1979). 
Prolonged nutrient restriction during the period 
of hyperplastic growth slows the rate of cell 
division and can result in a reduction in the 
final number of cells in the organ and perma­
nent reduction in ultimate organ size (Winick 
and Noble, 1966; Dobbing and Sands, 1971). 
After the period of hyperplastic growth is com­
pleted, variation in organ size results almost 
entirely from variation in cell size. Prolonged 
under-nutrition (Masters, 1963) or the severe 
infestation with gastrointestinal nematodes 
(Symons and Jones, 1975) dramatically reduces 
the size of many organs in adult sheep but does 
not alter the number of cells in the heart, liver, 
kidneys, spleen, lungs or muscles. Similarly, a 
change in cell size totally accounts for an in­
crease in organ size in adult animals following 
an enhanced work load, such as results in the 
heart from partial restriction of the aorta 
(Morkin and Ashford, 1968). 
Rate of Tissue Accretion 
The rate of tissue accretion depends pri­
marily on the rate of mitogenesis and the net 
rates of protein and lipid deposition. These 
functions, particularly the rate of mitogenesis 
and protein accretion, are closely associated 
(White et al., 1988). Many cells must reach 
about 80% of their mature size before they are 
capable of dividing (Bertalanffy, 1960). 
Baserga (1984) speculates that procession of 
cells though the cell cycle depends on the 
successive synthesis of receptors for different 
growth factors responsible for mitosis. Be­
cause of the similarity in cell size within organs 
across species, differences in the rate of tissue 
accretion must reflect closely differences in the 
rate of mitogenesis. Thus, despite a similar 
mature muscle mass in man and sheep, the 
DNA content of human muscle continues to 
increase in individuals until they are at least 
17 yr of age (Cheek et al., 1975), whereas it

reaches a peak in well-fed sheep by the time 
the
y are 6 mo old (Johns and Bergen, 1976). 
Similarly, faster growing, large breeds of dogs 
(Walton et al., 1988) and strains of mice 
(Falconer et al., 1978a; Bernier et al., 1987) 
have a faster rate of mitogenesis than their 
slower growing, smaller counterparts. The rate 
of mitogenesis is also reduced following feed 
restriction (Bernier et al., 1987; Walton et al., 
1988), inadequate dietary protein (Campbell 
and Dunkin, 1983) and hypophysectomy 
(Everitt, 1976). There is an interesting negative 
association between the rate of mitogenesis 
and longevity in mammals. Smaller dogs can 
live about twice as long as larger dogs, and rats 
deprived of food live longer than those that 
grow quickly (Walton et al., 1988). However, 
the opposite appears to exist with aquatic 
species, which maintain a high mitogenic rate 
throughout their existence and are thought to 
have no inherent limit to longevity (Goss, 
1974). 
Net rate of protein deposition within an 
organ depends on the differences between rates 
of synthesis and degradation of the proteins it 
contains. There are large differences in the turn­
over rates of proteins within each tissue, with 
estimates for the half-life of liver proteins 
ranging from .2 to 150 h (Dice and Goldberg, 
1975). Many dietary, animal and environmental 
factors are known to affect the rate of protein 
deposition, but this does not result from any 
consistent change in the rates of protein synthe­
sis and degradation. The only consistency is 
that protein deposition is increased whenever 
the difference between the rate of synthesis and 
degradation is enhanced. For example, refeed-
ing animals after protein deprivation stimulates 
both synthesis and degradation, but the effect 
on synthesis is greater (Millward et al., 1975; 
Reeds et al., 1981). In contrast, an increase in 
protein deposition following an improvement in 
the amino acid balance of dietary protein does 
not change synthesis rate but depresses degrada­
tion rate (Fuller et al., 1987). Black bears 
(Ursus americanus) fasting during winter sleep, 
in contrast to summer starvation, totally pre­
serve body protein by increasing the rate of 
protein turnover threefold to fivefold and pre­
sumably equalize the rates of protein synthesis 
and degradation (Nelson, 1978). Stretch and 
work hypertrophy in muscles as well as ad­
ministration of triiodothyronine increase pro­
tein deposition by increasing synthesis rate 
more than degradation rate (Reeds and Palmer, 
1986). However, growth hormone administra­
tion appears to stimulate synthesis with little 
change in degradation rate (Eisemann et al., 
1986). The synthetic anabolic steroid tren-
bolone acetate stimulates protein deposition by 
reducing both synthesis and degradation rates, 
but having a greater effect on degradation 
(Vernon and Buttery, 1976). Examination of 
protein turnover rates in genetically fast and 
slow growing mice indicate that there is no 
more than a 10% difference in either synthesis 
or degradation rate between the strains despite 
a 45% difference in growth rate (Bernier and 
Calvert, 1987). It is suggested that a difference 
in the energetic efficiency of fat deposition 
may be more important than a difference in 
protein turnover for determining differences 
in growth rate of these mice (Bernier et al., 
1987). 
Prediction of Cellularity 
and Protein Turnover 
Baldwin and Black (1979) developed a 
computer program to simulate the changes 
described above in tissue cellularity and turn­
over rates for mammals ranging in size from 
mice to humans. Although the program could 
predict satisfactorily differences in growth 
between species and the effects on organ 
growth and cellularity of changes in nutrient 
supply, it required a better understanding of 
the biochemical regulation of the processes of 
mitogenesis, protein turnover and metabolism 
to have wide application for assessing the deter­
minants of growth.

Stability of the Biochemical System 
Basic biochemical pathways of metabolism 
have remained essentially unchanged during 
evolution from the prokaryote cell. It has been 
proposed by Sager (1965) that life began with 
the stabilization of three interacting systems: 
nucleic acids for replication, either photo­
synthesis or chemosynthesis for trapping 
energy, and a protein-enzyme system to cata­
lyze the two processes, to mediate responses 
and to form structures. After this scheme 
stabilized, any mutation that strongly modified 
it would be lethal; hence the extreme conser­
vatism in evolution of biochemical reactions. 
Variation in metabolism between animals has 

14 BLACK 
resulted occasionally from additions to the sys­
te
m through gene replication, but most of the 
variation comes from modifications to the 
existing system. For example, Hochachka 
(1976) reviewed the biochemical features in 
muscle tissue ranging from obligate-anaerobe 
to obligate-aerobe in mammals from many  dif­
ferent environments. He showed that the basic 
biochemical features were identical in all tissues 
but that each was adapted to its particular en­
vironment by differences in the concentrations 
of enzymes and the regulation of reaction rates. 
Regulation of Biochemical Reactions 
The principles underlying the control of the 
rate of each biochemical reaction (V) are 
embodied in the Michaelis-Menten kinetic 
equation 
V = VmBX(l+Km/[S]) [4] 
where V,^ is the maximum rate of the reac­
tion and Km represents the binding affinity 
between substrate and enzyme catalyzing the 
reaction, and is defined as the concentration 
of the substrate ([S]) when the reaction is 
half maximum. VmJ of a reaction depends pri­
marily on the amount of enzyme required to 
catalyze the reaction that is availabe at the 
reaction site. This is influenced by many fac­
tors, including the rate of transcription of the 
gene that leads to the formation of the mRNA 
required for the synthesis of the particular 
enzyme and the rate of degradation of that 
mRNA. The rate of enzyme synthesis is influ­
enced by the availability of several other RNA 
species, particular amino acids, GTP, ATP and 
initiation factor proteins, whereas its degrada­
tion is determined by its susceptibility to inter­
cellular proteases, which is a feature of its 
amino acid sequence, the availability of these 
proteases and of their specific inhibitors. The 
binding affinity between the enzyme and sub-
state is influenced primarily by the tertiary 
structure of the enzyme and its electronic prop­
erties, which together determine the availability 
of suitable binding sites. This is influenced by 
the amino acid sequence of the enzyme and the 
presence of other substances that bind to either 
the enzyme or substrate to alter their con­
figuration and, hence, affinity. The concentra­
tion of substrates available for metabolism 
depends on the relative rate of supply and re­
moval at the reaction site. The rate of sub­
strate supply depends partially on the rate of 
transport across cell membranes, on its concen­
tration in blood and on the rate of blood flow. 
Membrane transport is influenced by several 
endocrine factors, whereas substrate concentra­
tion in blood is determined by its absorption 
from the gut and its uptake and release by all 
body tissues. The importance of blood flow for 
regulating the rate of metabolic reactions has 
been emphasized by Coulson and Herbert 
(1981). They argue that the small differences 
between a shrew and a whale in enzyme con­
centrations, substrate concentrations or Km 
of biochemical reactions are quite insufficient 
to explain the  1,000-fold  difference in the rate 
of metabolic reactions, and that the major 
determinant is the rate of blood flow and hence 
delivery of substrates for metabolism. An in­
crease in mammary blood flow is also impli­
cated in the milk production response to 
thyroxine and growth hormone (Davis et al., 
1988). 
When competition between alternative bio­
chemical pathways within a tissue is considered, 
the final outcome depends on the relative 
velocities of the competing reactions; that is, 
the relative V,^, affinities and substrate con­
centrations for each reaction. For example, 
acetyl Co A can be catabolized via the tri­
carboxylic acid cycle, used for the synthesis of 
fatty acids or converted to acetate. All reac­
tions are likely to proceed, but the flux of 
acetyl CoA through each pathway depends on 
the relative kinetics of the three reactions and 
will vary with concentration of acetyl CoA 
(Black etal., 1987b). 
Mediators of Metabolism and Growth 
There are many substances that influence 
the rate of metabolic reactions in animals. They 
include hormones, peptides, growth factors, 
prostaglandins, catecholamines, metabolites, 
free ions, toxins and other substances. These 
mediators regulate metabolism, and thereby 
growth, primarily by altering the kinetics of 
biochemical reactions through an effect on 
Vmax, Km and substrate availability. They bind 
to "receptors" that are typically proteins and 
elicit a conformational change that results in 
the metabolic response. There is close speci­
ficity between a transmitter and its receptor, 
and this allows considerable control over the 
regulation of metabolism. However, specificity 
is not absolute, and several transmitters with

similar morphological structure may bind the 
one receptor. Receptors occur on cell mem­
branes, within the cytoplasm and in the 
nucleus. Some mediators elicit their response as 
a direct result of interaction with the receptor, 
whereas with others, it is the mediator-receptor 
complex that binds with a further receptor to 
elicit the response. Many mediators regulate 
the production and action of other mediators, 
and it is common for them to regulate the num­
ber of their own receptors. Examples of how 
mediators influence V^x by altering gene 
transcription, Km by altering binding affinities 
and substrate availability by altering blood flow 
are given by Black (1984) and Di Liegro et al. 
(1987). 
Many endocrine-related manipulators of 
growth have been identified (Buttery et al., 
1986). They fall broadly into three groups with 
overlapping actions: those associated with the 
growth hormone mode of action, those of 
steroid origin and the beta-adrenergic agonists. 
Administration of growth hormone (Hart and 
Johnsson, 1986), growth hormone-releasing 
factor (Baile et al., 1986) or immunization 
against somatostatin (Spencer, 1986) have all 
been reported to stimulate animal growth. 
Their effects on protein synthesis are mediated 
mainly through an increase in the production of 
insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1), whereas 
their effects on lipolysis are probably a direct 
result of growth hormone action (Boyd and 
Bauman, 1988). Pygmies (Merimee et al., 
1987), miniature poodles and sheep selected for 
slow growth (P. C. Wynn and P. Speck, unpub­
lished data) all have lower serum levels of IGF-1 
than faster growing counterparts. However, the 
same positive relationship between IGF-1 levels 
and growth has not been observed in different 
strains of chickens (Goddard et al., 1988). Al­
though the administration of testosterone, 
estrogens and their analogues stimulate mam­
malian growth (Roche and Quirk, 1986), 
immunization against luteinizing hormone-
releasing hormone (LHRH), which reduces 
serum levels of testosterone and sexual beha­
vior, does not depress growth rate of bulls 
(Robertson et al., 1982). The beta-adrenergic 
agonists, cimaterol and clenbuterol, have been 
shown to increase weight gain, increase the 
proportion of muscle and decrease the propor­
tion of fat in rats and sheep (Baker et al., 
1984; Sharpe et al., 1986), but their action 
depends on the presence of glucocorticoids 
(Sharpe et al., 1986). 
Prediction of the Effects 
of Nutrient Supply and Metabolic 
Mediators on Growth 
The principles outlined above on the regu­
lation of biochemical reactions have been used 
in the development of several computer pro­
grams that simulate nutrient utilization in ani­
mals (Smith et al., 1975; Gill et al., 1984; 
Baldwin et al., 1987; Sainz and  Wolff,  1987). 
These models provide the means for testing the 
validity of current concepts and assist in the 
evaluation of factors that may influence the 
pathways of nutrient utilization. The model 
described by Gill et al. (1984) was developed to 
assist the understanding of factors that influ­
ence the efficiency of utilization of absorbed 
nutrients in young sheep. It simulates the utili­
zation of acetic acid, amino acids, butyric acid, 
glucose, lipid and propionic acid, and each sub­
strate is partitioned between synthetic, oxidative 
and intermediate reactions. The flow of material 
through the model is calculated on the basis of 
accepted stoichiometrics of biochemical path­
ways, and reaction rates are determined using 
standard enzyme kinetics. The simulation 
model was used to investigate the effects of 
varying the proportions of absorbed nutrients 
on the efficiency of energy utilization and the 
rate of protein and fat deposition (Black et al., 
1987b,c). 
A similar model developed by Sainz and 
Wolff (1987) that included the endocrine regu­
lation of protein and lipid synthesis through 
variations in Vmj and km was used to evaluate 
alternative hypotheses regarding the mode of 
action of j3-adrenergic agonists in altering 
growth and body composition in lambs. 
Changes in parameters of adipose tissue or 
muscle protein metabolism alone were insuffi­
cient to account for the observed effects of 
cimaterol on growth. Simultaneous changes of 
only 13% in both muscle and adipose meta­
bolism were required to produce the observed 
doubling of carcass protein and depression 
in carcass fat. The magnitude of the effects 
on metabolism required to produce the ob­
served changes was within the experimental 
variance of the measurements and indicates the 
extreme difficulty in identifying the reasons 
for differences in growth in many situations. 
Computer simulation models could play an 
important role in identifying the significance 
of possible alternative means of metabolic 
control of growth.

Role of Peptide Growth Factors

r the past decade, it has become clear 
that many aspects of animal growth and differ­
entiation are controlled by interactions among 
peptide growth factors and between them and 
other mediators including free ions, cyclic 
nucleotides, prostaglandins and the oncogenes 
(Rozengurt, 1986; Mercola and Stiles, 1988). 
There is evidence that these peptides were pres­
ent during the early period of animal evolu­
tion. Similar molecules are present in mammals, 
as in Caenorhadhitis elegans, Drosophilia and 
Xenopus (Mercola and Stiles, 1988). The high 
degree of conservation is seen in insulin, which 
has a 62% homology between the human and 
the hagfish (agnathan), even though these 
species have had divergent evolutionary paths 
for about 500 million years (Barrington, 1986). 
The amino acid sequence of many growth fac­

several related families shown in Table 6

strong evidence that many cells are able to pro­

which may regulate their own receptors (Sporn 
and Roberts, 1985). It is also thought that 
many of the oncogenes code for the growth fac­

factor action is to bind to a cell surface recep­

kinases. This binding produces soluble intra­

phosphoproteins, inositol phosphates, cyclic 
nucleotides and ions which, within minutes, 
result in a cascade of events that change gene 
expression (Rozengurt, 1986). 
Regulation of Mitosis 
Stages in the regulation of cell proliferation 
have recently been reviewed by Pardee et al. 
(1986). There appear to be two stages con­
cerned with bringing a quiescent cell into full

mitosis. The first takes about 6 h and involves 
a
n activation that brings the cell to the stage 
similar to that existing in cells that have just 
completed mitosis. This activation is dependent 
on the binding of platelet-derived growth factor 
(PDGF) to the cell surface and is followed by a 
cascade of events involving the transmodulation 
of EGF, changes in free ion concentrations, the 
formation of stable E-type prostaglandins and 
the production of cyclic AMP (Rozengurt, 
1986). These events lead to a marked increase 
in the mRNA concent of the cell (Stiles, 1983). 
The second phase, which starts about 6 h 
before the onset of DNA synthesis, also in­
volves rapid gene transcription and protein 
synthesis. This stage is dependent on the pres­
ence of IGF-1 and it may be associated with the 
synthesis of the reptilase complex that contains 
many of the enzymes required for DNA synthe­
sis (Pardee et al., 1986). 
There is considerable evidence showing that 
transforming growth factor-beta (TGFj3) is a 
potent inhibitor of mitosis in many cell types 
(Sporn et al., 1986). Nevertheless, depending 
on the cell type and other growth factors pres­
ent, there are some situations in which TGFj3 
stimulates mitogenesis. The concentration of 
TGFJ3 is extremely high in osteoblasts and is 
associated with collagen and bone formation 
and may be concerned with epyphaseal closure 
in mammals (Sandberg et al., 1988). The serum 
from fasted animals is known also to inhibit the 
growth of cultured cells (Sporn et al., 1986; 
White et al., 1988). It contains low levels of 
IGF-1, but it also appears to contain a mitotic 
inhibition factor (White et al., 1988), which 
may be related to its high levels of TGF0. It 
would be interesting to know whether TGF0 
has a role in the cessation of hyperplasia in 
mammals. 
Regulation of Protein Synthesis 
and Degradation 
There is now clear evidence that the control 
of protein synthesis and degradation within 
muscle cells is mediated through second mes­
sengers that are derived from the metabolism 
of arachidonic acid (Reeds and Palmer, 1986). 
A series of elegant studies with isolated exten­
sor muscles from the forelimb of immature 
rabbits involving stretch hypertrophy and 
various metabolic inhibitors demonstrated the 
close association between the formation from 
arachidonic acid of the prostaglandins PGF2O1; 
and PGE2  and, respectively, protein synthesis 
and protein degradation. Muscle stretch and 
elevated calcium concentrations activate the 
membrane phospholipase A2, and the increased 
release of arachidonic acid leads to the synthe­
sis of the prostaglandins. Subsequent studies 
with other mediators of protein turnover such 
as dexamethasone, which results in a long-term 
depression in protein deposition, insulin, which 
stimulates protein deposition, and inter-
leukin-1, which causes elevated protein degrada­
tion in fever, have all shown close associations 
between prostaglandin formation and the rates 
of protein synthesis and degradation. 
Differences in protein deposition result from 
changes in the relative rates of synthesis and 
degradation. Because the release of both pros­
taglandins occur together, Reeds and Palmer 
(1986) suggest that the relative rates of synthe­
sis and degradation change with the rate of 
arachidonate metabolism. They speculate that 
at low rates of metabolism degradation pre­
dominates, at intermediate rates synthesis pre­
dominates and at high rates degradation again 
predominates because the maximum synthetic 
rate has been achieved. However, there are 
some observations not fully explained by this 
hypothesis. It is likely that part of the associa­
tion between mitosis and protein turnover is 
mediated through the prostaglandins (Reeds 
and Palmer, 1986; Rozengurt, 1986). 
Regulation of Differentiation 
The final size of an organ or tissue depends 
on the number of germ cells committed to a 
particular cell type and on the increase in num­
ber of those cells before the end of the hyper­
plastic growth phase. There is now strong evi­
dence that the commitment of germ cells to a 
particular type is determined by the concen­
tration of certain inducing signals. Experiments 
with the nematode C. elegans demonstrate that 
the fate of precursor cells in the developing 
gonads and vulva is dependent on the presence 
of specific inducing cells and on the distance 
from these cells (Emmons, 1987). In one 
experiment, a single vulval precursor cell was 
shown to follow one of three pathways depend­
ing on its distance from the "anchor" or in­
ducing cell. Similarly, in slime molds a small 
group of cells known as the "tip" determine the 
direction of morphogenic movement of two cell 
types, the pattern of arrangement of these cells 
and the final morphology of the cell group

(Schaap, 1986). Some of the morphogenic 
factor
s responsible for this control have been 
identified. In the slime molds, an oscillating 
concentration of cyclic-AMP appears to be the 
main regulating factor, but a feedback with 
adenosine finally determines the size of the 
organism and the distribution of cell types. 
Ammonia and an unidentified stalk-inducing 
factor are also thought to be involved with 
certain phases of the differentiation of slime 
molds (Schaap, 1986). In addition, several of 
the peptide growth factors including TGF(3, 
EGF, fibroblast growth factor and retinoic acid 
have been implicated in the control of mor­
phogenesis and differentiation in several animal 
species (Eichele and Thaller, 1987; Heine et al., 
1987; Mercola and Stiles, 1988; Woodland and 
Jones, 1988). It is thought that these factors 
may operate in two ways. They are involved in 
determining the fate of a particular cell at a 
critical period of differentiation (Mercola and 
Stiles, 1988) and they also may influence final 
organ size by inhibiting of mitosis, as has been 
suggested for TGF/3 (Sporn et al., 1986). 
Mathematical Models of Differentiation. 
Because the translation of genotype into pheno-
type seems to involve spatial patterning, 
Turing (1952) suggested the concept of a wave­
like pattern developing in the concentration 
of morphogenic substances that diffuse through 
tissue, react with one another to alter the 
pattern and affect differentiation. This reac­
tion-diffusion system can be represented 
mathematically and has been applied to the 
differentiation of several biological systems, in­
cluding the pattern of wool follicle initiation 
(Nagorcka, 1986), the committment of wool 
follicle bulb cells to the fiber and cortical cell 
types in mature wool follicles (Nagorcka and 
Mooney, 1982) and coat color patterns in mam­
mals (Murray, 1988). These models presume 
that there is a certain threshold concentration 
of the morphogen that is required to commit a 
precursor cell to alternative cell types. An 
essential component of the reaction-diffusion 
system is the dependence of the pattern on the 
size and shape of the tissue containing the mor-
phogens. Thus, the commitment of cells to 
one type may alter the domain in which diffu­
sion will take place and establish an entirely 
new pattern. In this way, a progressing pattern 
of differentiation can take place within one 
tissue. A good example of this is seen in the 
predictions of the waves of wool follicle initia­
tion in sheep (Nagorcka, 1986). 
Conclusions 
There is a large variation between animals in 
size, growth rates, body composition and 
metabolism, yet the fundamental biochemical 
reactions and methods of their control are simi­
lar for all species. The differences result from 
minor variations in the regulation of these pro­
cesses. The regulation is controlled by the 
intricate interaction of many mediators of 
metabolism, particularly the peptide growth 
factors. Knowledge of these mediators and their 
actions has expanded enormously during the 
last half decade, and it is clear that the control 
of many of the interactions is indeed subtle. 
Further research concentrating on the mecha­
nism of these reactions, using in particular the 
modern biochemical techniques of monoclonal 
antibodies, DNA probes and metabolic inhibi­
tors, should rapidly enhance knowledge in the 
next few years. However, because of the intri­
cacy of these interactions, further progress can 
probably be made by integrating the knowledge 
and concepts into mathematical simulation 
models. This process will allow the rigorous 
evaluation of concepts and pre-experimental 
assessment of hypotheses. It also has been 
shown that the sensitivity of minor changes in 
an intricate system is often better demonstrated 
using simulation models than experiments with 
their inherent biological variation. The next few 
years should be both exciting and rewarding in 
terms of understanding the regulation of animal 
growth and its manipulation.
