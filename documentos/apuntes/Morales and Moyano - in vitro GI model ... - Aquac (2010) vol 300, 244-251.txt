Application of an in vitro gastrointestinal model to evaluate nitrogen and phosphorus
bioaccessibility and bioavailability in ﬁsh feed ingredients

In the present work a gastrointestinal model (GIM) designed to reproduce closely the conditions existing in
the gut of live ﬁsh was used to evaluate differences in bioaccessibility and bioavailability of nitrogen and
phosphorus present in two different feedstuffs (ﬁsh meal and soybean meal) as a result of the action of
rainbow trout digestive enzymes. In contrast to previous similar works, the operating conditions are
established considering the enzyme:substrate ratio estimated from data obtained from live ﬁsh, as well as pH
changes taking place during digestion. In addition, the GIM allows evaluation of other important parameters,
like the solubilisation of proteins and phosphorus, or the relative contribution of the enzymes to total
bioavailability of those nutrients. The results obtained showed the key role of the gastric digestion on further
intestinal bioavailability of N and P, and clearly established the higher biological value of the ﬁsh meal
compared to the soybean meal tested. Considering its versatility and the great amount of information which
it can provide, the use of this GIM is suggested as a complementary approach that may help to provide a
better understanding of results obtained in vivo.

1. Introduction
From a long time ago, research in animal or human nutrition has
beneﬁted from the use of in vitro models to mimic to a variable extent
digestive processes. Once their correlation to the in vivo response is
demonstrated, those assays have a number of advantages since they are
rapid, safe, and do not have the ethical restrictions affecting assays carried
out with live animals. Thus, in vitro assays are now routinely used to assess
digestibility of ingredients used in feeds for terrestrial animals (Boissen
and Eggum, 1991; Fuller, 1991). Various techniques have been utilized in
digestibility studies of aquatic organisms involving in vitro methods. For
example, the pH-stat method has been applied to evaluate digestibility of
materials based on the estimation of the percentage of hydrolysis of
peptide bonds by protease enzymes during incubation (Dong et al., 1993;
Dimes and Haard, 1994; Ezquerra et al., 1997). Changes in the pH value of
an enzyme and ingredient incubation (pH-drop method) have also been
used in a number of in vitro studies (Lazo et al., 1998; Eid and Matty, 1989).
Incubation of an ingredient with enzymes to determine the amount of
left-over undigested nutrient in order to estimate the relative amount of
digested nutrient as compared with the level in ingredient have been also
extensively used (Grabner and Hofer, 1985; Carter et al., 1999;
Rungruangsak-Torrissen et al., 2002; Tonheim et al., 2007).
As pointed by Carter et al. (1999), the major issues to consider in the
validation in such methods are related to the sensitivity of the assays,
whether they have the potential to separate different feed ingredients and
how accurately they predict in vivo digestibility values. In this sense it is
noteworthy that most of the above mentioned works were carried out
using systems that do not reproduce the main functional characteristics of
ﬁshdigestivetracts.Forexample,someassaysarebasedintheuseof
mammal or bacterial enzymes, although different authors have demon-
strated the suitability of using enzyme extracts obtained from the selected
ﬁsh species (Rungruangsak-Torrissen et al., 2002; Chong et al., 2002).
With very few exceptions (Bassompierre et al., 1998) the assays are based
in the use of closed reactors on which the accumulation of the ﬁnal
products of hydrolysis in the reaction mixture limits the reaction and
makes difﬁcult their evaluation. On the other hand, most studies only
reproduce the intestinal digestion, thus omitting the gastric phase, which
represents a relevant step of food digestion, particularly in carnivorous
ﬁsh (Buddington et al., 1997). Finally, the enzyme:substrate (E:S) ratios
used in the assays are rarely indicated (Eid and Matty, 1989; Tonheim
et al., 2007) and when done, they are not justiﬁed by in vivo estimations.
There are computer-controlled systems that allow closer simula-
tion of in vivo dynamic physiological processes occurring within the
stomach and small intestine of humans. For example the gastric-small
intestine system TIM-1 (TNO, Zeist, The Netherlands) reproduces with
high reliability the main parameters of digestion, such as pH,
temperature, gastric, biliary and pancreatic secretion, peristaltic
mixing and passive absorption of water and small molecules. This
system has been extensively used in pharmaceutical studies of drugs
sensitive to digestive conditions (Blanquet et al., 2004). Even though
this system might be closest to an in vivo situation, and could be used
as a reference to design a similar model for studies in ﬁsh nutrition, it

has the disadvantages of the high cost and the difﬁculty of carrying

Recently, Hamdan et al. (2009) optimized a ﬁsh gastrointestinal
model (GIM) which develops a two-step hydrolysis using sequentially
a closed reactor for acid digestion and a semi-permeable membrane
reactor for alkaline digestion. In this system, the continuous removal
of digestion products during hydrolysis of substrates represents a
more practical and realistic model of the in vivo mechanism, where
the epithelial brush-border membranes remove the products of
digestion from the lumen by independent mechanisms (Moyano
and Savoie, 2001). The efﬁciency of digestion can be evaluated by
measuring either bioaccessibility (fraction which can be potentially
hydrolyzed from the food matrix by the combined action of digestive
enzymes and pH) and the bioavailability (fraction which could be
really available for intestinal absorption and further physiological
utilization and storage) (Parada and Aguilera, 2007). The GIM
operates using ﬁsh enzyme extracts and can be used to estimate the
bioaccessibility of some major nutrients, like protein or carbohy-
drates, present in the feeds, as well as the resulting bioavailability of
amino acids, oligosaccharides or even mineral elements. In this sense,
assays performed with such GIM may be highly interesting not only
from a nutritional perspective, but also considering that the
environmental impact of aquaculture is mainly due to the release of
N and P to the water, both in the form of soluble excretions, faecal
wastes and not consumed feed (Talbot and Hole, 1994). The
formulation of diets with highly digestible nutrients and low pollution
capacity has been a priority study area in aquaculture in the last
decades (Lall, 1991; Sugiura et al., 2000; Cho and Bureau, 2001). In
this sense, the development of methodologies allowing an easy
preliminary and comparative evaluation of the effect of the different
factors affecting the digestibility of both N and P present in raw
materials has become a major need.
The aim of the present study was to evaluate the suitability of that
GIM to evaluate the bioaccessibility and bioavailability of N and P
present in ﬁsh feed ingredients. This latter was considered particularly
interesting since to date very few studies have used an in vitro
methodology to assess the digestive utilization of P in ﬁsh (Weerasinghe
et al., 2001). Two ingredients widely used in feed ﬁsh formulations,
ﬁsh meal (FM) and soybean meal (SM) were evaluated, with the
GIM adapted to simulate the digestive tract of rainbow trout
(Oncorhynchus mykiss). Prior to the assays, a preliminary experiment
with live ﬁsh was carried out to estimate the E:S ratio to be used in
protein hydrolysis. The experiments were designed to evaluate the
relative importance of the gastric phase on the whole digestion
process, the relative contribution of free amino acids present in the
protein sources to the whole release of products, as well as the effect of
pH changes, this latter being considered the most important factor
affecting bioavailability of phosphorus.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Experiment 1: Estimation of the enzyme:substrate (E:S) ratio
existing for proteases in the gut of rainbow trout
The operation of the GIM required to use of an E:S ratio close to
that existing in the gut of live ﬁsh. This was estimated considering, in
one hand, the average total protease production measured in the
stomach and intestine of a number of ﬁsh and in the other hand,
the mean protein intake of ﬁsh in a single meal (calculated from the
amount of feed ingested by day and the proximate composition of
the feed) as follows:

Eight-month-old rainbow trout Oncorhynchus mykiss (n=25; 162 ±
34 g body weight) provided by a local farmer ﬁsh were sampled from a
rearing raceway and maintained in a 500-l ﬁbreglass tank during 1 week
at an average temperature of 16 °C being fed (3% body weight day-1)
with a commercial diet (44% crude protein). Prior to sampling, ﬁsh were
starved for 6 h, and then sacriﬁced by immersion in ice-cold water
containing some drops of clove oil. After dissection of the organs
(stomach or proximal intestine including pyloric caeca), crude extracts
were prepared by manual homogenization in distilled water (1:10, w/v)
followed by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 3 °C, 15 min). Supernatants
were stored at -20 °C until used in the assays. Acid protease activity in
stomach extracts was measured using the method of Anson (1938)
using substrate haemoglobin (5 g l-1) in 100 mmol l-1 glycine-HCl buffer
(pH 2.0). Total alkaline protease activity in the extracts from intestine
was evaluated using the method of Kunitz as modiﬁed by Walter (1984)
using substrate casein (5 g l-1)in50mmoll-1 Tris-HCl buffer (pH 9.0).
Trypsin activity was measured using as substrate Nα-benzoyl-DL-
arginine-p-nitroanilide (BAPNA) by the method of Erlanger et al. (1961)
while chymotrypsin activity was measured using as substrate N-
succinyl-Ala-Ala-Pro-Phe-p-nitroanilide (SAPNA) using the method
described by Ásgeirsson and Bjarnasson (1991).
2.2. Experiment 2: In vitro assays
The in vitro assays involved a two-step hydrolysis designed to
simulate stomach and intestinal digestion in the GIM. The acid phase
was simulated in a closed chamber; after inclusion of substrate,
between 0.9 and 1.1 ml 500 mmol l-1 HCl were added to achieve the
desired pH (2.0), this being followed by the addition of crude
enzymatic extract from ﬁsh stomach. During this phase (60 min),
reaction products were not removed. After this time, the reaction
mixture was transferred to a semi-permeable membrane reactor
adapted from that described by Savoie and Gauthier (1986). The
device was formed by an inner reaction chamber separated from an
outer chamber by a dialysis membrane of 1,000 Da MWCO (Spec-
traPor 6, Spectrum Medical Industries, Los Angeles, CA, USA). Crude
extract from pyloric caeca providing alkaline proteases was added to
the inner chamber and the pH was gradually raised to 9.0 by pumping
100 mmol l-1 borate buffer (Fig. 3). The alkaline hydrolysis was
maintained for 180 min. Solubilization of protein, as well as changes
in pH during the assays were continuously monitored. The small
molecules released during this digestion phase passed across the
membrane and were continuously removed from the outer chamber
by the continuous ﬂow of buffer controlled by a peristaltic pump
(0.5 ml min-1). Both reaction chambers were placed in a thermal
water bath which maintained reaction temperature at 37 °C. Although
this is not a physiological temperature for salmonids, and considering
that maximum activity of digestive ﬁsh proteases is placed within the
range 50–60 C, it was selected in order to increase the activity of the
enzymes, this resulting into a shorter time required for each analysis
and also into a better discriminating power of the assay, as stated in
our previous work (Hamdan et al., 2009). The inner reaction chamber
has a valve, which allowed taking samples from the reaction mixture
as well as monitoring the pH of reaction. Different sampling points
were established during each digestion phase to measure the release
of both N and P hydrolysis products both in the dialysate and in the
reaction mixture inside the reaction chamber.
Once the suitable E:S ratio to be used in the assays was established,
different assays were designed to determine the bioaccessibility and
bioavailability of N and P in two common ﬁsh feed ingredients; ﬁsh
meal (750 g kg-1 crude protein; 22,7 g kg-1 total P) and a soybean
meal (470 g kg-1 crude protein; 4,2 g kg-1 total P) obtained from a
national feedstuffs supplier (EPSA, Valencia, Spain). The effect of acid
digestion was assessed by comparing results obtained in assays
comprising both stages of hydrolysis (acid + alkaline) compared with
those simulating only the alkaline phase. Amino acids and soluble P

were determined in dialysates using the o-phthaldialdehyde method
(Church et al., 1983) and the phospho-molybdovanadate method
(Engelen et al., 1994), respectively. Soluble protein in the reaction
mixture was determined after centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 3 °C,
15 min) of samples obtained from the inner chamber by the method
of Bradford (1976). Total amino acids and soluble P were determined
in those same samples after precipitation of protein by 200 g l-1

(4,000 rpm, 3 °C, 15 min) of the supernatant solution. Protein-
bound P was determined as the amount of total P in TCA precipitated
by the phospho-molybdovanadate method after acid mineralization
of the sample (AOAC, 1995). All the assays were run in triplicate. The
schematic diagram of the device used for assays in the gastrointestinal
model is presented in Fig. 1.
Speciﬁc assays were run to estimate release of N or P either from
the enzyme extract or the protein sources when incubated solely. The
values obtained allowed the estimation of what could be considered
the “basal liberation” of either amino acids or P not derived from the
action of the enzyme extract on the substrates, being subtracted to
determine the net release of products obtained when both the
enzymes and substrates were present in the reaction mixture. In all
cases, the amount of substrate used was equivalent to 80 mg substrate
protein in each digester. Taking into account the protease activities
determined in Experiment 1 (2,486 and 1,537 U ml crude extract,
respectively, for stomach and intestine), the E:S ratio used were 31
and 19 U mg-1 substrate protein in the acid and alkaline phases,
respectively. The general conditions and parameters evaluated in the

2.3. Statistical analysis
All assays were carried out in triplicate. Results were always
presented as the total amount of product released during the different
digestion phases. After veriﬁcation of the assumptions of normality
and homocedasticity, data were subjected to Student's t-test. In the
case of dialysed products, cumulated values were plotted against time,
ﬁtted to straight lines the slope of which represented the rate of
amino acid or soluble P released during the time of hydrolysis.
Progressive liberations of amino acids or soluble P were subjected to
ANCOVA, using time as the covariate. All the analyses were performed
using the Statgraphics software package (STSC Software Group,
Rockville, MD, USA).
3. Results
3.1. Experiment 1: Estimation of the E:S ratio existing for proteases in the
gut of rainbow trout
In spite of being maintained under similar conditions, ﬁsh showed
a great variability in the amount of protease activity determined in
their stomachs, which ranged from 10,000 to nearly 35,000 U g-1 fresh
tissue. Such variability was clearly evidenced when total activity of
acid protease was plotted against the amount of feed found in the
stomachs, previously normalised as the ratio existing between the
weights of feed contained and that of the stomach itself (Fig. 2). In
contrast, a mean value of 5,000 U g-1 fresh tissue was determined for
the activity of intestinal proteases, irrespective of the amount of feed
present in the stomach. The enzyme activities considered for further
calculations (23,162 and 5,075 U g-1 fresh tissue, for gastric and
intestinal proteases, respectively) were those corresponding to ﬁsh
with a 50% ﬁlling of the stomach, assuming that this ﬁgure could
better represent the enzyme activity existing at any moment in the
gut of a given ﬁsh. The calculation of the E:S also required to estimate
the average food intake in ﬁsh of such size (3% body weight day-1)as
well as the protein contents in feeds (44%), this resulting in 31.1 U and
19.2 U of total protease per mg ingested protein for gastric and
intestinal hydrolysis, respectively (Table 2). In this latter case, the
speciﬁc E:S ratio for trypsin or chymotrypsin was also calculated,
being 988 U BAEE and 17.3 U BTEE per mg substrate protein,
respectively.
3.2. Experiment 2: In vitro assays
The evolution of pH during the assays is shown in Fig. 2. The lapse
between acid and alkaline hydrolysis was considered a transition
phase (TP) and lasted nearly 30 min. Solubilization of protein during
the course of the hydrolysis of both protein sources is detailed in
Fig. 3. Nearly ﬁve times more soluble protein was released from SM
than from FM. Protein solubilisation was signiﬁcantly affected by the
pH change (from 2.0 to 9.0) taking place between the acid and the
alkaline phases of hydrolysis. A sharp decrease in protein solubility
was observed during this TP, with the effect more evident for SM. On

the other hand, the 60-min acid pre-digestion resulted in a 61 and 50%

The course of protein hydrolysis and P release are detailed in Figs. 5
and 6, while the total amount of amino acids or P produced by each
protein source and type of digestion are summarized in Table 3. In the
absence of enzyme extracts, a signiﬁcant release of amino acids from
both protein sources was measured, accounting for nearly 3 mg in the
case of FM or around 2 mg for SM. Released amino acids from FM were
not signiﬁcantly affected by the acid preincubation in assays
simulating the two phases of digestion, while SM showed a slight
increase. As expected, the action of proteases present in enzyme
extracts signiﬁcantly increased the amount of amino acids released
from the protein sources. In assays simulating only the intestinal
hydrolysis, the net release of products resulting from enzyme action
on FM and SM (obtained after subtraction of the basal values of N and
P released from either the extract and the substrate) resulted in a
104% and 45% increase over that obtained in the absence of enzyme,
respectively. Corresponding values obtained with the two-step
hydrolysis, were 143% and 72%, respectively. Then, the relative
increment produced by the acid pre-digestion over alkaline hydrolysis
accounted for 50% in FM and for more than 60% in SM. The total
amount of amino acids released from FM was signiﬁcantly higher than
that of SM for any type of digestion. The relative contribution of the
amino acids present in enzyme extracts to the reaction mixture
ranged from 37% (FM) to 53% (SM) and 45% to 59% for one and two-
step digestion, respectively (Table 3).
In a similar way, the different factors evaluated in the hydrolysis
also resulted in changes in available P. In the absence of enzyme
extracts, basal release of P was signiﬁcantly higher for FM (1.9-
3.7 mg) than for SM (0.5-0.6 mg) but a signiﬁcant effect of the acid
pre-incubation on this basal release was observed for FM. On the other
hand, soluble P released from enzyme extracts accounted for 19% or
47% of total P measured in one-step assays and 9% or 58% in two-step
assays for FM or SM, respectively. The net increase of P release over
the aforementioned values as a result of enzyme action on the protein
substrates was 9% and 6% (alkaline digestion) or 75% and 23%
(complete digestion) for FM and SM, respectively.
When cumulated values of both amino acids and P measured at
each sampling point were plotted against time, the slope of the linear
equations represented the rate of product release per min (Table 4).
For both protein sources, the presence of the acid pre-hydrolysis
resulted in a signiﬁcant increase in the rate of release of amino acids
over that measured with only alkaline hydrolysis. In the case of P, this
effect was also signiﬁcant and highly appreciable for FM.
All the above detailed information was resumed after calculation
of the bioaccessibility and bioavailability for both N and P in the
protein sources evaluated (Fig. 7). It was concluded that acid pre-
digestion signiﬁcantly increased protein bioaccesibility in both feed-
stuffs. On the other hand, a signiﬁcantly higher fraction of the protein-
N was potentially accessible for enzyme hydrolysis in SM (from 17 to
25%) when compared to FM (less than 10%). In contrast, the
bioavailability of nitrogen obtained after enzyme action was compar-
atively lower for SM. In the case of P, acid pre-digestion signiﬁcantly
increased its bioaccesibility from FM (from 26 to 87%) but to a lesser
extent from SM (from 34 to 65%). Bioavailability calculated for both
protein sources and types of digestion followed a similar pattern,
showing a clear reduction (30% and 20% for FM and SM, respectively,
for this latter type of digestion).

4. Discussion
Most studies oriented to the in vitro simulation of ﬁsh digestion
have been focused on the evaluation of protein hydrolysis. However,
regardless of the system used, few studies have considered the use of
an E:S ratio close to that existing in live ﬁsh, this being an important
requisite for a more realistic simulation of the digestion process.
Moreover, in order to decrease time for analysis and to increase
sensitivity, most studies use high concentrations of puriﬁed enzymes
or crude extracts which lie far from physiological conditions. In the
present study an attempt to correlate total production of digestive
proteases in live ﬁsh to their protein intake was carried out. The values
obtained closely agreed to estimations made by Alarcón et al. (2002)
for in vitro assays with pH-stat using enzyme extracts of sea bream,
but were far from those estimated by the works of Eid and Matty
(1989) or Tonheim et al. (2007). In this last work, the authors used a
closed in vitro method to estimate the digestibility of water-soluble
and water-insoluble protein fractions of some common ﬁsh larval
feeds and feed ingredients using two puriﬁed mammal enzymes and
one bacterial protease. The E:S ratios calculated from their data were
2,190 U BAEE, 29 U BTEE and 1.2 U BP per mg substrate protein, which
represent proximately twice the E:S ratio used in the present work for
adult ﬁsh.
In the present study, the simulation of pH variations taking place
during the different stages of feed digestion by the ﬁsh showed a clear
effect on protein solubilisation (and its further bioaccesibility). In
addition, the transition from acid to alkaline pH taking place in the
duodenum of live ﬁsh was simulated by a gradual modiﬁcation during
a certain time considered the “transition phase” of the hydrolysis.
During this stage, the solubility of the protein fraction of either FM or
SM (Fig. 4) or total P (Fig. 6) was signiﬁcantly affected. It is widely
recognized that pH is an important factor affecting protein solubility
(Turgeon et al., 1992; Monahan et al., 1995), since as the pH
approaches the isoelectric point, the negative and positive charges
existing among protein molecules are neutralized, this resulting in
precipitation. In contrast, at pH above or below the isoelectric point
the net charges of proteins provide more binding sites for water, what
increases their solubility (Hamm, 1960). In the case of FM, the soluble
protein fraction should be mainly constituted by myosin, which
accounts for nearly 55-60% of the myoﬁbrillar proteins (Lanier et al.,
2005) which has an isoelectric point of pH 4.8–6.2 (Malamud and
Drysdale, 1978; Kinsella, 1981).
Similarly, the major fraction of soluble protein in SM is formed by
globulins (glycinin and b-conglycinin), whose isoelectric point is close
to pH 4.8 (Virkar et al., 1982). Despite the quantitative differences
observed in the precipitation of FM and SM soluble protein fractions
during the TP, the amount of soluble protein available for enzyme
hydrolysis during the alkaline phase was signiﬁcantly increased when
the ingredients were exposed to a gastric pre-digestion (Fig. 4). In
fact, although simulation of the alkaline phase of the digestion seems
to be a good predictor of proteins hydrolysis in vivo (Dimes et al.,
1994, Bassompierre et al., 1998, Carter et al., 1999), probably it does
not reproduce accurately what occurs in the digestive tract of
carnivorous ﬁsh, on which the stomach pre-digestion produced by
HCl and pepsin enhances subsequent hydrolysis by intestinal
endopeptidases and exopeptidases. This facilitates the liberation of
amino acids particularly from some proteins like those present in ﬁsh,
meat and bone, blood and soybean meals (Alarcón et al., 2002). This
fact is supported by authors developing in vitro digestibility assays
aimed to reproduce human digestion on some feeds (Gatellier and
Santé-Lhoutellier, 2009) who point that treatment by pepsin prior to
trypsin/chymotrypsin treatment undoubtedly affects the nature of the
latter enzyme products obtained. In addition, the use of a two-step
digestion model provides interesting information about the relative
importance of each stage on total hydrolysis. As shown in Fig. 5, and
detailed in Table 3, acid pre-digestion with enzyme extracts of both
FM and SM resulted in a signiﬁcant increment of the amount of amino
acids released (48% and 62%, respectively) over those obtained only
during the intestinal phase of the hydrolysis. This effect can be
observed also when calculating the rates of amino acids release during
the dialysis phase (linear ﬁtting equations in Table 4). These results
completely agree with those obtained previously in assays performed
with a similar GIM using commercial feeds and enzyme extracts from
Argyrosomus regius (Hamdan et al., 2009).
The experimental design also evidenced the relative contribution
of amino acids released from either FM or SM in the absence of
enzymes (considered a “basal release”) to their total bioavailability, as

well as the enzyme extract contribution to the reaction mixture. This

methodologies, but it offers important information about the
differences in the composition of the molecular fractions of nitrogen
compounds present in a given protein source and also allows the
quantiﬁcation of the amount of amino acids really released by the
action of digestive enzymes.
Release of P was not so closely linked to the action of the enzymes,
but more dependent on the pH changes taking place in the digestive
tract. This could explain the relatively high levels of P measured in
assays carried out in the absence of enzyme extracts, as well as the
very signiﬁcant effect of the acid phase on its bioaccesibility (Sugiura
et al., 2006). Nevertheless, the presence of enzyme extracts in the
digestion mixture signiﬁcantly increased the amount of P released
(Table 3). This could be explained taking into account that a fraction of
the organic phosphates covalently linked to proteins may be
hydrolyzed by alkaline phosphatases present in the intestinal extracts
used in the GIM.
Nutrient bioavailability is an important but often nebulous concept
associated with the efﬁciency of absorption and metabolic utilization
of an ingested nutrient (Solomons, 2001; Gregory et al., 2005). In vitro
digestibility models can be used to measure accurately the bioacces-
sibility of a given nutrient and to estimate its bioavailability (Parada
and Aguilera, 2007). In the present study, important differences in
both parameters were found between the two protein sources tested,
as well as signiﬁcant effects of other factors, like the existence of acid
digestion prior to that taking place in the intestine. A higher N
bioaccessibility was determined for SM when compared to that of FM,
what can be apparently due to the high contents of soluble protein
potentially available for enzyme action in SM. However, digestive
enzymes present in trout extracts hydrolyzed more efﬁciently the
soluble protein present in FM, this resulting in a signiﬁcantly higher
bioavailability of amino acids for this latter protein source. Similarly,
in plant protein sources a great amount of P, ranging from 60% to 80%
of total P, is present as phytate (myo-inositol hexaphosphate)
(Ravindran et al., 1995). Since most carnivorous ﬁsh do not possess
phytase in their digestive tracts, the digestibility of phytate-P is very
poor (Ogino et al., 1979; Lall, 1991) and this could explain the low
values of P released in SM (Fig. 6).
Similar to the results obtained by Hamdan et al. (2009), the GIM
proved to be a good tool to separate the protein bioavailability
between two raw materials, but also demonstrated the potential to be
adapted for some nutrients, like dietary P, hence could allow an easy
preliminary and comparative evaluation of P bioaccessibility and
bioavailability. In this sense, assays performed with such GIM may be
highly interesting not only from a nutritional perspective, but also
from an environmental perspective.
In vitro values for both N and P bioaccessibility obtained in the
present study were appreciably less than those frequently obtained by
mean of in vivo digestibility trials. For example, N apparent
digestibility coefﬁcient of ﬁsh meal and soybean meal in rainbow
trout are near 89 to 97% (Gaylord and Barrows, 2008). Similarly, high
values have been reported by other authors who developed in vitro
assays using mammal enzymes at E:S ratios not based on data
obtained from live ﬁsh (Tonheim et al., 2007). Our results showed
values lesser to 30% of N bioaccessibility or bioavailability as in other

in vitro studies (e.g., Moyano and Savoie, 2001) and this could be
explained by a combination of the following factors:
a) Although the E:S ratio in the reactor was maintained close to that
estimated to exist in ﬁsh gut, their relative concentration was
much lower, due to the greater volume of the cell reaction
chamber (20 ml) when compared to that of ﬁsh stomach or
intestine. The presence of both enzymes and substrate in a more
diluted form should result in a much slower rate of hydrolysis. In
addition, the contractions of the gut taking place in the living ﬁsh
ensure a proper mixing of food and enzymes and results in a close
contact between them, an effect that is not achieved to the same
extent by the agitation provided by the magnetic stirrer within the
reaction chamber.
b) Diffusion of amino acids released by the action of proteases
through the semi- permeable membrane is a passive process based
only on the differences in their relative concentrations at both
sides of the membrane. This means that amino acids are probably
not evacuated by the circulating buffer at a similar rate to what
they are hydrolysed from protein. In contrast, amino acids released
as a result of digestion in vivo are also actively transported across
the intestinal mucosa (Berge et al., 2004) this resulting in a much
faster reduction of their concentration within the intestinal lumen.
c) The time established for digestion in the GIM is much shorter than
that available for digestion in live ﬁsh.
In spite of the above mentioned limitations, which are common to any
other in vitro assays routinely developed in nutritional evaluation of feeds
for terrestrial animals or humans, the key point is to consider that the
proposed assay was not designed to reproduce,butto simulate digestion
taking place in ﬁshgut,throughanevidentsimpliﬁcation of the real
process. As pointed by Larsson et al. (1997), the simulation of the
successive changing conditions in the gastrointestinal tract is probably the
most critical step for the estimation of the bioavailability of minerals and
trace elements. In addition, the availability of other nutritional compounds
is compromised by factors like low permeability and/or solubility within
the gut, as well as variations in gastrointestinal conditions (pH, enzymes,
interactions with other nutrients, etc). In the present work, the use of a
GIM which simulates the changes in pH and E:S ratios existing in the
digestive of a selected ﬁsh species evidenced the importance of including
an acid phase for a more realistic simulation of the effects that may take
place in the gut of stomached ﬁsh.
The proposed GIM may be an interesting alternative to other
methods widely used in the evaluation of protein hydrolysis (i.e., the
pH-stat method) because it allows better insight on the factors which
may affect bioavailability of N and P in live ﬁsh. The results obtained in
assays performed with different protein sources are clear enough to
support it as a powerful tool to making it applicable in the evaluation
and quality control of ingredients and feeds for aquaculture.
5. Conclusion
To our knowledge, the present study is the ﬁrst developing a GIM for
ﬁsh which allows to evaluate simultaneously the changes in bioaccessi-
bility and bioavailability of both N and P in the ingredients assayed. This
point is of a high relevance as a preliminary step in the nutritional
evaluation of feed ingredients and compounds feeds and could help lead
to a better understanding of the responses obtained with in vivo assays.
