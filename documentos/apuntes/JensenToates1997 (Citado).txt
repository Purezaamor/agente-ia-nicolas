Abstract 
The complexity and apparent inconsistency in most physiological ‘stress indicators’ has led 
many scientists to doubt the scientific value of the concept. However, there is clear evidence that 
the psychological perception of the stressor and the situation modulates the physiological 
reactions, and psychological concepts such as predictability and controllability are central to 
contemporary stress research. In this paper, we explore the possible advantages that can be gained 
from integrating the psychological and physiological stress theories into a formal motivational 
framework. We argue that stress can be perceived as a state where a feedback-regulated 
motivational system cannot be down-regulated owing to insufficient feedback, i.e. the system is on 
‘open loop’. With the motivational model we advocate, stress can arise both as a consequence of 
adverse external stimulation and as a consequence of internal causal factors driving the animal to 
attempt to carry out species-specific behaviour, such as nest building or dustbathing. We conclude 
that physiological variables will continue to be central in assessing stress, but they can only be 
properly interpreted in a motivational context. 0 1997 Elsevier Science B.V. 
Keywords: Coping; Nest building; Dustbathing; Stress indicators; Feedback 
1. Introduction 
It is recognised that a reliable assessment of stress cannot be made solely with the aid 
of hormonal variables (Toates, 1995). However, possibly owing to the historical burden 
from the early stress theory of Selye (1950), there is still a strong emphasis on the 
hypothalamic-pituitary axis (HPA) and other physiological pathways in contemporary 
stress research (for a review see Manser, 1992).

The physiological responses of animals to aversive situations are complex and often 
inconsistent (Mason, 1975). In addition, different individuals may react consistently 
differently to the same challenge and in situations of unambiguous stress there can either 
be a sympathetic or a parasympathetic/HPA dominance, due to constitutional differ- 
ences between individuals of the same species (Van Holst, 1985; Benus et al., 1991). 
Furthermore, there is evidence to suggest that the psychological context is crucial in 
assessing stress (Mason, 1971, 1975). Weiss (1971) identified controllability as an 
important causal factor in the stress response in addition to the physical quality of the 
stressor. This has effectively undermined all attempts to develop one uniform physio- 
logical stress index, since stress is simply not characterised by physiological processes 
only. 
Findings such as these have led some researchers to suggest that the term should be 
abandoned altogether in scientific contexts (e.g. Rushen, 1986; Freeman, 1987). Others 
have attempted to rescue it by using various subdivisions and qualifiers, such as 
eustress, distress, positive stress, social stress, etc. (see e.g. Broom and Johnson, 1993). 
This has caused theoretical and terminological confusion, and there is a certain need for 
synthetic enterprises in this area. 
We will argue that physiological variables are and will continue to be essential 
indicators of the stress responses of animals, but, in agreement with Mason (1975), we 
will regard stress as primarily a behavioural/psychological phenomenon. Therefore, we 
expect that there will probably never be a single unambiguous ‘stress indicator’ based 
upon a physiological measure. Rather, we will argue, any physiological measure must be 
evaluated against a thorough analysis of the behaviour and the motivational state of the 
animal. 
The aim of this paper is to explore the possibilities of including stress in a formal 
motivational theory, and to examine some of the possible consequences for strategies of 
stress assessment. 
2. What do we mean by ‘stress’? 
Even if there is no consensus about the precise meaning of the word ‘stress’, its 
frequent usage suggests that it captures some essential features of the reactions of 
animals to harmful stimuli. A concept defends its place in science if it describes a 
reliable phenomenon and increases parsimony of language and explanation. 
In exploring stress responses from a motivational perspective, we expect that some of 
the original elements of the stress concept put forward by Selye (1950) will probably be 
essential for a fruitful theory to emerge. For example, we will search for some degree of 
non-specificity in the response patterns. If the forms of responses to challenges and 
threats were completely unique to every single type of challenge, there would be little 
use of the concept of stress. However, the non-specific and unifying state need not be 
primarily physiological. Likewise, it appears to us to make little sense to talk about 
stress if the reactions vary completely between sets of individuals. However, if only 
some aspect of the reaction is the same to all individuals, that aspect would be of great 
importance to stress theory.

The main reasons for the assumption that stress is a behavioural rather than a 
physiological phenomenon are the results from a series of experiments conducted in the 
seventies. Mason (1975) observed in experiments with monkeys that slow increases in 
temperature left the hypothalamic-pituitary axis (HPA) activity of the animals unaf- 
fected, whereas rapid increase of exactly the same magnitude caused severe activity in 
the HPA. Psychological factors were suggested to be the crucial difference between the 
situations. 
Weiss (1971) demonstrated the importance of three psychological key concepts: 
predictability, controllability and feedback. When rats where exposed to electric shocks 
via the tail, the responses in terms of HPA activity and ulcerations were much smaller if 
the shocks were pre-signalled to the rat and even more if the rat could influence the 
situation, by turning a wheel to postpone or shorten the shock. When the rats were 
offered independent feedback to certify that their control attempts were successful, their 
physiological reactions were as small as yoked conspecifics which never received any 
shock. The conclusion of the experiments is that aversive stimuli of similar nature and 
magnitude may cause completely different levels of physiological reactions dependent 
on the experiences of the animals. 
Clearly these types of experiments call for a change of emphasis from measuring only 
physiological variables to include the behavioural and psychological situation of the 
exposed individuals. However, there are some additional problems, in particular with 
respect to the use of stress in relation to applied ethology and animal welfare. Here, 
questions are frequently raised about whether a particular housing system or husbandry 
routine is stressful (see for example Broom and Johnson, 1993). It is not always clear 
what the nature of the stressors would be, for example to a battery hen which can not 
dustbathe, perch or prepare a nest. Obviously, if the hens are stressed in the same sense 
as those rats of Weiss (1971) which were shocked without prediction and control, the 
stressors must be of a different nature; the external stimuli appear not particularly 
harmful to the hen in comparison to the inescapable electric shocks of the rats. In cases 
like this, it is our impression that most researchers assume stress to arise from a conflict 
between the motivation of the animals and the environmental conditions in which it 
lives. Here is an unresolved conflict of theories: the stress theories which emphasis the 
role of psychological experiences (Weiss, 1971; Mason, 1975) apply to stressors 
imposed from outside the animals but it is not clear how this can be extended to the 
situation where stress arises due to conflicts between internal states and environmental 
conditions. 
We will argue that this apparent conflict of theories can be resolved by incorporating 
stress (in the sense of loss of predictability/controllability) into a control systems model 
of motivation. 
3. A control systems motivational model 
Earlier theoretical work clearly suggests that motivational aspects are central to 
understanding stress (see Archer, 1979; Broom and Johnson, 1993; Jensen, 1995; 
Toates, 1995). Ever since Lorenz (1950) presented his famous ‘psychohydraulic’

Somewhat simplified, the models fall into either a class of homeostatic models, which 
rely on feedback and setpoints, or ‘drive-models’, such as those of Lorenz. We have

different models (Toates and Jensen, 1991) and applied this synthesis to the concept of 
‘behavioural needs’ (Jensen and Toates, 1993). In doing so, we have suggested a 
synthetic and conceptual model (Jensen and Toates, 19931, and we will argue that it can 
be of help in understanding the phenomenon of stress. A slightly modified version of our 
original model is shown in Fig. 1. 
The model is not explanatory, but should be seen as an aid in organising thoughts and 
questions about animal behaviour. Essential parts of the model are the three feedback 
loops down-regulating the motivational system and allowing the animal to switch 
activities. The loops are: 
1. modification of external causal factors by means of the behaviour (e.g. depletion of 
food items at a certain place), which may be recognised by the animal in relation to 
some cognitive goal representation; 
2. modification of internal causal factors due to behaviour (e.g. filling of the gut); 
3. proprioceptive feedback achieved through execution of the species-specific move- 
ment patterns. 
The model states that when a certain motivational system is stimulated by the joint 
effect of external and internal stimulation, neural events will cause the animal to decide 
to engage in behaviour specific to that system. Simultaneously a goal representation for 
the behaviour may arise, and these combined events (decision making, goal representa- 
tion) are what we refer to as motivation.

The important aspect of the model for the present discussion is that it assumes that an 
animal will rely on different sorts of feedback to turn the motivation off. In some cases 
this feedback will include the achievement of a specific goal, which may be cognitively 
represented. 
We are, of course, not unaware that simple systems representations of motivation 
suffer from specific weaknesses, as has been demonstrated by McFarland and Houston 
(1981). In particular, it leaves open the question about what rules that may govern an 
animal’s selection among different systems, i.e. the rules of optimal sequences of 
behaviour. However, to start out, we will make the simplifying (and obviously not 
totally correct) assumption that a motivational system can be analysed in isolation from 
other systems. However, even if it is beyond the scope of the present paper, it is clear 
that interactions of motivational systems will be important to incorporate into a more 
complex theory of motivation and stress. This will be evident from some of the 
examples described later. 
3.1. Motivation and stress 
Can stress be incorporated into this framework? We argue that ‘classical’ stressors, 
like heat or electric shock, can be regarded as causal factors affecting specific motiva- 
tional systems-in these cases presumably temperature regulation and escape. The 
experiments of Weiss (1971) and Mason (1975) show that these causal factors become 
harmful, and can thus be regarded as stressors, when the animal fails to take appropriate 
action. 
A stressor can therefore be regarded simply as a causal factor-we would tend to call 
this causal factor a stressor only when the animal fails to respond adequately. As 
demonstrated by Weiss (1971), successful responses (i.e. responses that turn off or 
inhibit HPA activation, or minimise any harmful effects of HPA activity) include two 
different aspects. 
1. The animal needs to take some action, using behaviour patterns that appear motiva- 
tionally relevant. Turning a wheel with the forepaws to terminate a shock appears 
sufficiently like the behaviour recruited for normal escape and thus allows the rat 
control, whereas an operant requirement to, for example, groom in order to terminate 
shock does not achieve the same effects. Hence, it is important as to how the animal 
achieves the termination of the stressor, not only that it can do it. 
2. The consequences of the action must be perceived by the animal. The termination of 
the shock is of course an obvious signal, but Weiss (1971) also demonstrated that any 
feedback signal that would increase the certainty of the rat that it was its actual 
behaviour that caused the termination will decrease the adverse physiological reac- 
tions even more. 
With the approach outlined here, an animal managing to respond adequately, and 
succeeding in handling a stimulus (a potential stressor) by means of executing a relevant 
behaviour, can be said to cope. In contrast, when the feedback is not sufficient, the 
animal does not cope and will enter a state of stress. 
To us, this line of reasoning suggests that stress may be conceived of as a state of a 
motivational system, where the system is activated, but the actions of the animal are not

sufficient to deactivate the system. Such a system would, in control theory language, be 
said to be in an open-loop state (for a more in-depth analysis of the same approach see 
Toates, 1995). 
As we have argued elsewhere (Toates and Jensen, 1991; Jensen and Toates, 1993) the 
commonly applied dichotomy between internal and external causal factors (for example 
in relation to the problem of ‘behavioural needs’; Hughes, 1980) is not a motivationally 
relevant dichotomy. To the decision making of the animal, it makes no difference 
whether motivation is affected by variations in external or in internal causal factors, and 
the model in Fig. 1 treats both equally. Therefore, with regard to stress, we conclude that 
it is irrelevant whether a motivational system has been activated by, for example, a 
sudden increase in specific hormones or, for example, an electric shock. A state that we 
would identify as stress would arise when the animal fails to achieve proper feedback 
when it attempts to act according to its motivational state. 
4. Applications to some selected examples 
In a first step towards application of our model, we examine three cases where we 
believe our approach will be helpful. The examples all deal with possible instances of 
acute, short-lasting stress, which are the most obvious first type of cases to examine. 
Later, we will briefly extend the reasoning to deal with long-lasting, chronic stress. The 
examples we have chosen represent examples where, under normal conditions for the 
animal, motivation is aroused by changes in the external environment (temperature 
regulation), by variations in internal factors (nest building) and by the joint variation of 
internal and external factors (dustbathing). 
4.1. Example I: temperature regulation 
Homeothermic animals try to keep body temperature within specific limits regardless 
of ambient temperature. Deviations from the optimal outer temperature can be regarded 
as external causal factors activating cooling behaviour (e.g. panting, wallowing), or 
heating behaviour (e.g. sunbasking, shivering). 
As long as the animal succeeds in its attempts and the body temperature is maintained 
within the required limits, it can be said to cope. Coping can be costly in that it demands 
high energy requirements, but as long as the animal successfully maintains the required 
temperature, it is not stressed according to our model. Only when the attempts are 
fruitless and body temperature moves out of the acceptable range is the animal in a state 
of stress as we view the concept here. 
This can be visualised in a formal way through a control system representation (Fig. 
2), which consists of an extract of relevant specific pathways from the general model in 
Fig. 1. Self evidently, this is an overly simplified model of temperature regulation, 
which is only presented to give an indication of the difference we would like to make 
between coping and stress. When more factors are invoked in the model in order to 
make it more realistic, a simple algebraic representation of the state of stress will be 
difficult to achieve. However, we argue that what counts qualitatively is whether or not 
the animal is successful in behaving so as to down-regulate the system.

4.2. Example 2: nest building in sows 
Pre-farrowing nest building in sows appears to be triggered almost completely by 
physiological changes occurring at about 15-20 h pre-partum (Widowski et al., 1990). 
Some studies suggest that an increase in prolactin is an important part of this, whereas 
other data suggest that other physiological processes may be involved (Widowski et al., 
1990; Lawrence et al., 1994). Proprioceptive feedback seems to be very important in the 
control of the behaviour (Jensen, 1993), but environmental stimuli also play a significant 
role in the structuring of the behaviour (Jensen et al., 1993). All in all, this behaviour 
clearly represents a case where under normal conditions, physiological events associated 
with the normal gestation and parturition process play the key role in activating the 
behaviour. 
A suggested simplified control system flow chart of nest building is shown in Fig. 3. 
Again, we are aware of the simplifications made in the model, but we believe the 
representation is sufficient for the present arguments. 
In this case, according to our model stress can arise when the sow is confined and 
unable to perform all or some motor patterns of the behaviour or when the causal 
consequences (a nest) cannot be achieved. Baxter (1982) observed tethered sows that 
were apparently ‘locked up’ in the initial part of nest building and which performed 
exaggerated rooting and pawing on the floor to the extent that one sow had a bleeding 
nose disc. This is precisely what would be expected if we view this state as being one of 
a motivational control system with a goal but in which the loop is impossible to close. 
Furthermore, Lawrence et al. (1994) reported that sows in farrowing crates without

4.3. Example 3: dustbathing in hens 
Dustbathing in hens has been thoroughly investigated from a motivational perspec- 
tive. Internal factors are clearly important (Vestergaard, 1980), but the behaviour is also 
modified by external factors (Van Liere et al., 1991). 
An essential part of the release mechanism seems to be a build-up of motivation in a 
Lorenzian manner in interaction with a pronounced diurnal rhythm (Vestergaard, 1980). 
According to a model by Hogan (1996, this volume), this rhythm may involve diurnally 
shifting thresholds. It also appears that proprioceptive feedback is important even if the 
duration and organisation of different phases are affected by external factors in the form 
of lipids in the plumage (Van Liere et al., 1991). For battery hens, normal dustbathing is 
not possible. 
Fig. 4 shows the same type of simplified flow chart as before for dustbathing 
behaviour. According to the model hens may enter a state of stress when they are 
prevented from normal dustbathing. However, a crucial question is the relative role of 
proprioceptive feedback: if this is the dominant feedback pathway, vacuum dustbathing 
might be sufficient to cause an adequate down-regulation of the motivational system. To 
our knowledge, there are no physiological data available on vacuum dustbathing birds. 
However, our model would predict a certain increased HPA activity in vacuum 
dustbathing birds in comparison with normally behaving animals.

extended temporally to cover cases of chronic stress. If we assume that dustbathing is

5. Implications for stress assessment 
We believe that our model may help to resolve the apparent discontinuities between 
stress theories focusing on physiological assessment on the one hand and on psychologi- 
cal experiences on the other. As is well recognised in physiology, HPA activation will 
be caused by almost any perceived or real threat to an animal, and under conditions 
where the animal can act appropriately, this activation is adaptive (Munck et al., 1984). 
The view we have presented here calls for a different approach to the measurement of 
stress than traditional. Rather than focusing solely on physiological changes we need to 
connect these with behavioural signs that an animal’s motivation is in a state of open 
loop. The foundation for such searching is a firm knowledge of the natural behaviour of 
the species, which will provide a basis against which to interpret behavioural changes in 
captivity. Studies such as those of Jensen (1988) on pigs in semi-natural enclosures may 
provide some of the necessary basis. 
Important signs of stress can be for example excessive performance of certain 
behavioural elements in a system or indications that the animal is unable to achieve the 
goal for a behaviour. High frequencies of vacuum activities and displacement activities 
may also be warning signals. Maybe stereotypies offer one of the most dramatic

demonstrations of an animal being trapped in a motivational open-loop state, even if 
there is still considerable ambiguity with regard to the motivational significance of 
stereotyped activities (Lawrence and Rushen, 1993). 
Even if we generally have chosen to discuss only one isolated motivational system at 
a time, we are of course aware of the complications that arise when we start considering 
interactions between systems. However, we argue that the same basic line of reasoning 
can be applied. For example, if an animal is ‘locked’ in a particular motivational system, 
the disproportionate amount of time it spends in this system may have consequences for 
other systems. For example, the animal that manages to cope with an extreme tempera- 
ture situation at a high cost might well be ‘unstressed’ from the perspective of this 
system. However, since so much energy is used up for coping with temperature, other 
systems may soon enter an open-loop state. For example, the animal may fail to feed or 
drink adequately, and will therefore quickly be stressed from the perspective of those 
systems. Elaborating further on this complication of the model lies beyond our present 
goals, but it is clear that stress would have to be viewed as a summed state of 
disturbances in all motivational systems of the animal. Since animals normally perform 
behavioural sequences according to an optimal trajectory through the causal factor space 
(McFarland and Houston, 198 I), sub-optimal switching between activities may therefore 
also prove to be valuable signs of stress. 
Of course, physiological indices are essential, but they will be of limited value if they 
are not interpreted in the light of the behaviour of the animal. For example, elevated 
cortisol levels are found both in sows engaged in proper nest building and in tethered 
sows during the period when they are motivated for nest building, even if the level is 
higher in tethered sows (Lawrence et al., 1994). However, according to our model of 
stress it is only the tethered ones that are out of control and thereby suffering from 
stress, an aspect that would not be possible to incorporate with traditional stress 
physiology models. 
As already mentioned, the examples we have examined are primarily concerned with 
acute stress states. However, there is no logical difficulty in extending the reasoning to 
situations where animals live continuously in chronic or intermittent states of open 
loops. We would expect the total time an animal spends in the open-loop state to be 
related to the degree of damage that is caused to it by stress. Hence, brief, rare periods 
of stress, such as in the case of nest building in the sow, which only occurs for about 20 
h about two times a year, may not be sufficient to cause measurable pathological stress 
consequences. On the other hand, animals living under consistently crowded conditions, 
where for example the agonistic systems are permanently on open loop, may develop 
severe stress disorders. 
Another aspect that is out of the scope of this paper, but which we believe can be 
incorporated into the same line of thinking, is the possibility that animals may have 
either active or passive coping strategies (Benus et al., 1991). Even if animals may have 
different strategies to cope with aversive situations, both the active and the passive ones 
will probably find themselves locked in an open loop when they fail to achieve the goals 
of their strategies. Although the physiological reactions to this situation may be very 
different according to coping strategy, the open-loop state of the motivational system 
will probably be the common denominator.

6. Conclusion 
We have suggested approaching stress as a state in which a normally closed loop 
motivational system is on open loop. We believe that such a model will offer a more 
integrative perspective enabling the multiple factors that can underlie stress to be better 
defined. We also believe that this offers a greater hope of establishing criteria of stress 
and stress management than does a criterion of a particular physiological state such as 
corticosteroid levels. Such indices remain central to stress assessment, but it is necessary 
to interpret them in terms of the performance of a control system.
