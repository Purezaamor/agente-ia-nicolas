The Obesity Gene in Swine: Sequence and Expression
of Porcine Leptin1,2
T. G. Ramsay, X. Yan, and C. Morrison
Pennington Biomedical Research Center and The Department of Animal Science,
Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge 70808
ABSTRACT: Leptin is a 16-kDa protein secreted by
adipocytes that has been proposed to regulate feed
intake in mice, rats, and humans. The present study
was designed to characterize porcine leptin structure
and expression. Successful RT-PCR resulted in de-
velopment of a cDNA clone to the full length coding
region of porcine leptin. Sequence data demonstrate
85% base homology to rodent, 88% to human, and a
92% homology to the bovine sequence. For assessment
of porcine leptin gene expression, total RNA was
extracted from the subcutaneous adipose tissue of
genetically selected high backfat pigs and from
contemporary crossbred swine. Total RNA derived
from genetically selected high fat pigs contained 113%
higher ( P < .05) concentrations of porcine leptin
mRNA than total RNA derived from contemporary
crossbred pigs. Western blotting was used to evaluate
serum levels of porcine leptin in genetically selected
high backfat and contemporary, crossbred pigs. Rela-
tive levels of porcine leptin in sera from obese swine
were approximately 306% higher ( P < .05) than levels
present in sera from contemporary, crossbred swine.
These data indicate that leptin is expressed in pigs,
the expressed protein is secreted into the bloodstream,
and obese swine express higher levels of leptin mRNA
and protein than nonobese swine at similar body
weight.

Introduction
Leptin is a protein secreted by adipocytes
(Frederich et al., 1995). Leptin was originally identi-
fied following physical mapping of the gene present in
obese, ob/ob mice (Zhang et al., 1994). The ob/ob
mice are leptin-deficient because of a mutation in this
gene. Replacement therapy with mouse leptin cor-
rected the hypothermia, hyperinsulinemia, and the
elevated feed intake in ob/ob mice (Pellymounter,
1995). Intracerebral ventricular administration to
mice without this mutation in the leptin gene also
produces a reduction in feed intake (Campfield et al.,
1995). The effects of leptin on feed intake have led to
the hypothesis that leptin has a role in the feedback
regulation of adipose mass on feed intake. Further
studies have now demonstrated that leptin can induce
infertile, ob/ob mice to reproduce (Chehab et al.,
1996) and can hasten the age at which puberty begins
in normal mice (Chehab et al., 1997). Thus, this
protein has potent actions on the physiology of the
mice.
Southern analysis by Zhang et al. (1994) demon-
strated the presence of base sequences within porcine
DNA homologous to mouse leptin. Therefore, the
primary purpose of this experiment was to find a
porcine leptin cDNA sequence comprising the com-
plete coding sequence. The function of this gene and
its expression to pig physiology/growth is currently
unknown. Therefore, this experiment tested the
hypothesis that porcine leptin expression (i.e., mRNA
and protein) varies with propensity for adipose tissue
deposition. This study was designed to assess porcine
leptin mRNA levels in the adipose tissue of high fat
pigs vs contemporary, crossbred pigs and to assess
differences in relative porcine leptin protein levels in
the blood of these genetically selected high backfat
and contemporary, crossbred, low backfat pigs.
Methodology
Obese lines of swine were developed by selection for
maximal thickness (High fat) of backfat (Hetzer and
Harvey, 1967; Hetzer and Miller, 1973). Subcutane-

Pig Leptin cDNA Isolation
Initial attempts at RT-PCR utilizing primers
designed to the 5′ and 3′ ends of the human leptin
cDNA sequence did not produce a PCR product.
Therefore, internal sequences homologous among rats,
mice, and human leptin cDNA were used to design
primers that permitted amplification of a cDNA
sequence coding from base 130 to 504 (relevant to the
human coding sequence). The PCR was conducted
using plasmid DNA prepared from a porcine adipocyte
cDNA library that was generated through subtractive
hybridization of adipocyte mRNA from stromal vascu-
lar cell mRNA derived from the subcutaneous adipose
tissue of 14-d-old pigs (Srinivas and Ramsay, 1991).
Following PCR amplification, this 374-bp DNA
fragment was subsequently cloned into PCRII (TA
cloning kit; Invitrogen, San Diego CA). The fragment
was identified as the 3′ coding region of the porcine
leptin gene by DNA sequencing (Sequenase kit; USB,
Cleveland, OH). Based on the 3′ porcine leptin
sequence, a 190-bp 5′ coding region was identified by
using RT-PCR. The RT-PCR (RT-PCR Access Kit,
Promega, Madison, WI) was performed using total
RNA isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue from a
45-kg crossbred pig. Finally, the complete coding
region of the porcine ob gene was amplified by PCR
(forward primer: ATGAATTCATGCGCTGTG-
GACCCCTGTG; reverse primer: ATAAGCTTTCAG-
CAGCCAGGGCTGAGGT) and subcloned into PCRII.
Four clones were subjected to DNA sequencing in both
directions using the dideoxy method (Sequenase kit,
USB, Cleveland, OH) for confirmation.
RNA Extraction
Total RNA was extracted from individual sub-
cutaneous adipose tissue samples from three high fat
and crossbred swine with TriReagent using a slight
modification of the standard procedure. No RNA was
obtainable from samples of USDA low backfat pigs
due to sample degradation. For one experiment,
human abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue was
obtained at open laparoscopy and processed in a
similarly to porcine tissue. Briefly, 250 mg of tissue
was extracted with 2.5 mL of TriReagent (Molecular
Research Center, Cincinnati, OH) and permitted to
react for 10 min instead of the standard 5 min.
Chloroform (750 mL) was subsequently added, and
the mixture was shaken briefly and permitted to sit at
room temperature for 30 min, rather than the
standard 10 min. All remaining steps were identical to
the original protocol that accompanies the product.
Use of this procedure typically increases the recovery
of adipose tissue RNA by two- to fourfold in compari-
son to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Total RNA
was dissolved in formamide:water (50:50) and quanti-
fied spectrophotometrically at 260 nm. The RNA was
electrophoresed in a 1.0% agarose gel and stained with
ethidium bromide to determine integrity of the RNA
and to ensure equivalent loading for Northern analy-
sis.
Northern Blot Analysis
Blot Preparation. Total RNA from individual tissue
samples was denatured and separated by electropho-
resis in a 1.2% agarose, 2.2 M formaldehyde gel in .01
M MOPS running buffer. The RNA was stained with
ethidium bromide and visualized on an ultraviolet
transilluminator to ensure consistent loading between
treatment groups and to record the distance of
migration of the 18S and 28S ribosomal bands for use
as molecular weight markers. The RNA was trans-
ferred to nylon membrane (Hybond-N, Amersham
Corporation, Arlington Heights, IL) by capillary
transfer.
The DNA (25 ng) was radiolabeled using a
multiprime kit purchased from Amersham (Arlington
Heights, IL) and 50 mCi of [a-32P]dCTP purchased
from Du Pont NEN (Boston, MA). The 370-bp porcine
leptin cDNA was derived as described above. The
human 28S RNA oligonucleotide was purchased from
Ambion (Austin, TX). The oligonucleotide was end-
labeled using T4 polynucleotide kinase (Promega,
Madison WI) and 50 mCi of [g-32P]ATP.
Hybridization. Membranes were prehybridized in
RapidHyb buffer (Amersham) for 1 h at 65°C. Heat-
denatured radiolabeled porcine leptin probe (1.5 to 2.0
× 108 cpm/g DNA) was added, and the hybridization
was continued at 65°C for an additional 4 h. Mem-
branes were rinsed twice with 2× SSC and .1% SDS at
room temperature, then once with 1× SSC and .1%
SDS at 65°C. Each wash was for 15 min. Membranes
were exposed to a phosphorimaging cassette (Molecu-
lar Dynamics, Sunnyvale, CA) for 72 h before
quantification. Hybridized probe was removed by
boiling blots in .1% SDS, according to the manufac-
turer’s (Amersham) recommendation. For assessment
of 28S RNA, prehybridization was performed with
RapidHyb buffer for 30 min at 42°C. The oligonucleo-
tide 28S ribosomal RNA probe was added and the
hybridization continued for 45 min at 42°C. Mem-
branes were rinsed twice with 5× SSC and .1% SDS at
room temperature, then once with 1× SSC and .1%
SDS, and finally with .1× SSC and .1% SDS at room
temperature. Following the washes, membranes were
exposed to a phosphorimaging cassette for 4 h before
quantification.

Ligand Blotting
Total porcine leptin protein in pig sera was
estimated using SDS-PAGE, followed by
chemiluminescent detection with rabbit anti-human
leptin IgG antibody. Primary antibody was generated
against the predicted amino terminal sequence of
human leptin (CRLQGSLQDMLWQLDLSPG; Harris
et al., 1997). This antibody detects a protein of ∼16 kd
in serum. The band disappears from swine serum
when the antiserum is preincubated with excess
recombinant mouse leptin (Peprotech, Rocky Hill,
NJ). Ten microliters of each serum sample, a recom-
binant mouse leptin standard (PeproTech), and a
pooled serum sample from 4-mo-old, obese fa/fa rats
were diluted (1:6.5) in sample buffer (.125 M Tris-
HCl, pH 6.8), mixed with 4× loading buffer (100 mM
Tris, pH 8.3, 40% glycerol, 20% b-mercaptoethanol,
10% SDS, and bromphenol blue), and heat-treated at
95°C for 3 to 5 min. Serum samples were separated in
12% SDS polyacrylamide denaturing gels in Tris-
glycine buffer (.025 M Tris, .192 M glycine, .1% SDS,
pH 8.3). They were transferred to nitrocellulose
membrane (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Richmond, CA) by
electroblotting in .025 M Tris, .192 glycine, 20%
methanol buffer at 250 mA for 8 h at 4°C. The
membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dried milk in
Tris-buffered saline ( TBS:10mMTris HCl, pH 7.4,
150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl) and washed once with
TBS, .05% Tween 20 ( TTBS) and again once in TBS.
The blots were incubated with primary antiserum (1:
500 in TBS, .1% nonfat dried milk) for 2 h. Blots were
washed three times in TTBS and once in TBS before a
1-h incubation at room temperature with goat anti-
rabbit IgG (H+L) horseradish-peroxidase conjugate
(Zymed Co., South San Francisco, CA). Membranes
were washed again and the protein-antibody com-
plexes were detected with the ECL Western blotting
detection system (Amersham).
Molecular weights of the proteins were estimated
by comparison to prestained standards (Novex, San
Diego, CA). Relative quantities of leptin in each
serum sample were determined by densitometry with
the use of an Ambis image acquisition and analysis
system (Ambis, San Diego, CA). The recombinant
mouse leptin (.5 ng) was included as a standard on all
blots to permit relative comparison between blots.
Densitometric values for individual serum samples (n
= 4/treatment) were standardized against the recom-
binant mouse leptin and the derived values for
duplicate gels were then averaged before statistical
analysis.
Data were analyzed by t-test using the SigmaStat
for Windows Release 1.0 computer program (Jandel
Scientific, San Rafael, CA). Effects were considered
significant at the 95% confidence level ( P < .05).
Results
Isolation of a cDNA for porcine leptin was initially
difficult because primers designed against the open
reading frame of either mouse or human sequences did
not produce a relevant PCR product. Following release
of the bovine sequence (Genbank accession #U43943),
the inability to identify the initiation and termination
sites within the porcine sequence was understood.
Homology at these 5′ and 3′ regions of the open
reading frame was limited when compared with the
corresponding regions of the human or rodent leptin
cDNA sequences. Genbank BLAST analysis of the
complete coding sequence of porcine leptin (Figure 1,

Northern analysis indicated that pig Leptin gene
codes for a single mRNA transcript of approximately
4.4 kb, similar in size to the human mRNA (Figure
2). Comparison of the pLeptin mRNA level in high fat
and contemporary pigs demonstrated that the levels
are elevated in these few, obese pigs but only a
fraction of the level in fa/fa (Zucker) obese rats
(Figure 3). pLeptin mRNA concentrations were in-
creased by 113% ( P < .05) in these obese swine
relative to contemporary swine.
Genbank BLAST peptide prediction analysis sug-
gests that the estimated peptide sequence (Figure 4)
represents 93% homology to the bovine sequence, but
only 83 to 87% homology to the human or rat
sequence. Western analysis of serum from high fat
and contemporary crossbred pigs demonstrated that
serum levels of leptin vary with the propensity for fat
deposition (Figure 5). Leptin levels were elevated in
obese swine by 306% ( P < .05) relative to contem-
porary, crossbred swine, suggesting that the high fat
pigs have elevated serum levels. These changes in free
leptin protein level are in agreement with the changes
in leptin mRNA steady state level in obese, high fat
pigs.

The existence of leptin in pigs was predicted by the
Southern analysis in the original report on the
discovery of leptin by Zhang et al. (1994). Neuensch-
wander et al. (1996) have reported a partial cDNA

polymorphisms but will require further evaluation.
The present experiment also demonstrated that the
leptin protein is secreted into the bloodstream of pigs.
The sequence analysis demonstrates high homology
with bovine leptin, yet lower with human or rodent
leptins. We have previously attempted to use commer-
cially available leptin kits to measure porcine leptin,
but with no success (data not presented). Analysis on
Western ligand blots has also demonstrated a lack of
affinity of the antibodies from human or mouse leptin
RIA kits for porcine leptin (data not presented). Thus,
the epitopes for the commercially available antibodies
are different from the relevant regions of the porcine
leptin molecule, necessitating development of antibod-
ies specific to porcine leptin or to other epitopes of
human or mouse leptin.
Peptide sequence prediction suggests that porcine
leptin may be necessary for experimentation in swine.
The limited homology of the predicted peptide se-
quence with human or mouse leptin may result in
differences in tertiary structure such that binding to
the porcine receptor may be species-specific. However,
this hypothesis is yet to be tested.

expression of this gene differs between lean and obese

The role of leptin in pigs is unknown at this time.
In ob/ob mice, leptin has been shown to reduce feed
intake (Campfield et al., 1995; Halaas et al., 1995;
Pellymounter et al., 1995). In other animal models of
obesity or their normal controls, leptin has, at most, a
very limited effect on feed intake, except at pharmaco-
logical concentrations. Feed intake has not been
reported to differ between high fat pigs and low fat or
contemporary lines of pigs (Steele et al., 1982; Yen et
al., 1983), suggesting perhaps a limited relationship
between leptin serum concentrations and feed intake
in swine. However, additional studies are necessary
using modern facilities and more accurate equipment
to monitor feed intake to confirm this similarity of
intake between lines of pigs.
Leptin’s major function in swine may lie elsewhere.
Leptin has also been reported to reverse the infertility
of ob/ob mice. Female ob/ob mice are capable of
becoming pregnant and maintaining pregnancy follow-
ing leptin administration (Chehab et al., 1996),
although they fail to lactate. A reproductive effect is
not sex-specific, because leptin administration to male
or female ob/ob mice alters serum levels of reproduc-
tive hormones and gonadal function (Barash et al.,
1996). The db/db mouse is also relatively infertile but
is leptin-resistant rather than -deficient (Hummel et
al., 1966; Chua et al., 1996). Like the db/db mouse,
the high fat line of pigs has elevated serum leptin
levels relative to nonobese animals. The high fat pig
also produces small litters (seven to nine pigs/litter;
Hetzer and Miller, 1973); however this small litter
size may be related as much to inbreeding of the line
as to the obesity in these pigs. We hypothesize that
high fat pigs are resistant to leptin, and this
resistance contributes to the reduced reproductive
function in these animals in comparison to contem-
porary, crossbred swine.
Implications
Leptin may have applications for enhancing growth
and(or) reproduction. Because leptin treatment in-
hibits feeding, leptin immunoneutralization could
produce an increase in feed intake. Greater feed
intake would permit faster growth during the grower
phase in swine and faster growth in the finishing
phase in cattle, because these animals could then eat
to gut fill. Early results from reproductive experi-
ments in rodents suggest that leptin administration
could be used to induce an early return to estrus in
lactating pigs or dairy cattle. Alternatively, it may be
useful for early induction of puberty by using trans-
genic animals to overexpress the protein.
