Abstract
Methodology used to measure in vitro gas production is reviewed to determine impacts of sources
of variation on resultant gas production proﬁles (GPP). Current methods include measurement of
gas production at constant pressure (e.g., use of gas tight syringes), a system that is inexpensive,
but may be less sensitive than others thereby affecting its suitability in some situations. Automated
systems that measure gas production at constant volume allow pressure to accumulate in the bottle,
which is recorded at different times to produce a GPP, and may result in sufﬁciently high pressure
that solubility of evolved gases in the medium is affected, thereby resulting in a recorded volume of
gas that is lower than that predicted from stoichiometric calculations. Several other methods measure
gas production at constant pressure and volume with either pressure transducers or sensors, and these
may be manual, semi-automated or fully automated in operation. In these systems, gas is released as
pressure increases, and vented gas is recorded. Agitating the medium does not consistently produce
more gas with automated systems, and little or no effect of agitation was observed with manual
systems. The apparatus affects GPP, but mathematical manipulation may enable effects of apparatus
to be removed. The amount of substrate affects the volume of gas produced, but not rate of gas
production, provided there is sufﬁcient buffering capacity in the medium. Systems that use a very
small amount of substrate are prone to experimental error in sample weighing. Effect of sample
preparation on GPP has been found to be important, but further research is required to determine the

1. Introduction
In vitro cumulative gas production techniques were developed to predict fermentation of
ruminant feedstuffs. A feedstuff is incubated with buffered rumen ﬂuid and gas produced
is measured as an indirect indicator of fermentation kinetics. When a feedstuff is incubated
with buffered rumen ﬂuid, it is ﬁrst degraded and the degraded fraction may either be
fermented to produce gas and fermentation acids, or incorporated into microbial biomass.
When combined with measures of degradation, gas production techniques provide a measure
of the proportion of feed that is fermented as opposed to that which is partitioned to microbial
growth.
The principle of determining potential rumen degradability/fermentability of a feed by
measuring gas produced from a batch culture was ﬁrst developed by McBee (1953) and
Hungate (1966). Trei et al. (1970) adapted the earlier techniques by attaching a water
displacement manometer to each vessel to measure the gas produced. Similarly, Jouany and
Thivend (1986) and Beuvink and Spoelstra (1992) used inverted measuring cylinders to
determine the volume of water displaced. Beuvink et al. (1992) then automated this water
displacement technique.
Direct displacement of a plunger by fermenting a feedstuff within a glass syringe was
developed by Czerkawski and Breckenridge (1975) and was the basis of the ‘Hohenheim
Gas Test’ later developed by Menke et al. (1979). Bl¨ummel and Ørskov (1993) modiﬁed the
technique by incubating syringes in a waterbath rather than a rotating incubator. The syringe
technique was originally developed to determine end-point fermentability of feedstuffs, at
24 h, and has been incorporated into the German national feedstuff evaluation system.
However, by recording plunger displacement at more frequent intervals, the kinetics of the
fermentation proﬁle can also be determined.

Wilkins (1974) described a different approach to measure fermentation kinetics in vitro,
whereby fermentation took place in a sealed vessel and gas produced was determined using
a pressure transducer to measure accumulation of pressure in the vessel headspace. This
principle of measuring pressure with a sensor or transducer has been widely adopted as a
simple, yet sensitive, method of determining fermentation kinetics. The simplest pressure
measurement technique requires manual measurement of headspace pressure, as described
by Theodorou et al. (1994). Semi- and full automation of headspace pressure recording
have also been developed, as described by Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993), Cone et al. (1996),
Mauricio et al. (1999a) and Davies et al. (2000).
Although the pressure transducer technique is a simple approach to estimate fermentation
kinetics of a feed, a number of factors in application of the method could potentially affect
the gas production proﬁle of any feedstuff. The objective of this review is to consider these
sources of variation and assess what impact they have on the GPP, how this variation may
be reduced, and how results produced from one laboratory, and from one experiment within
a laboratory, may be compared with, and extrapolated to, data from other laboratories.
2. Effect of venting gas during the incubation
Gas produced during the in vitro fermentation technique of Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) is
not vented, but the accumulating headspace pressure is recorded at regular time intervals.
However, in the techniques described by Theodorou et al. (1994), Cone et al. (1996) and
Davies et al. (2000), headspace gases are released at pre-determined times, or when pre-set
threshold pressures have been reached. The accumulated pressure, venting times, and/or
number of times that gas is released are recorded, and these data are used to calculate the
cumulative volume of gas produced.
Theodorou et al. (1998) suggested that the system of Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) would
produce less gas than other systems because of lack of venting, with its resultant accumu-
lation of pressure. Pell et al. (1998) argued that this was not the case, as no difference in
the volume of gas produced was observed when the headspace of the fermentation vessel
was increased by 100 ml, with a concurrent reduction in system pressure. However, Pell
et al. (1998) did acknowledge the observation of Theodorou et al. (1994), that pressures
in excess of 7 psi (48 kPa) did perturb microbial growth, but argued that this was avoided
by increasing vessel size or decreasing the amount of substrate fermented. Mertens and
Weimer (1998) modiﬁed the Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) system to allow venting, and ob-
served a small, though insigniﬁcant, increase in gas production when bottles were vented,
and attributed this response to the relatively large ratio of headspace/culture medium vol-
ume. Theodorou et al. (1998) suggested that the interface between headspace and culture
medium would affect the amount of gaseous exchange that can occur, and cited Lowenadler
and Ronner (1994) who observed that as the ratio of headspace to culture medium volume
increased, solubility of gases in the medium decreased.
In a comparison of the systems of Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) and Cone et al. (1996),
Moss et al. (1998) observed little difference in GPP of grass silages, but noted that for
maize silage and molassed sugarbeet feed, the system of Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) tended
to produce more gas, at an earlier stage, than the system of Cone et al. (1996), the opposite

of what would have been expected. These differences could have been due to differences
in apparatus, or because of differences in diets of the donor animals (Moss et al., 1998).
Results suggest that accumulation of gas in the headspace affects GPP, although perhaps
not in the way predicted. Results could also reﬂect differences in the relative surface area
of the culture at the headspace, whereby exchange of fermentation gases between medium
and headspace occurs more readily in a culture with a larger surface area.
Lowman et al. (1998), using a manual pressure transducer system, allowed headspace
pressure to vary by changing the interval between recordings of gas pressure. Although
production of short chain fatty acids , and the proportion of dry matter degraded, were not
affected by reading interval, the GPP was affected as a smaller volume of gas was released
when recordings were made every 6 rather than every 2 h. Similarly, Mauricio et al. (1999b)
reported a reduced gas volume when the recording interval was increased from 1 to 3 h,
suggesting that accumulated pressure in the fermentation vessel did not affect fermentation
stoichiometry, but that the volume of gas that could be released was reduced if the pressure
was increased. This is most likely a consequence of increased CO2 solubility with increasing
headspace pressure.
3. Effect of changes in atmospheric pressure
Pell et al. (1998) noted that in the course of in vitro incubation, atmospheric pressure
can change. It was noted that during summer thunderstorms at Cornell University in Ithaca
(NY, USA), the change in pressure could be as much as 0.4 kPa. For systems that use
pressure transducers, this could be a problem as pressure in the fermentation vessel is
measured relative to atmospheric pressure. In the system of Cone et al. (1996), the pressure
accumulation that is required to cause the valve to open is only 0.25 kPa. Therefore, during
a storm, Pell et al. (1998) suggested that valve opening could be delayed as a low-pressure
weather front passes and accelerated as atmospheric pressure rises at the end of a storm.
Theodorou et al. (1994) also observed that the volume of gas produced, with respect to
a change in atmospheric pressure, was affected by altitude, and that this needed to be
considered when comparing results between laboratories.
Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) and Pell et al. (1998) postulated that changes in atmospheric
pressure could be corrected in unvented systems by use of blanks. However, in vented
systems, this is only possible if blank fermentations produce enough gas to cause regular
valve openings, usually during the ﬁrst 6 h of fermentation (Pell et al., 1998), and these
authors advised that atmospheric pressure changes be recorded during incubation. Later,
Schoﬁeld (2000) suggested that it was possible to allow for changes in atmospheric pressure
by recording results relative to a reference pressure. However, it was concluded that most
changes in atmospheric pressure are not sufﬁcient to have much effect on GPP.
4. Effect of agitation of the medium
There is wide variation among systems in agitation of bottles during incubation For
example, the automated system of Cone et al. (1996) uses a shaking water bath which

is continually agitated, while the system of Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) uses a magnetic
stir bar that intermittently stirs the medium. The manual pressure transducer technique of
Theodorou et al. (1994), in contrast, permits shaking only when a measurement is made.
Wilkins (1974) vigorously agitated vessels at 200 oscillations/min, which was four times
faster than that of Cone et al. (1996) and more vigorous than the intermittent stirring of Pell
and Schoﬁeld (1993). Results of Wilkins (1974) showed no effect of agitation on lag time,
but he did observe that shaking increased rate of gas production. Conversely, Stevenson
et al. (1997) observed no change in the pattern of fermentation, or in yield of microbial
biomass, when the medium was agitated. However, Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) observed
that the coefﬁcient of variation of the GPP increased if the incubation medium was not
agitated.
Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) suggested that the CO2 tended to form supersaturated solu-
tions if it was not shaken or stirred. In a study of Rymer et al. (1998b), volume of gas
produced by adding propionic acid to a medium increased 58% when shaking speed was
increased from 0 to 45 oscillations/min, although there was no further increase in volume
of gas produced when shaking speed was increased to 60 oscillations/min. In a subsequent
experiment using a manual pressure transducer technique, there was no effect of agita-
tion (Rymer et al., 1998a). A comparison between laboratories in which the automated
system of Cone et al. (1996), which agitates the medium, was compared with the auto-
mated system of Davies et al. (2000), which does not, found no consistent differences
between apparatus, and data between apparatus were well correlated (Rymer et al., 2005),
suggesting that agitating the medium has relatively little effect on reproducibility of re-
sults within and between laboratories, though a consistent approach within experiment is
advisable.
5. Effect of sample size and preparation
Sch¨oner (1981) and Theodorou et al. (1994) observed that increasing the amount of
substrate resulted in a linear increase in total gas volume, but the rate of gas production was
not affected. The sample size used by different research groups ranged from 100 to 1250 mg
(Getachew et al., 1998). Small sample sizes used by some (e.g., Pell and Schoﬁeld, 1993)
may make the system more prone to experimental errors in weighing of heterogeneous
samples. However, where large sample sizes are used, it is essential that the system is
capable of buffering the acid produced, and that the accumulated pressure is not so high
that it affects the GPP.
Using cereals as substrates resulted in particle size having no effect on GPP (Trei et al.,
1970; Lowman et al., 2002), provided that the grain kernel had been cut (Lowman et al.,
2002). With highly soluble feeds, it would appear that as long as the feed has undergone
some abrasion, its particle size does not affect estimates of gas production rate. However,
there is some evidence to contradict this argument, as maize grain that was steam-ﬂaked,
rolled or left intact, had the same rate and extent of gas production when it was ground
through a 1 mm screen, but not when it had been ground through a 4 mm screen (Pell,
personal communication) Investigating the effect of processing may require a larger mesh
size to ensure that zein protein is not disrupted.

With ﬁbrous and more slowly degraded feeds, gas production rate increases as particle
size decreases (Menke and Steingass, 1988; Lowman et al., 2002) and it seems likely that
this is a consequence of increased surface area as a result of grinding, thereby allowing
better microbial access. However, this presents a problem when using the in vitro GPP to
predict kinetics of degradation and fermentation in vivo, as substrate particles are contin-
ually changing shape, size and composition in the gut (Lowman et al., 2002). Although
adoption of a standardised approach to sample preparation may be possible to enable com-
parison between independently produced gas production data of different feeds, it seems
unlikely that GPP will represent kinetics of plant biomass as it is digested in the rumen
(Lowman et al., 2002) or in the hindgut of monogastric animals, although in the case
of the latter, processes of mastication will not further disturb the physical nature of the
feed.
Menke and Steingass (1988) showed that drying silage at 60 or 105 ◦C had no ef-
fect on total gas volume produced, but it did affect GPP. However, there was no differ-
ence between the GPP of maize meal or maize residues when they were freeze dried or
dried at 60 ◦C. In contrast, Cone (1998) observed that freeze-drying enhanced gas pro-
duction of substrates compared with oven-drying. He also noted that fresh silage samples
had a different GPP compared with their dried counterparts. Sanderson et al. (1997) ob-
served that rate of fermentation of silage in vitro was grossly overestimated if a ground
and freeze-dried sample was used, rather than a fresh or chopped sample, although ef-
fects of drying were confounded by differences in particle size of samples. Davies et al.
(1998), comparing four silages, were unable to detect differences in GPP when samples
were freeze-dried and ground. However, there were differences when unprocessed sam-
ples were compared. Lowman et al. (2002) observed that GPP of freeze-dried, oven-dried
or microwave-dried ryegrass were similar, but all differed from the GPP of fresh grass,
with the fresh grass producing less gas (and SCFA) than dried samples. The estimated
apparent DM degradability of fresh grass was similar to frozen- and oven-dried grass
(Lowman et al., 2002), and they suggested that this discrepancy was due to the dried
samples being altered or damaged in such a way that microbial attachment was easier,
thereby facilitating degradation and fermentation (i.e., resulting in increased gas produc-
tion), but the attached microbial biomass resulted in apparently reduced DM loss. Data
from these sources indicate that dried samples will overestimate fermentability of feed,
and this should be considered when using GPP data to predict fermentability of a feed in
vivo.
Hydrating samples with medium before inoculation increased the volume of gas produced
and advanced the onset of a second phase of gas production (Rymer et al., 1999). The time
to half gas volume was reduced, and the fractional rate of gas production at this time was
increased as hydration time increased to 6 h. However, there was then little difference in
GPP as hydration time increased from 6 to 48 h (Rymer et al., 1999). Comparisons between
laboratories should account for differences in hydration times since, for example, the Pell
and Schoﬁeld (1993) technique hydrates samples for a shorter time than the method of,
for example, Huntington et al. (1998). Preparation of samples may, therefore, have an
effect on fermentation dynamics and the GPP. Standardised approaches within and between
laboratories, and/or developing mathematical means of correcting for these differences,
could help overcome this situation.

6. Effect of inoculum
The husbandry conditions of the donor animals, their diet, and the timing of inoculum
collection may all have an effect on consistency of results between runs. This is true even for
animals fed the same diet and living under the same conditions. It seems clear that samples
should be collected from several animals, and then combined, to reduce variation (Williams,
2000), because variability among animals, as assessed by the in situ technique, can be higher
than between day variation within animals (Mehrez and Ørskov, 1977). However, there are
other factors affecting the inoculum.
6.1. Effect of inoculum concentration and sampling time
A wide range of inoculum concentrations have been used in the various gas production
techniques. Increasing the proportion of rumen ﬂuid in the incubation medium increased
the volume of gas produced (Wood et al., 1998b), the rate of gas production (Pell and
Schoﬁeld, 1993; Rymer et al., 1999), and reduced lag time (Pell and Schoﬁeld, 1993). Rymer
et al. (1999) calculated that gas production rate was ﬁrst order with respect to inoculum
concentration, while data from other sources indicated that rates were zero order with
respect to substrate concentration (see Section 5). Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) recommended
a minimum concentration of 20 ml inoculum/100 ml incubation medium, as this reduced
chances of inoculum limiting gas production.
Jessop and Herrero (1998) described gas production as a three pool exponential model,
where the pools corresponded to fermentation of soluble material, neutral detergent ﬁbre
and microbial biomass. The model simulations were well related to other experimental
observations (Jessop and Herrero, 1998). They observed that if NDF degradation rate was
ﬁxed, but initial microbial activity was allowed to vary, there were large differences in GPP.
There were also differences if NDF degradation rate varied, but initial microbial activity
was ﬁxed. However, differences in GPP were much smaller when it was due to changes
in NDF degradation rate, compared with changes in initial microbial activity. These model
simulations demonstrate the importance of ensuring that microbial activity of inocula is
constant between experiments, and that the initial microbial activity is measured.
Nagadi et al. (1999) investigated changes in microbial activity at various rumen sampling
times. Activity was measured as bacterial DM, absorbance at 600 nm, and gas produced
by ‘blank’ fermentations. Rumen ﬂuid was sampled every day for 5 days, or at 72 h in-
tervals, and there was no difference in microbial activity when rumen ﬂuid was sampled
at 72 h intervals. However, with the daily sampling regime, microbial activity declined
and gas production proﬁles of cellulose and glucose were affected after 3 days. Nagadi et
al. (1999) recommended a minimum microbial activity of 94 mg bacterial DM/ml, which
corresponded to an absorbance of 0.111 (when diluted 50-fold) or a blank gas volume of
15.5 ml (when total incubation volume was 30 ml). This was twice the minimum microbial
activity recommended by Menke and Steingass (1988), which was 7 ml/24 h when the incu-
bation volume was also 30 ml, and higher than that used by many research groups (Nagadi
et al., 1999). Menke and Steingass (1988) suggested that a minimum acceptable microbial
activity was likely to occur in normal circumstances if the donor animals had been fed
within the previous 16 h. Cone et al. (1996) observed that rate of fermentation increased
16 C. Rymer et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 123–124 (2005) 9–30
when rumen ﬂuid was collected after the morning feed, although it had no effect on the
total gas volume produced. Payne et al. (2002) observed that the GPP of both starch and
ground straw were less variable between replicate bottles, and between weeks of collec-
tion, when rumen ﬂuid inoculum was collected either 4 or 8 h post feeding, compared with
samples collected just before, or 2 h after feeding. However, Menke and Steingass (1988)
stated that sampling rumen contents just before feeding reduced variation in composition
(and activity) of the inoculum. Although differences in microbial activity of inoculum occur
at different sampling times, it appears that the most important factor is whether the sam-
pling schedule will allow collection of inoculum with sufﬁcient microbial activity, which
can be determined by measuring absorbance of the inoculum (following a 50-fold dilu-
tion) at 600 nm (Nagadi et al., 2000). A minimum absorbance of 0.11 is required to ensure
that GPP are not affected by low microbial activity of the inoculum (Nagadi et al., 2000),
when the inoculum is rumen ﬂuid, and a ratio of rumen ﬂuid: medium of 1:2 (v/v) is used.
However, this does not account for the presence of very small particles in the inoculum.
It would seem likely that higher absorbencies would be required if smaller concentrations
of inoculum were used in the incubation mixture, and data regarding optimum absorben-
cies for other sources of inocula (e.g., caecal ﬂuid or faeces) could not be found in the
literature.
6.2. Effect of donor animal diet
Trei et al. (1970) observed that the volume of gas produced was higher when rumen ﬂuid
was collected from steers fed grain rather than hay, and grain was the substrate. However,
Mertens et al. (1998) fed four diets to lactating Holstein cows, consisting of either maize
or lucerne silage with 240 or 320 g NDF/kg DM in the diet, and found no differences in
fermentation rates (as measured by the time to half maximum gas production), although the
low ﬁbre maize silage diet produced less total gas. The higher ﬁbre diets tended to produce
more gas than the lower ﬁbre diets, but this effect was not as large as the effect of ﬁbre source.
Huntington et al. (1998) showed a similar response when dry cows were fed a diet of either
straw or grass silage with rolled barley, and no differences in the GPP of high temperature
dried grass occurred. Cone et al. (1996) also observed only small differences in GPP due to
changes in the diet of the donor animals. Menke and Steingass (1988) cited Sch¨oner’s (1981)
observation that there was little difference in GPP of treated straw when hay in the diet of
donor animals was replaced with treated straw, and stated that it was more important that a
minimum level of microbial activity was attained in the rumen ﬂuid, rather than ensuring
that donor animals were fed the feedstuffs incubated. This observation is consistent with
Nagadi et al. (2000), who fed sheep with diets varying in relative proportions of hay and
concentrate, and showed that rate and extent of gas production increased when sheep were
fed diets consisting of 400, rather than 200 g concentrate/kg diet DM, and that this was
associated with an increase in bacterial DM content of the inoculum. However, no further
increase in rate and extent of gas production occurred when the proportion of concentrate
increased to 800 g concentrate/kg diet DM. All these data support the contention that, as
noted in the discussion of sampling time, a minimum level of microbial activity is required
in the inoculum, above which no effects on GPP are evident. Diet of the donor animals
is of importance only in so far as it produces rumen ﬂuid with the required microbial
C. Rymer et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 123–124 (2005) 9–30 17
activity. Generally, this can be achieved by feeding a diet of 600 g/kg forage and 400 g/kg
concentrate (Nagadi et al., 2000), although this will be affected by the nature of the forage
and concentrate. Provided that this level of microbial activity is attained, the composition of
the donor animals’ diet appears to have little effect. However, it is also possible that when
animals are well adapted to a speciﬁc substrate, such as starch, or tannin-containing browse
trees, that there may be differences in the in vitro GPP of these substrates, compared with
proﬁles from inocula collected from animals not fed these ingredients.
6.3. Effect of donor species
Menke and Steingass (1988) suggested that the variation in the amount of gas produced
using inocula from different species of animal could be compensated for by use of blanks
and standards. Goncalves and Borba (1996) observed that rumen ﬂuid collected from sheep
yielded more gas than rumen ﬂuid from cows. In contrast, a study by Bueno et al. (1999)
compared rumen ﬂuid from either a dry cow or six wether sheep when all were fed similar
diets, and found that total gas production was similar between the two sources of inocula, as
was in vitro organic matter degradability. However, rumen ﬂuid from sheep resulted in GPP
with a longer lag time (6.1 h versus 4.2 h) when a range of tropical and temperate forages
were the substrates. Differences in microbial composition of rumen ﬂuid from these two
species appeared to affect kinetics of fermentation, but not end point measures. Cone et al.
(2002) compared rumen ﬂuid from cows and sheep fed a similar diet, and observed that the
volume of gas produced was lower with rumen ﬂuid from sheep, although there was a good
relationship between volumes of gas produced by the two inocula. However, this was not
true for estimates of the gas production rate and, as with Bueno et al. (1999), inocula from
sheep resulted in lower rates of gas production compared with inocula from cows. Calabr`o
et al. (2004) compared rumen ﬂuid from cows and buffalo fed a similar diet, and found
that while the total gas volume was generally higher with cow inoculum, maximum rate of
substrate degradation generally occurred earlier, even though actual rates did not differ. All
of these data indicate that species of donor animal will affect GPP. Appropriate use of blanks
and standards may correct for some of the differences (Menke and Steingass, 1988), and
mathematical relationships might permit extrapolation of data from one species to another
for estimation of end-point measures (Cone et al., 2002). However, rates of fermentation
based on inocula from different species are different, and may not be accounted for by
simple mathematical equations.
6.4. Alternative sources of inoculum
A considerable amount of research has investigated the use of alternatives to rumen
ﬂuid as a source of inoculum. In part, this has been to devise more appropriate inocula
for studying hindgut fermentation in monogastric species. Thus caecal ﬂuid from broilers
(Williams et al., 1997), pigs (Williams et al., 1998) and ponies (Lowman et al., 1996) has
been used in avian, porcine and equine nutrition, respectively. In ruminant nutrition, the
driving force has either been to reduce use of surgically modiﬁed animals and/or to provide
a more standardised inoculum than is possible with variable rumen ﬂuid.

6.4.1. Continuous culture (in vitro) efﬂuent
Barbi et al. (1993) investigated use of efﬂuent from a simulated rumen fermentation
(i.e., Rusitec) to replace rumen ﬂuid. The microbial activity of efﬂuent was much lower
than that observed for rumen ﬂuid, and so Barbi et al. (1994a,b) tried to increase this
activity by increasing feed input and decreasing particle size of the feed that was fed to the
Rusitec, and by infusing glucose. These interventions did not increase microbial activity, but
addition of hay to the efﬂuent ﬂask did increase the microbial concentration. van Kessel and
Russell (1996) were able to maintain a mixed culture of predominantly ruminal bacteria in
continuous culture for up to four months, but methanogens did not persist and Selenomonas
ruminantium had to be added. Variation in Rusitec efﬂuent ﬂuid between days was also
found to be similar to that in rumen ﬂuid (Barbi et al., 1995).
6.4.2. Faeces
Use of faeces as an inoculum would overcome the need for surgically modiﬁed ani-
mals, although the effect of the donor animal’s diet might still have an impact on faecal
microbial activity, although presumably this would be much less than its impact on rumen
ﬂuid. In ruminants, the faecal microbial population differs from that of rumen ﬂuid, as it
has been inﬂuenced by gastric digestion and the contributions of the caecum to the mi-
crobial population. Intuitively, the study of monogastric hindgut digestion would be easier
to simulate using monogastric faeces as a replacement for caecal digesta. Lowman et al.
(1996) observed similar results when using faeces or caecal ﬂuid from ponies. Machboeuf
et al. (1998) also observed that caecal ﬂuid and faeces from horses produced similar po-
tential gas volume from 54 forages. However, faecal inoculum resulted in faster rates
of fermentation with some forages. It was thought that this was because faecal inocu-
lum had a higher density of microﬂora compared with caecal digesta. For pigs, Bauer
et al. (2004) showed that faeces were highly representative of microbial activity of di-
gesta from the rest of the large intestine, although there were small differences with caecal
contents.
Endpoint measures, such as gas production or digestibility after 24 h or more, from
faecal inocula are well related to estimates using rumen ﬂuid inocula (Akhter et al., 1994,
1995; Harris et al., 1995; Altaf et al., 1998; Cone et al., 2002; Zhao and Chen, 2004), or
estimates of in vivo digestibility in horses (Lowman et al., 1999). However, the rate of
gas production is usually lower with faeces compared with rumen ﬂuid (Mauricio et al.,
1998a,b, 2001; Cone et al., 2002), making estimations of dynamic parameters of the GPP
difﬁcult. If the diet of the donor animal is highly ﬁbrous, such that the microbial activity
of the rumen is low, then differences between rumen ﬂuid and faeces are much smaller
(Mauricio et al., 1999c), but when characterising feeds that will be fed to highly productive
animals, such inocula are of limited value. These data show that microbial activity of faecal
inocula is generally much lower than that of rumen ﬂuid inocula, and apart from the drastic
changes to the diet as in the work of Mauricio et al. (1999c), it is not clear how activity of
faecal inocula might be increased. However, a mathematical means of adjusting GPP from
faecal inocula to those produced by rumen ﬂuid inocula has been proposed (Dhanoa et al.,
2004). While this approach needs development, it does offer a means whereby dynamics of
feed fermentation in ruminants might be estimated without relying on surgically modiﬁed
animals.

6.4.3. Cell-free enzymes
Use of a cocktail of cell-free enzymes could overcome the need to maintain experimental
animals, and provide a more consistent inoculum source. To date, relatively little has been
done to develop means of predicting fermentation dynamics (Broderick, 1998), and it could
be that for this approach to be viable, at least in the context of the gas production technique,
that the enzymes used would need to be those associated with fermentation, as well as
degradation, pathways as any gas produced would be indirect rather than a combination of
direct and indirect. It is not known whether or not this is feasible.
6.4.4. Bacterial cultures
Bacterial cultures could, in theory, be used to produce standard inoculum without the
need for experimental animals, provided such a culture could be developed with a sufﬁ-
ciently wide range of activity, and that conditions could be maintained to reduce variation in
its activity. Luchini et al. (1996) compared freeze-dried and frozen rumen bacteria with fresh
cultures, and observed initial differences in fermentation pattern, although there were no
differences at later times. Rates of protein degradation were similar for fresh and frozen bac-
teria, but much lower for freeze-dried bacteria. They concluded that if frozen bacteria were
incubated overnight, they could be used as an alternative inoculum, but that freeze-dried
bacteria were not a viable option. Rymer et al. (2000) investigated use of a bacterial culture
made from mixing pure cultures of Ruminococcus ﬂavefaciens, Fibrobacter succinogenes,
Ruminococcus albus, Selenomonas ruminantium, Butyrivibrio ﬁbrisolvens, Prevoletta ru-
minicola and Streptococcus bovis, and compared GPP of several feeds produced by use of
this inoculum with GPP produced by rumen ﬂuid inoculum. Although there were differ-
ences in the shape of the GPP, with the bacterial culture producing gas at a much lower
rate and in two distinct phases, there were good mathematical relationships between GPP
parameters of the inocula. Much would need to be done, and it seems likely that many more
bacterial species would need to be included in the mixture, before such an approach could
be used to replace rumen ﬂuid. However, it could produce an inoculum that would eliminate
the need for surgically modiﬁed animals, and it would be a deﬁned inoculum, in contrast to
rumen ﬂuid or faeces, with its speciﬁcity and activity adjustable by addition or removal of
any bacterial species.
6.5. Effect of inoculum preparation
Pell and Schoﬁeld (1993) and Rymer et al. (1999) observed no differences in GPP
when inocula were strained or blended to include attached bacteria. However, Mertens
and Weimer (1998) observed that mixing rumen solids with chilled medium, and then
blending the mixture, resulted in slightly decreased lag times and increased rates of gas
production. Senshu et al. (1980) repeatedly squeezed and washed rumen contents under
CO2 to incorporate adherent bacteria in the inoculum. This resulted in the SCFA proﬁle in
vitro becoming more similar to that in vivo. Clearly there is a danger that, particularly with
ﬁbrous feedstuffs, not blending the inoculum will exclude adherent, cellulolytic bacteria
from the inoculum, and this may adversely affect the GPP. However, Pell and Schoﬁeld
(1993) suggested that blending inoculum potentially exposes bacteria to O2, and results in
small amounts of undegraded feed in the inoculum being added to the incubation medium.

Since the evidence suggests that blending inoculum has only a limited, if any, effect on
GPP, it seems that simply straining inoculum provides adequate preparation.
7. Use of blanks
Fermentation vessels containing identical components to all other vessels, with exclu-
sion of any substrate, are termed ‘blank’ incubations and are routinely used during gas
production runs. Blanks can be used to correct for changes in atmospheric pressure (Pell
and Schoﬁeld, 1993; Cone et al., 1996), as discussed earlier, as well as to correct for residual
fermentable OM that is included with the inoculum. However, Cone (1998) noted that blank
incubations do not produce gas at the same rate as the sample because the blanks begin mi-
crobial turnover sooner. Calculated dynamics of fermentation are therefore incorrect if the
cumulative volume of gas from the blank is simply deducted from the cumulative volume
of gas from the sample at each time interval. Williams (2000) suggested that blank results
should always be reported in tables of results, but not subtracted from sample results. Blank
incubations also provide conﬁrmation of activity of the initial inoculum and, for end point
measures, it is a useful correction for contribution of OM in the inoculum.
8. Effect of medium composition
There is considerable variation in the composition of the medium used between gas
production techniques. A distinction should be made between ‘medium’ (i.e., a solution
containing a number of components including buffering agents, trace elements, true protein
and reducing agents) and the ‘buffer’ that is a component of the medium (Williams, 1998).
The bicarbonate component of the buffer complicates interpretation of the GPP, because
of indirect gas that is produced by the reaction between bicarbonate ions and fermenta-
tion acids. However, it is an important component of the rumen buffering system, and is
usually included in all media to simulate rumen conditions more closely. However, Omed
et al. (1998) suggested that phosphate could completely replace bicarbonate in the buffer,
although microorganisms require both bicarbonate and phosphate for growth (Williams,
1998). Phosphates also increase ﬁbre digestibility to some extent (Kennedy et al., 2000)
and so phosphate buffers might also alter the GPP.
Rymer et al. (1998b) examined the composition of a variety of media used in gas produc-
tion techniques, with the objective of determining effects of various media compositions on
the volume of indirect gas produced. The media of Goering and van Soest (1970), Theodorou
(1993), Steingass (1983) and Huntington et al. (1998) were compared (Table 1). Aliquots

been added, and the volume of gas produced was measured. Although the Goering and van

was very small (14.8 ml/mmol acid compared with 13.7, 14.1 and 13.7 ml/mmol acid for
the Theodorou (1993), Steingass (1983) and Huntington et al. (1998) media, respectively).
There was no difference between media in initial pH, but there were large differences in the

8.1. Effect of N in medium
The original medium developed by Theodorou et al. (1994) was designed to study sub-
strates as energy sources, and so all other components, including N, were present in excess
(Williams, 1998). Casein or trypticase may be added as a source of amino acids and peptides,
and Debersaques et al., (1998) showed that addition of a source of peptides was essential for
in vitro fermentation of citrus pulp, a comparatively pure carbohydrate. However, they may
also contribute to gas production if the microbes use it as an energy source (Williams, 1998).
If a gas production technique is used to characterise carbohydrate sources, it is important
that sufﬁcient N is present. If carbohydrate is in excess, relative to N, then microorganisms
will ‘spill’ energy (Russell and Strobel, 1993), because an excess of ATP appears to be
toxic to microbes, and so they have to adopt strategies that yield less ATP from fermenta-
tion of carbohydrates (Russell, 1998). This results in different fermentation pathways being
adopted that yield lactate and propionate rather than acetate. Adding amino N to the culture
will alter fermentation stoichiometry, as the microorganisms are able to grow and do not
need to spill the energy provided by the fermentation of carbohydrates (van Kessel and
Russell, 1995).
22 C. Rymer et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 123–124 (2005) 9–30
Dryhurst and Wood (1998) observed that the incubation medium, including the contri-
bution of N from the feed, should contain at least 80 mg N/l, if the rate of carbohydrate
degradation is to be maximised. This is more than the 50 mg N/l recommended by Satter
and Slyter (1974) determined with continuous culture fermenters but, as noted by Dryhurst
and Wood (1998), the requirement for N is dependant on the supply of carbohydrate. When
more carbohydrate was supplied, particularly if it is rapidly fermentable, then more N is re-
quired to meet bacterial growth requirements. Sufﬁcient N should be added to the medium
to ensure that carbohydrate from the substrate is not in excess, so that the fermentation
pathway adopted is a consequence of the feed, and not the result of an N deﬁcient medium.
However, if the feed is being evaluated for its N sufﬁciency, then it is appropriate to
compare fermentation of the substrate in N-rich and N-poor media. Wood et al. (1998a)
incubated wheat straw (an N-poor substrate) in both N-rich and N-free media. Differences in
gas production were considered to be an indication of N availability of the substrate. When
Romney et al. (1998), used this technique, differences in gas production were negatively
related to the N content of the feed, particularly as incubation time increased. This technique
could also be used to study interactions between substrates. For example, adding dried
grass or dried lucerne as a supplement to straw, in the studies of Wood et al. (1998a),
increased total gas from the N-free medium, but had little effect with the N-rich medium. It
appeared that the amount of supplementation required was at least 50% (w/w) before gas
production was no longer limited. In a similar study by Rosales et al. (1998), interactions
between tropical fodder trees were observed in some instances when an N-free medium
was used, but not when an N-rich medium was used. It may be that this was because
fodder trees with a rich supply of N were able to stimulate fermentation when an N-free
medium was used, but this effect was masked when ample N was available from the medium.
However, the reverse was also true, in that some fermentations were stimulated with an N-
rich media but not with an N-free media. Other nutrients, present in one of the fodder trees
must have been stimulating fermentation in these cases, but the ﬁrst limiting nutrient was
not N.
9. Effect of apparatus
Rymer and Givens (1997) compared three types of apparatus using the same methodol-
ogy. High temperature dried grass, soybean meal and straw were the substrates in an auto-
mated pressure transducer (Cone et al., 1996), a manual pressure transducer (Theodorou et
al., 1994) and ﬂexible plastic bottles. In general, the automated pressure transducer produced
more gas than the manual methods, but the combined fractional rates of gas production were
similar for the automated technique and the ﬂexible plastic bottles, but lower for the manual
pressure transducer. In contrast, a comparison of the manual pressure transducer (Theodorou
et al., 1994) with the automated pressure transducers of Cone et al. (1996) and Davies et
al. (2000) indicated that the manual pressure transducer produced more gas, but at a slower
rate, than automated transducers (Rymer et al., 2005). Despite large differences between
apparatus used, and interactions of the laboratories involved, there were good mathematical
relationships between GPP from the different apparatus, suggesting that GPP produced by
one apparatus can be corrected to standardised apparatus.

11. Conclusions
In vitro gas production techniques have been developed in a number of laboratories,
and this has resulted in a range of methods and apparatus to record GPP. Of the studies
examined in this review, there appear to be a number of factors that are of lesser importance
in terms of their effect on GPP. These include whether or not the medium is agitated during
fermentation (although in the case of small quantities of substrate, it may have an effect
on the coefﬁcient of variation between replicates), and the diet fed to the inoculum donor
(as long as a speciﬁed minimum microbial activity is reached). The apparatus that have
been developed to measure gas production affect GPP obtained, but there are few reports
of comparisons between apparatus. However, the evidence suggests that, while apparatus
does have an effect on GPP, it should be possible to develop mathematical corrections to
allow comparisons between GPP produced by different apparatus.
In contrast, there are factors that have a substantial inﬂuence on GPP, and care must
be taken to ensure that these are consistent, certainly between runs within laboratory, and
preferably between laboratories. For example, it is essential that buffering capacity of the
medium is appropriate to the amount of substrate used. It is also important that enough
substrate is used to minimize experimental error associated with weighing. The actual
fermentation vessels should not allow gas pressure to accumulate beyond 48 kPa, as this
affects microbial growth and causes development of a super-saturated solution, which results
in a reduced amount of gas production being measured.
An ideal system of substrate preparation requires further investigation, since ground
and dried materials are not what reach the rumen in vivo. While particle size of readily
degradable feeds appears to have only a limited effect on GPP, optimum particle size of
forage samples should be further investigated to ensure that rates of fermentation in vitro
relate to rates of digestion in vivo. Similarly, fresh forages should be used where possible, as
drying exerts large effects on GPP of forages. Physical and chemical factors that enhance,
or inhibit, digestion of the feed in vivo should also be present in the sample that is incubated
in vitro.
The largest source of variation in the gas production technique is the source of inoculum,
and the activity of the microbial inoculum can have a larger effect on GPP than substrate.
Inocula samples should be collected and pooled from more than one animal, and the diet and
sampling schedule should be designed to minimise variation between runs (and maximise
microbial activity of the inocula). The microbial activity of the inoculum should be deter-
mined before inoculation begins, and this is possible with rumen ﬂuid inocula by measuring
C. Rymer et al. / Animal Feed Science and Technology 123–124 (2005) 9–30 25
absorbance at 600 nm after 50-fold dilution. Future research should focus on developing in-
ocula that minimise requirements for surgically modiﬁed animals, and this may involve use
of mathematical manipulations to ‘shift’ GPP produced by one inoculum to that produced
by an alternative ‘standard’ inoculum.
It may also be necessary to re-examine the objectives of gas production techniques.
Should the objective be to simulate the animal, or should substrate fermentability in vitro be
considered as a characteristic per se? If it is the former, then a lot of time is required to further
alter fermentation conditions, while knowing that they will always suffer the limitations of
a batch culture system, given that animals more closely resemble a continuous culture.
However, if it is the latter, the aim should be to standardize the methods that already exist,
and accept that the maximum fermentability of a substrate in vitro may not emulate what
occurs in animals, but that it is a useful measure to assist in ration formulation. Research
could then focus on relating standardised GPP for individual feeds to in vivo measures of
importance, making the GPP useful in animal nutrition.
