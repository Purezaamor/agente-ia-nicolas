ABSTRACT 
Somatotropin (S") and &adrenergic agonists @-agonists) have afforded scientists a 
significant opportunity to alter nutrient metabolism and, thereby, the partition of absorbed 
nutrients for lean tissue growth. The potential is well illustrated with growing swine treated 
with porcine ST during the 50 to 100 kg phase of growth. Relative changes and levels 
achieved for both protein and lipid deposition are unprecedented. Data presented herein 
also demonstrate that the biological response to bovine ST in growing cattle may have 
previously been constrained by inadequate provision of indispensable amino acids. The 
effects of both ST and &agonists on protein (amino acids) and energy utilization are 
primarily postabsorptive. Somatotropin improved the partition of absorbed amino acids to 
protein deposition in growing pigs, cattle, and sheep fed conventional diets by as much as 
20 to 40%. Accordingly, the opportunity exists to minimize a significant inefficiency in 
nutrient expenditure. Whether an increase in protein intake is required to accommodate the 
ST-stimulated increase depends on the partial efficiency of absorbed amino acid use for 
protein deposition. The dynamic natures of these and other components were considered 
using simple and complex models to estimate how selected indispensable amino acid and 
nitrogen needs would be altered for growing swine and cattle. The impact of J3-agonists on 
the partial efficiency of amino acid use for protein deposition remains unclear because the 
data are not sufficiently quantitative. Of special interest, and in contrast to ST, is the 
differential muscle growth that characterizes the response to &agonists. This suggests that 
protein input for muscle growth may be less because of conserved visceral growth and that 
the pattern of dietary amino acids needed to accOmmOdate the tissue requirement for 
protein deposition in pigs treated with &agonists may differ from that in pigs fed 
conventimal diets or treated with ST. Neither metabolism modifier improves the efficiency 
of energy use. In fact, a subtle decrease may exist because the protein component of energy 
gah is inherently inefficient compared with lipid. This would increase maintenance energy 
expenditure. Thus, any opportunity for improvement in energetic efficiency may exist only 
to the extent that the daily maintenance requirement is diluted via more rapid growth.

whether nutrient requirements will be quantita-

observed for the rate of mineral (ash) accretion

endogenous secretion of ST is not adequate to

The relationship between protein (amino 
acid) input and tissue deposition is illustrated 
in Figure 1A. Increases in dietary protein result 
in corresponding increases in protein accretion 
to an apparent maximum. The slope of the 
linear response is determined by the digestibil- 
ity of the dietary protein, the extent to which 
the pattern of absorbed amino acids cor- 
responds with the tissue requirement, and the 
proportion of absorbed amino acids that are 
utilized for protein deposition. A protein 
source that is proportionately low in one or 
more amino acids (relative to the tissue 
requirement) must be supplied in greater 
amounts to achieve the same rate of protein 
accretion. Differences in the ability of two 
dietary proteins to provide adequate quantities 
of each indispensable amino acid are illus- 
trated by the slope of respective lines (measure 
of overall utilization efficiency). Thus, it is 
inappropriate to express tissue needs for amino 
acids in terms of a dietary protein requirement 
unless the dietary proteins have an ideal 
pattern of amino acids. 
The mminant presents some special chal- 
lenges in meeting the tissue amino acid 
requirement because of ruminal fermentation 
and the dynamic nature of protein metabolism 
in the rumen. As a result, the actual amino acid 
supply (from ruminal escape protein and 
microbial protein) cannot be predicted with a 
high degree of certainty, and amino acid 
requirements have not been defined (Buttery 
and Foulds, 1985; NRC, 1985). Comparison of 
the amino acid pattern of microbial protein 
with that of body tissue suggests that it may 
have a high biological value (shown later), but 
microbial protein provides only 50% to 70% of 
the requirement (NRC, 1985). Accommodating 
residual needs for essential amino acids re- 
quires that escape protein be sufficient in 
quantity and have an amino acid pattern 
commensurate with tissue needs (i.e., quality). 
This is particularly difficult to determine and 
may likewise be difficult to accommodate in 
practice. We propose that the less dramatic 
response to ST of growing ruminants com- 
pared with that of swine is due, at least in part, 
to inadequate &mino acid nutrition. 
Protein accretion may also be constrained 
by inadequate energy intake (Figure 1B). The 
interrelationship between energy intake and 
protein accretion is also best illustrated with 
swine (Campbell, 1988). For example, if pigs 
are fed increasing amounts of protein, in 
conjunction with a restricted amount of energy 
(El), protein deposition increases to an appar- 
ent maximum. Additional increments of pro- 
tein will not result in a further increase. 
However, when the energy constraint is re- 
moved &), deposition increases to a new 
maximum allowable by protein (amino acid) 
intake. One cannot determine whether the 
expressed level of protein accretion is limited 
by energy intake without a plot of the 
relationship between the two variables over a 
range of intakes to ad libitum. 
Thus, constraint of protein accretion is 
common to both amino acid and energy 
inadequacy, but the mechanisms differ. An 
energy limit to protein deposition would 
generally not occur with pigs given ad libitum 
access to feed (castrate male, female) that 
weigh more than 50 kg, because energy intake 
(approximately four times maintenance) gener- 
ally exceeds that required for maximum 
protein accretion (I three times maintenance). 
This is not the case for pigs weighing less than 
50 kg, and perhaps also not true for genetically 
elite, intact males for which ad libitum intake 
(approximately four times maintenance) is 
inadequate (Campbell, 1988). The apparent 
disparity between pigs in the two phases of 
growth may be due to the fact that the latter 
have a much higher rate of protein accretion 
when expressed per unit of body size (Carr et 
al., 1977). Growing ruminants, on the other 
hand, have inherently low energy intakes (El) 
(e.g., I 2.5 times maintenance) that may be 
marginal and may even be limiting to protein 
accretion under some circumstances.

Treatment of growing pigs with pST has 
been shown to alter the maintenance require- 
ment for both energy and protein (NRC, 
1991b). The higher maintenance energy re- 
quirement for pST-treated pigs (Campbell et

vs castrate males; lean genotypes; Dickerson,

er, approximately 10 to 12% of the increase in

accounted for by the gain in lean body mass 
(Noblet, 1990; Verstegen et al., 1990). This 
small difference may be related to changes in 
metabolic activity of various organs, including 
the liver (Noblet, personal communication).

or amino acids are utilized for protein deposi- 
tion is best illustrated with rapidly growing 
broilers (Baker, 1991) and young growing pigs 
(Wang and Fuller, 1989; Moughan and Smith, 
1984). Estimates in the range of .80 to .94 
have been achieved, which suggests that the 
partition of amino acids to protein deposition 
is a physiological priority. The extent to which 
this varies with age has not been adequately 
documented, but Van Weerden et al. (1981) 
cited data suggesting a decline with age. 
The prevailing belief has been that a partial 
efficiency of .65 is appropriate for pigs fed 
conventional diets (Whittemore, 1983). How- 
ever, this review reemphasizes the low effi- 
ciency with which absorbed amino acids are 
utilized for protein accretion in pigs fed 
accordingly (Rerat, 1972) and points out that 
considerable disparity exists (.39 to .62) over 
the 20 to 100 kg phase of growth. Thus, a 
glaring inefficiency in amino acid use seems to 
exist in commercial practice. The biological 
and nutritional basis for this is not entirely 
clear, but the impact on productive efficiency 
is enormous. 
Achieving or closely approximating the 
potential for amino acid partition for protein 
deposition in the nonruminant seems to require 
provision of a highly digestible protein source 
with a nearly ideal balance of amino acids. The 
high biological values cited for growing swine 
were achieved with intact and partially hydro- 
lyzed casein. We anticipate that the "simul-

Metabolism modifiers provide a potentially

The effects of ST (and Bagonists) on the
