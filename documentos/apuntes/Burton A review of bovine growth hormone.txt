Can. J. Anim. Sci. 74: 16'7-201. Unprecedented numbers of technical papers, abstracts, and short communications have been
published in the past decade regarding the effects of exogenous bovine growth hormone on milk production, health, and reproductive
efficiency of treated dairy cows. In well-managed dairy herds, exogenous growth hormone increases milk production without
altering normal variability in milk composition. This has held true regardless of dairy breed tested, geographical location studied,
or feeding management system used. Also consistent across studies is the rapidity of the galactopoietic effect of administered
bovine growth hormone, which arises from altered partitioning and use of post-absorptive nutrients and increased synthetic capacity
of the mammary gland. Growth hormone and its associated peptide, insulin-like growth factor-I, are now known to provide chronic
lipolytic, diabetogenic, and gluconeogenic signals to target tissues culminating in increased mammary gland availability of glucose
and nonesterified fatty acids. Together with yet ill-defined effects on mammary secretory tissue, this homeorhetic control of
metabolism elicited by exogenous growth hormone is so efficient that treated cows are not more susceptible to metabolic disorders
than untreated cows. However, some studies have reported an increased frequency of mastitis in groups of treated cows. This
has been attributed mainly to increased milk volume in the mammary glands of treated cows and no convincing data are available
that show decreased mammary gland immunity as a result of growth hormone treatments. On the contrary, an expanding body
of evidence implicates growth hormone as a key neuroendocrine factor that is required for immunological competence. Trends
of decreased reproductive efficiency in cows treated with growth hormone have also been reported, but available data imply
that this is probably an indirect effect via prolonged negative energy balance in cows treated in early lactation rather than a direct
negative effect on estrous cycling via altered reproductive hormone profiles. The objectives of the present review are to bring
into focus and summarize pertinent biological discoveries regarding the treatment of dairy cows with recombinant bovine growth
hormone, and to explore areas where additional growth hormone research is needed or warranted.

Growth hormone (GH) belongs to a family of somatolacto-
genic hormones that have classically included prolactin and
placental lactogen and more recently have been expanded
to include a number of the haematopoietic growth factors
(reviewed by Cosman et al. 1990; Arkins et al. 1993;
De Vos et al. 1993). The members of this protein family are
considered related primarily because their receptors have
similar structures and signal transduction pathways (Cosman
et al. 1990; De Vos et al. 1993). However, despite their
tissue-specific synthesis, the hormones themselves overlap
in a number of evolutionary, structural, immunological, and
biological properties, each having varying degrees oflacto-
genic, somatotropic, metabolic, and immunomodulatory
effects across marnmalian species (reviewed by Miller and
Eberhardt 1983; Bauman and McCutcheon 1986; Nicoll
et al. 1986; Berczi and Nagy 1987, l99l; Kelley 1989;
Arkins et al. 1993). Therefore, although the objectives and
focus of the present review are to discuss the biology and
consequences of increasing miik production by treating dairy
cows with exogenous bovine GH, it must be recognized that
other members of the somatolactogenic hormone family
would contribute to most of the processes described.
It has been known for the past five decades that exogenous
bovine GH is galactopoietic when injected into lactating dairy
cows. Only with the advent and sophistication of recombinant
DNA techniques in the 1980s, however, were sufficient quan-
tities of recombinant bovine GH (rbGH) made available able
to support in-depth study ofits galactopoietic effects. During
that decade and into the 1990s, rbGH research was the focus
of many industry, university, and government laboratories
and produced unprecedented volumes of literature. At the
same time, mass media coverage of this first product of
biotechnology for animal agriculture created controversy and
even animosity between administrators, scientists, dairy
farmers, and consumers. In the end, however, we have
learned some fascinating facts about lactation physiology as
a direct result of experimentation with this hormone. Of
potential practical importance for future commercial appli-
cation of rbGH, recent research on disease resistance and
aging suggests that growth hormone may be fundamentally
involved in the maintenance of animal health.
The present review consists of three major sections, namely
production performance of dairy cows treated with rbGH,
mechanism of growth hormone action, and practical
implications of treating dairy cows with rbGH. Discussions
in the first section are based predominantly on those of other
comprehensive reviews of the literature, because exhaustive
research data from multitudes of single and multiple rbGH
trials reach the clear consensus that rbGH is galactopoietic,
improves the biological efficienry of milk production, and does
not jeopardize milk quality or processing characteristics. In
the second section, scientific evidence in combination with
some speculation lends support to the hypothesis that chronic
lipolysis and diabetogenesis are inherent properties of the
rbGH molecule. These properties allow rbGH to orchestrate
homeorhetic control over the intermediary metabolism of
glucose, fat, and protein and, in combination with direct and
indirect effects on marnmary secretory tissue, encourage
increased synthesis and secretion of milk by the mammary
gland. The third section is broken down into four subsections
that deal with the practical implications of rbGH administration
to dairy cows. Subsections include effects of rbGH on
reproductive efficiency and metabolic disorders, occurrence
of infectious diseases in rbGH-treated cows, rbGH and the
immune system, and the safety of milk from rbGH-treated
cows. The third section is particularly important because it
underlines the main issues which concern dairy furmers and
consumers, and provides direction for exciting new avenues
of research concerning growth hormone and animal physiology.
I. PRODUCTION PERFORMANCE OF DAIRY COWS
TREATED WITH GROWTH HORMONE
The galactopoietic effect of exogenous bovine GH for dairy
cows was first discovered more than 50 yr ago, when Russian
scientists demonstrated that injections ofcrude extracts from
the pituitary gland could increase milk yield (Asimov and
Krouze 1937). These frndings were confirmed by researchers
in the United Kingdom (Folley and Young 1940), who also
showed that the galactopoietic effect was particularly dramatic
during declining lactation and that diminishing returns in milk
yields were obtained beyond a certain dose of the injected
extract (Folley and Young 1945). Shortly after, the galacto-
poietic component of the extracts was identihed as anterior
pituitary GH (Young 1947; Cotes et aL. 1949). This prompted
British scientists to attempt mass-purification of pituitary
bovine GH in hopes of alleviating food shortages during World
War II. However, these attempts fell short of their expecta-
tions because the extraction process was expensive and it took
pituitaries from approximately 200 slaughter cows to obtain
enough GH to treat one cow for 1 day. Even so, enough GH
could be purified to support limited basic, short-term research
using exogenous bovine GH (Brumby and Hancock 1955;
Cotes et aL. 1949|' Johnsson and Hart 1986; Hutton 1957;
Machlin 1973).
The earliest hypothesis regarding the mechanism of action
of bovine GH was that it probably enhanced marnmary
alveolarlobular development (Asimov and Krouze 1937). By
1952 Hutton postulated that GH also increased the availability

of GH action was that of acute lipolysis of adipose tissue
because short-term administration of the hormone caused
acute elevations of free fatty acids in blood of treated cows
(reviewed by Bauman and McCutcheon 1986; Johnsson and

In the late 1970s, however, Bines, Hart, and colleagues
in England (reviewed by Bines et al. 1980; Bines and Hart
1982;Hart et al. 1980; Hart 1983; Johnsson and Hart 1986),
and Bauman and colleagues in the USA (Bauman and Currie
1980; reviewed by Bauman and McCutcheon 1986; Bauman
and Vernon 1993) challenged the accepted dogma sur-
rounding the galactopoietic action of GH. Having observed
the physiology of GH in lactating cows of diverse genetic
merit and varying lactational stage and nutritional status, they
proposed that GH exerted a more chronic (homeorhetic)
regulation of metabolism by redirecting the partitioning and
use of absorbed nutrients in favour of milk production. This
theory was particularly relevant for high producing cows and
numerous studies that followed verihed that injected bovine
GH could increase milk yields by 6-35% in such animals
(Machlin 1973; Peel et al. 1983; Richard et al. 1985).
The means to test the new hypothesis of chronic regulation
of nutrient partitioning by bovine GH was finally made pos-
sible in the early 1980s when break-through biotechnology
allowed the production of large supplies of purified bovine
GH from recombinant DNA technology. The ideas for this
technology were first generated in the 1960s when scientists
began to understand that microbial cells could be utilized to
mass-produce protein. Experiments were then performed in
which a specific segment of mammalian DNA (a gene) was
spliced into bacterial DNA, resulting in bacterial production
of the mammalian protein encoded for by the inserted gene.
The gene for bovine GH was so cloned irrto Escherichia coli
in the late 1970s and mass production of recombinant bovine
GH (rbGH) became a reality by the early 1980s.
By most immunological, receptor-binding, bioassay, and
biopotency criteria, pituitary- and recombinant DNA-derived
sources of bovine GH were originally shown to be similar
or identical (Hart et al. 1984; Wingfield et al. 1987).
Research published in 1984 by Hart et al. gave further
support to the nutrient partitioning theory of Bines, Hart,
Bauman and colleagues by demonstrating that chronic
diabetogenesis and lipolysis were inherent properties ofthe
rbGH molecule when administered in vivo. The first report
of dramatically increased milk yields from high-producing
dairy cows injected with rbGH was published in abstract form
in 1982 by Bauman et al. and, since then, the volume and
scope of rbGH research has increased exponentially. The
chronic galactopoietic properties of rbGH have now been
shown to span altered tissue sensitivities to acutely acting
hormones, diabetogenesis, nitrogen retention by muscle,
gluconeogenesis by the liver, lipogenesis and lipolysis by
adipose tissue, increased cardiac output and blood flow to

One major difference has been detected between pituitary
and recombinant DNA sources of bovine GH. Within batch
produced, rbGH molecules are totally size-homogeneous
while those from pituitary extracts are highly size-hetero-
geneous (e.g., Hart et al. 1984; Wingfield et al. 1987). This
suggests that several variants of bovine GH exist in vivo,
each which could differ with regard to physiology. The first
long-term study which compared simulataneously the galac-
topoietic effects of pituitary GH versus rbGH (Bauman et al.
1985) demonstrated lower increases in the milk yield
responses for the former than for the latter product. A recent
study by Toutain et al. (1993) has shown further that
numerous dose-dependent pharmacokinetic paramaters differ
significantly between the two forms of bovine GH even
though time-dependent variables are similar. These differ-
ences must also exist between the various rbGH products
from different manufacturers because each has a slightly
different amino acid composition (reviewed by Hart et al.
1984; Wingfield et al. 1987; Bauman and Vernon 1993).
Although the basis and consequences of the size variations
of pituitary GH are not known for certain, some insight into
the tremendous biological versatility of this hormone has been
gained by recent studies that have employed modern bio-
chemical and molecular techniques to examine the GH
molecule. For example, site-directed mutagenesis combined
with molecular probing using monoclonal antibodies has
demonstrated that different segments of the bovine GH
molecule bind to different receptor types present in a variety
of target cells (Strasburger et al. 1989). Moreover, a novel
molecular configurati<ln of ligand-receptor binding has now
been visualized through X-ray crystallographic analysis of the
human GH-receptor complex (De Vos et al. 1993). Structure-
function diversity for bovine GH has also been implied by
studies which have detected point mutations in the bovine GH
molecule (Lucy et al. 1993). Molecular genetic studies have
confirmed that considerable polymorphism exists in the gene
that encodes bovine GI{ (Cowan et al. 1989; Theilmann et al.
1989; Kirkpatrick1992 Rocha et al. 1992;Zhanget al. 1993)
and studies designed to determine GH genotype-production
associations have been initiated (Hilbert et al. 1989; Hoj et al.
1993; Lucy et al. 1993). The possibility that genetic variation
has contributed to contradictory results across some experi-
ments employing rbGH from different manufacturers must
be acknowledged. Also, it is likely that GH receptor hetero-
geneity exists and that this source ofvariation contributes to
tissue-specific and animal-to-animal variations in activities
of a single rbGH preparation.

At present there is no biochemical means of identifying
rbGH-treated cows because the immunochemical techniques
used during the assay of blood GH cannot distinguish between
the recombinant and endogenous forms of the hormone. It
would be ideal in terms of milk recording and genetic selec-
tion programs if a biochemical tag could be integrated into
the rbGH molecule for positive identification of treated cows.
However, current rates of genetic progress in milk yield are
not expected to be substantially altered by the commercial
application of presently-available forms of rbGH because
research has shown that bull and dam rankings will not
change dramatically (reviewed by Gravert 1989). In terms
of response to rbGH by individual cows, no interaction
between an animal's genotype and responsiveness has been
reported (Gravert 1989). On the other hand, the adequacy
of farm management programs, including herd health,
milking practice, environmental cleanliness, and nutritional

1991; Bauman 7992; Bauman et al. 7994).
Effects of Growth Hormone on Feed Intake
Voluntary feed intake does not increase until several weeks
after increases in milk yield in rbGH-treated cows but,
providing body condition is adequate, persists throughout the
treatment period once initiated (reviewed by McBride et al.
1988; Bauman et al. 1994). This is true even when a wide
variety of diets and managerial conditions are employed
(Chalupa and Galligan 1989; Chilliard 1989; Bauman 1992;
Bauman et al. 7994). The magnitude of the increase in feed
intake is dependent upon the increase in milk yield, the degree
of body condition change, and the nutrient density of the diet
(Chilliard 1989). Body fat stores are mobilized during intervals
of negative energy balance in high-producing and rbGH-treated
cows and, therefore, body condition of these animals is altered
depending on energy balance throughout lactation. The nuffient
partitioning response to rbGH treatrnent that supports increased
milk yield particularly concerns preferential oxidation of fatty
acids and sparing ofglucose by peripheral tissues (Section II;
McBride et al. 1988; Bauman et al. 1989b; De Boer and
Kennelly 1989a,b; Gallo 1989; Vernon 1989; Gallo and Block
1990b; Bauman and Vernon 1993), and increased substrate
utilization by the marnmary gland may in turn provide a
stimulus for increasins feed intake.

Effects of Growth Hormone on Body Condition
The effect of rbGH on body condition is related directly to
the milk production response elicited by the hormone
(Bauman et al. 1985; Eppard et al. 198'7; Bauman and
Vernon 1993). When rbGH is administered and the response
in milk energy output exceeds feed energy intake, the nega-
tive energy balance results in body condition loss (Soderholm
et al. 1988). This loss does not generally exceed that
predicted from NRC'tables for milk production and, there-
fore, the loss of body condition score is usually consistent
with gains in milk yield when rbGH is administered for the
entire lactation @auman et al. 1994). The period of minimum
body condition for rbGH-treated cows is longer than for con-
trol cows, which have lower milk yields (L6onard et al.
1990). Once cows return to positive energy balance, body
condition is regained to the extent that original condition is
restored prior to peak production in the subsequent lactation
(Soderholm et al. 1988; Gallo and Block 1990b; L6onard
et al. 1990). Increased feed intake relative to controls in mid-
and late treatment periods @urton et al. 1990a), and probably
during the dry period and pretreatment period of the subse-
quent lactation (McBride et al. 1990), must contribute to the
regain of body condition in rbGH-treated cows.
It is important to understand whether high-producing,
unffeated cows lose and gain body condition in a fashion similar
to rbGH-treated cows. Observations of Tyrrell et al. (1988)
and ofGallo et al. (1988) suggest that this is the case because
the digestibility of diets are not changed by rbGH. Furthermore,
Tyrrell et al. (1988) showed that heat production in rbGH-
fteated and conftol cows can be predicted with equal accuracies,
indicating that partial efficiency of nutrient utilization is
unaltered by rbGH ffeaffnent. The potential for carryover effects
of rbGH on body condition was addressed indirectly by
McBride et al. (1990), who showed that pretreatrnent (before
peak milk yield) feed intakes were elevated for previously
treated versus previously untreated cows. Therefore, elevated
intakes of previously neated cows early in ttre lactation compen-
sate for weight losses during the prior lactation. Another possi-
bilio/ is that rbGH may have carryover lipolytic effects. If true,
it would be more diffrcult for treated cows to regain condition
for the subsequent lactation. This does not seem to be the case,
however, because dry cows that received rbGH in the previous
lactation had the same lipolytic response to epinephrine
challenge as dry cows that did not receive rbGH (rfebvre et al.
1991). Therefore, adipocyte metabolism appears to return to
normal when rbGH injections are terminated.
It is of further importance to establish the degree to which
rbGH alters body composition during lactation, because
nutrients required for weight gain and nutrients obtained from
weight loss assume a specified body composition. Brown
et al. (1989) and McGuffey et al. (1989) both demonstrated
that rbGH changes body composition, but these changes were
predictable based on energy balance and milk yield. There-
fore, partial efficiencies for body weight gain or loss should
not differ as a result of rbGH treatrnent (Bauman et al. 1994).

The major components of milk include water, lactose, fat,
protein, vitamins, and minerals. The overall composition
and, therefore, manufacturing properties of milk are not
altered by rbGH treatment, but temporary changes in milk
fat (increased) and protein (decreased) percentages can occur
when rbGH is administered during negative or low energy
or nitrogen balance (such as in early lactation). Therefore,
management regime, including timing of rbGH administra-
tion, nutritional program, milking procedure, and herd health
practice will have an impact on the production response of
dairy cows to rbGH.

II. MECHANISM OF GROWTH HORMONE ACTION
Most endogenous bovine GH consists of protein molecules
that are either 191 or 190 amino acids long (Wingfield et al.
1987; Lucy et al. 1993), although many other smaller frag-
ments of pituitary GH have also been observed. Preformed
GH is stored in secretory granules of somatotroph cells in
the anterior pituitary and is released by exocytosis in response
to appropriate stimuli (Rawlings and Mason 1989). Some
physiological stimuli that have been reported to influence the
release of GH in numerous species are GH releasing factor
(GRF) and somatostatin (SS) from the hypothalamus,
fluctuations in blood concentrations of glucagon, insulin,
somatomedins, and estrogen, stress, and the onset of sleep
(Rawlings and Mason 1989). Regulation of GH action can
be considered at several physiological levels (Norman and
Litwack 1987), including concentrations of numerous blood
hormones, tissue distribution and concentration of GH
receptors, and transmembrane signalling mechanisms.
Administration of rbGH may override or alter some regula-
tory pathways normally encountered by endogenous GH,
nonetheless, actions of rbGH come under the control of a
similarly complex spectrum of regulatory processes
(reviewed by Prosser and Mepham 1989). Like any other
protein hormone, GH elicits its physiological actions by
binding to receptors in the plasma membranes of target cells.
The major activities of bovine GH are increased lipolysis,
diabetogenesis, protein accretion, bone growth, gluconeo-
genesis, marnmogenesis, and galactopoiesis (reviewed by
Bauman and McCutcheon 1986; Johnsson and Hart 1986;
Peel and Bauman 1987; McBride et al. 1988).

Growth Hormone and Insulin'like Growth Factor
Receptors
The initial step in the action of peptide hormones is binding
to receptors in target tissues (Grunberger 1987). To date'
GH receptors have been found on hepatocytes, adipocytes,
lymphocytes, macrophages, fibroblasts, preadipocytes,
chondrocytes, B islet cells, and osteoblasts in a variety of
species, with those of rabbit liver being the best character-
ized (reviewed by Waters et al. 1989). Rabbit liver GH
receptors are cysteine-rich sialoglycoproteins which have a
propensiry to form disulphide-linked dimers and trimers' The
molecular mass of the binding subunit is about 110 000 kDa
with one transmembrane sequence, five potential extra-
cellular N-glycosylation sites, and a long cytoplasmic tail.
Ubiquitin is covalently attached to the receptor and may be
partially responsible for its rapid turnover (approximately
45 min) independent of hormone binding (FiS. 2; Waters
et al. 1989). Scatchard analysis shows only one type ofrabbit
GH receptor that has no amino acid sequence homology to
any known peptides implicated in signal transduction
mechanisms. There is some indication that the rabbit GH
receptor is a tyrosine kinase, but it has been suggested that
signal transduction may also be mediated via micro-
aggregation of the receptor molecules (reviewed by Waters
et al. 1989).
A serum GH binding protein (BP) exists in rabbits (Leung
et al. 1987) and humans (Baumann et al. 1986, 1989;
Herington and Ymer 1986) whose amino acid sequence
corresponds to the NH2-terminal of the liver GH receptor.
Recent examination of the crystal structure of human GH
bound to its BP has revealed a novel 1,:2 ratio for this ligand-
receptor complex (De Vos et al. 1993). Although the two
binding sites on the human GH molecule have different
shapes, the residues on both BPs in the complex that interact
with these sites have a similar structure (De Vos et al. 1993).
These observations agree with those of Strasbuger et al.
(1989) who used high-affrnity monoclonal antibodies to probe
human GH and demonstrate two sites of somatogen receptor
binding by this molecule. Interestingly, dimerization of the
BPs that was visualized by De Vos et al. (1993) occurred
in the part of the COOH-terminal domain that would be
closest to the cell's plasma membrane in the intact receptor.
Combined, these results suggest that the intact human GH
receptor is dimerized when bound by a single GH molecule
and support a microaggregation theory for signal transduction
by this receptor tyrosine kinase (Waters et al. 1989; De Vos
et al. 1993). The presence of GH BPs in blood serum also
suggests that GH may have autocrine and (or) paracrine roles
in addition to its traditional endocrine activities.

Growth Hormone and Nutrient Partitioning
That rbGH treatment alone can enhance the oersistencv of
milk production, particularly after peak yield, ,ugg.ri, u
key regulatory role of GH during bovine galactopoiesis
(reviewed by Bauman and McCutcheon 1986; Johnsson and
Hart 1986; McBride et al. 1988). Substantial evidence now
exists to support a hypothesis that metabolic adaptations
leading to the galactopoietic response of rbGH-treated cows
are similar to those which support increasing milk yield
during early lactation (reviewed by Vernon 1989). The
mechanism of action of rbGH during galactopoiesis can be
considered as indirect effects (augmented avaiiability of
milk precursors in biood) and as direct effects (increased
synthetic capacity and, as suggested by Politis et al. (1990),
slowed involution of the mammary gland). As discussed
subsequently, chronic lipolytic and diabetogenic conditions
resulting from rbGH administration are an integral part of
the nutrient partitioning elicited by rbGH in support of
increased milk synthesis.
During natural lactation, increasing milk yield is associated
with an elevated blood concentration of GH (Ingalls et al.
1973; Hart et al. 1979; Vasilatos and Wangsness 1981 ; Bines
and Hart 1982; Sartin et al. 1985) which declines during later
lactation (Koprowski and Tucker 1973). High-yielding cows
have also been shown to have higher serum GH concentra-
tions than low-yielding cows during lactation (Hart et al.
1978; Sartin et al. 1988). On the other hand, high insulin
concentrations are negatively correlated with milk yield
(reviewed by Sartin et al. 1988; Collier et al. 1989) and,
despite its requirement during lactogenesis, administration
of insulin into lactating animals dramatically depresses milk
yield as glucose is utilized by peripheral tissues (Collier et al.
1989). Progression ofnatural lactation is also associated with
increased glucose sparing by peripheral tissues because the
insulin responsiveness to elevated blood glucose is reduced
(Sano et al. 1993) and tissues such as adipose and skeletal
muscle become progressively resistant to insulin (Wilson and
Foster 1985; McNamara et al. 1987; Vernon and Sasaki
1991). In turn, hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia prevail
making available large quantities of glucose for mammary
gland synthesis of lactose (Fig. 3). If plane of nutrition is
adequate, elevated glucose and insulin could provide up-
regulating signals for the high-afhnity GH receptor type in
the liver, as is proposed in Fig. 2. Together with increased
synthesis and secretion of milk fats and proteins by mammary
epithelial cells, increased production of milk lactose @auman
and Currie 1980) causes increased milk volume output into
mammary alveoii. Hyperglycemia and a heightened capacity
of liver GH receptors may help stimulate hepatic secretion
of IGF-I and, combined with the elevated blood concentra-
tion of GH, could cause further up-regulation of the type I
IGF receptor in responsive tissues such as liver, muscle, and
the mammary gland.
As described previously (Section I), there is a 7- to 10-wk
delay in increased voluntary feed intake after rbGH treat-
ments are initiated despite immediate and dramatic increases
in milk yield. During these first few weeks of rbGH treat-
ment, milk energy yields are increased by tp to 30%
(Bauman et al. 1988) while dietary component digestibili-
ties and heat losses are unaltered (reviewed by Bauman and
McCutcheon 1986; Tyrrell et al. 1988). Therefore, cows are
often in negative energy and nitrogen balance early in the
rbGH treatment period and must adapt metabolically to keep
producing elevated milk yield in response to rbGH. As pro-
posed subsequently, the inherent diabetogenic and lipolytic
properties of rbGH (Hart et al. 1984) must make such adap-
tations possible.
Treatment of lactating cows with exogenous bovine GH
has been shown to augment insulin resistance in peripheral
tissues (Sechen et al. 1990) with accompanying hyper-
glycemia, hyperinsulinemia (Cotes et al. 1949; Bourne et al.
1977), and increased persistency of lactation (Fig. 3). In a
14-d treatment regime, rbGH had no effect on plasma glucose
concentrations but increased the irreversible loss rate of
glucose by 12% (Bauman et al. 1988). In that study, milk
lactose production represented a major use of lost glucose.
McDowell et al. (1987b) demonstrated that rbGH treatments
reduced glucose uptake by the hindlimb muscle and increased
glucose uptake by the mammary gland, lending further sup-
port that diabetogenesis is an inherent property ofthe rbGH
molecule (Hart et al. 1984). We additionally propose that
IGF-I plays a fundamental role in the diabetogenic activities
of rbGH.
In the absence of rbGH treaffnents, and in response to nega-
tive energy balance, liver IGF-I secretion is normally
decreased in the face of rising GH concentrations (Fig. 2;
Elsasser et al. 1989). However, this natural uncoupling of
the GH-IGF-I axis has not been observed when rbGH is
administered to lactating cows in negative energy balance
(Gallo 1989; Gallo and Block 1990a). Chronic elevation of
blood IGF-I may be an important aspect of the diabetogenic
and galactopoietic properties of the rbGH molecule (reviewed
by McBride et al. 1988), as IGF-I would be available in high
enough concentrations to compete with insulin for the insulin
receptor in peripheral tissues (Zapf et al. 1986). This would
encourage glucose sparing by peripheral tissues and increase
the availability of glucose for milk production by the mam-
mary gland (Fig. 3). To our knowledge, data which show
increased tissue sensitivity to IGF-I during rbGH adminis-
tration are not available. However, if the IGF-I model of
diabetogenesis elicited by rbGH is true it has additional impli-
cations for enhanced milk yield at the level of the mammary
gland. In particular, IGF-I may cause an increase in blood

Summary
The role of GH during (natural) bovine lactation is in part
explained by the ability of GH to alter adipose tissue
metabolism by altering its tissue sensitivities to acutely
acting (homeostatic) hormones such as insulin and
epinephrine. Thus, the mechanism of action of injected GH
can also be partly reconciied as an augmentation of these
phenomena. Recent as well as earlier srudies in which various
forms of exogenous bovine GH were administered to
lactating dairy cows support the theory that chronic regulation
of metabolism via lipolytic and diabetogenic effects must ulti-
mately be responsible for enhanced galactopoiesis during
such treatments. Evidence revealing the lipolytic changes
during bovine GH treatment includes chronic elevations of
blood NEFA and NEFA irreversible losses, decreased body
condition score, increased feed efficiency with no changes
in digestibiliry of diets, and increased milk fat synthesis such
that percentage of milk fat is not altered by treatments even
in the face of up to 40% increases in milk yield. Enhanced
lipolysis induced by rbGH treatment, therefore, has clear bio-
logical meaning because it provides a source of free fatty
acids for energy requirements during periods of glucose
sparing in peripheral tissues and a source of precursors for
milk fat synthesis. There is also strong evidence that rbGH
treatrnent increases milk yields by augmenting diabetogenesis
through an IGF-I-mediated pathway, although data which
demonstrate increased tissue sensitivity to IGF-I are required
to substantiate this mechanism of action.
Important remaining issues which require clarification
before the precise mechanisms of rbGH action during
galactopoiesis can be expiained include elucidation of the
types, numbers, specificities, and affinities of GH and
IGF-I receptors and BPs involved in target tissues which
support lactation, and the participation (including receptor
transduction mechanisms) of GH and IGF-I in altered tissue
sensitivities to homeostatic hormones and in mammary
physiology. The influence of nutrition on GH action during
lactation should also be investigated further. Other
important discoveries will be the elucidation of other
hormones and growth factors involved in the lactation
response to bovine GH, and the role of the genotype during
the response.

(i) Effects of Growth Hormone on Reproduction and
Metabolic Disorders ol Dairy Cattle
The first publicised reports on the long-term treatment of
lactating dairy cows with rbGH had tremendous impact and
stirred much controversy based on speculated consequences
of its application. Such reaction may have been justified
at the time because up to 40% increases in milk yield, with
only moderate increases in feed intake which occurred
several weeks after milk yield responses, were reported
(Bauman et al. 1985). Immediate concern was expressed
in the scientific and lay communities that cows would be
overworked and suffer reproductive inefficiency and poor
health as a consequence of rbGH treatment. In particular,
it was feared that the heavy reliance upon body energy
reserves during initial phases of rbGH treatment in early
lactation may increase the incidence ofketosis, fatty livers,
and infertility.
Many trials using rbGH during full lactations have been
conducted (see McBride et al. 1988; Burton and McBride
1989; Bauman 1992; Bauman and Vernon 1993 for
reviews) since the original report by Bauman et al. (1982).
However, the number of full journal articles that deal with
the topics ofreproductive and health effects ofrbGH have
been relatively small because several problems have arisen
in validating true effects of rbGH on such variables. Related
to this is the length of rbGH trials. Short-term efficacy
studies (Fronk et al. 1983; Peel et al. 1983; Eppard et al.
1985) did not allow monitoring of overall reproductive per-
formance or health status. Furthermore, the long-term (i.e.,
at ieast one full lactation) efficacy studies have generally
monitored reproduction and health status as secondary
observations (Eppard et al. 1987; Elvinger et al. 1988;
Burton et al. 1990a,b; L6onard et al. 1990; McGuffey
et al. 1991; Rajamahendran et al. 1991) rather than as
primary objectives with intensive sampling and monitoring
regimes. Adequate statistical analysis and interpretation of
the resulting reproductive and health data have been
difficult or impossible due to the small number of cows on
individual trials. A further controversial issue, specifically
related to reproductive efficiency, has been the monitoring
of days open as an index of reproductive performance. This
method is valid only for trials in which rbGH injections
are commenced prior to 50 d postpartum (cows not preg-
nant). However, in trials that commence rbGH injections
after 90 d postpartum (where breeding commenced prior
to this time), the validity of variables such as days open
or services per conception is seriouslyjeopardized because
some cows in both control and rbGH groups will have con-
ceived prior to treatment initiation. A third problem has
been encountered in dealing with pretreatment milk produc-
tion of cows. Although most studies report roughly equal
production potentials of control and rbGH treatment
groups, interpretation ofreproductive and health data from
these trials is difficult because any difference between

Effects of Growth Hormone on Ketosis and Liver
Function
As previously described (Section II), elevated circulating
concentrations of GH due to the administration of exogenous
rbGH are related to metabolic conditions characteristic of
ascending lactation. Because the liver is a key organ in the
intermediary metabolism of lipids, glucose, and proteins,
some concern has been voiced that this organ may be more
susceptible to disease in high-producing cows, particularly
during the metabolic stress of early lactation (R6m6s,v et al.
i986). Kronfeld and Chalupa (1986) further speculated that
administration of rbGH during periods of high milk yield
may increase the incidence of ketosis.
Growth hormone administration during efficacy trials has
yielded some inconsistent results with respect to rate of
adipose tissue metabolism, but increased persistenry of
lactation and longer periods oflow or negative energy balance
seem to be patent (Sections I and II; Soderholm et al. 1988;
Bauman et al. 1989a,b). During rbGH administration, there
is an increase in the blood concentration of NEFA (Peel and
Bauman 1987; Soderholm et al. 1988) which results from
increased lipolysis and decreased lipogenesis in adipose
tissue (Peel et al. 1981; Hart et al. 1984; Gallo and Block
1990a). However, Gallo and Block (1990a) showed that cows
injected with rbGH, which responded to the injection with
increased milk yield, did not possess liver lipid concentra-
tions outside the normal range (Curtis et al. 1989). Simi-
larly, concentrations of plasma ketone bodies and glutamic
oxaloacetic transaminase also were not different between
rbGH-injected and control cows (Gallo and Block 1990a).
These results indicate that the extra NEFA mobilized during
rbGH treatment may be utilized completely by peripheral
tissues, probably as an alternative source ofenergy to glucose
(Fig. 3). Therefore, NEFA deposition as triglyceride in the
liver is not increased. The other metabolic changes associated
with the use of rbGH, such as hyperglycemia, hyper-
insulinemia (Fig. 3; Section II; Johnsson and Hart 1986;
Vicini et al. 1990), and increased hepatic gluconeogenesis
(Pocius and Herbein 1986) are contradictory to development
of ketosis. Therefore, conditions required for establishment
of the ketogenic state, namely low concentrations of blood
glucose and insulin (McGarry and Foster 1980), are not
present in rbGH-treated cows. While rbGH alters inter-
mediary metabolism in favour of elevated milk output by the
marnmary gland (Fig. 3), it also appears to allow overall
adaptation of metabolism such that the incidence of ketosis
and liver disease does not increase (reviewed by McBride
et al. 1988; Burton and McBride 1989; Chilliard 1989).

Influence of Growth Hormone on Reproductive Function
Effects of rbGH on reproductive variables have been
reviewed (Phipps 1989; Benzi 1990; Ferguson 1990; Aguilar
et al. 1991; Cole et aI. 1991; Wilkinson and Tarrant 1991;
Moore and Hutchinson 1992). An initial long-term study by
Bauman et al. (1985) revealed no differences in reproduc-
tive performance (days from calving to conception, services
per conception and conception rate) among rbGH-treated
cows, control cows, and other contemporaries in the herd.
However, more recent long-term studies showed tendencies
for longer calving to conception intervals (by 5-30 d;
Elvinger et al. 1988; Cleale et al. 1989; Hansen et al. 1989;
McDaniel et al. 1989; Burton et al. 1990b; Gibson et al.
1990), increased number of services per conception (by
0.5-1), and a lower conception rate (by 5-10%) in rbGH-
treated versus control cows (Cole et al. 1988; Huber et al.
1988; L6onard et al. 1990). These trends were especially
noticeable when higher doses (> 40 mg d-') of rbGH
were used (Thomas et al. 1987), but were not statistically
significant perhaps due to the limited number of cows and
the large variation among treatment groups in these individual
trials. When reproductive data were pooled from several
research sites, these trends were still apparent but, again,
were not statistically significant (Hard et al. 1988).
The trends of decreased reproductive efhciency from the
results listed above prompted research that was directed
towards determining whether there were definitive effects
of rbGH on reproductive function. Two main areas were
studied, namely, direct effects of rbGH on hormonal pro-
files associated with estrous cycles and indirect effects of
rbGH on reproductive efficiency resulting from negative

Pulsate secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH) by the
pituitary gland is required for normal ovuiation and is, there-
fore, a key factor for estrous cycles. Recently, Schemm et al.
(1990) reported positive correlations between rbGH treat-
ment and plasma LH concentrations during bovine estrous
cycles. Specifically, basal and average concentrations of
plasma LH were elevated (compared with values from control
cows) when blood was sampled during the first two estrous
cycles post-rbGH injection, and this was true even when cows
had a longer period of days open and a lower rate of con-
ception (Schemm et al. 1990). Subsequently, Gallo and Block
(1991) showed that the gonadotropin-releasing hormone
(GnRH)-induced LH response was augmented at 14 d posf
partum in cows treated with rbGH. At 14 d postpartum there
is no interference of ovarian steroids on the hypothalamic-
hypophyseal-ovarian axis (Carruthers et al. 1980), so rbGH
must have affected LH secretion either directly, or indirectly
through changes in the available energy pool (Iileaver 1987).
Administration of rbGH after day 14 postpartum did not
affect the endogenous secretion ofLH in terms ofpulse, fre-
quency, or amplitude (Schemm et al. 1990), or when induced
by GnRH (Gallo and Block 1991). It is anticipated, there-
fore, that rbGH will not negatively affect the regularity of
estrous cycles.
Progesterone (Pa) is another key hormone of estrus. It is
produced by the corpus luteum of the ovary under the
influence of LH and is required or maintenance of the first
2 mo of pregnancy. Furthermore, changes in Pa secretion
during an estrous cycle can lead to changes in estrus
behaviour (Davidge et al. 1987). It has been hypothesized,
therefore, that trends of lowered reproductive performance
in rbGH-treated cows may result from non-detected estrous
due to effects of rbGH on Pa secretion. In vitro studies show
that the rate of Pa synthesis is regulated by GH in bovine
granulosa cells, via the local production of IGF-I (Savion
et al. 1981; Colenbrander et al. 1984; Adashi et al. 1984,
1985). Schemm et al. (1990) then reported positive correla-
tions between rbGH treatment and concentrations of plasma
Pa during bovine estrous cycles. Gallo and Block (1991)
observed increased P4 secretion during estrous cycles and
pregnancy when rbGH was injected and speculated that these
effects may have been caused by an increase in IGF-I produc-
tion stimulated by the rbGH treatment (Spicer et al. 1990).
However, the length and regularity of estrous cycles were
not affected by rbGH treatment in the study by Gallo and
Block (1991), suggesting that rbGH did not affecr estrus
detection via altered Pa levels. In support of this theory,
Lefebvre et al. (1991) showed effects of rbGH on estrous
behaviour that were not attributable to Pa. Ovariectomized,
non-lactating heifers injected with rbGH and induced to
exhibit estrus with estradiol injections exhibited mounting
behaviour with 1/3 less frequency, and for a shorter dura-
tion, than heifers not injected with rbGH (Lefebvre et al.
1991). It is possible that this apparently direct effect of
rbGH on estrous behaviour may have been mediated through
changes in other blood metabolites and hormones (e.g.,
NEFA, glucose, insulin), binding of estrogen receptors, or
by increased secretion B-endorphin. For example, it has been
shown that B-endorphin decreases the intensity of estrous
behaviour and decreases pulsatility of GnRH secretion
(Sirinathsinghji et al. 1983). No data are available which
demonstrate effects of rbGH or milk yield on p-endorphin
production and all of the above listed possibilities require
investigation.
The influence of negative energy balance on reproduction
has been studied extensively and was recently reviewed by
Butler and Smith (1989), with its effects on hormonal secre-
tion profiles (Brockman and Laarveld 1986) and ovarian
follicular growth (Spicer and Echternkamp 1986) described
in cattle. Dietary effects on IGF-I and reproduction in dairy
cattle have also been reported (Spicer et al. 1990). Further-
more, the importance of energy reserves during lactation
(Gearhart et al. 1988; Upham et al. 1990) and level ofenergy
reserves at calving (Garnsworthy 1988) on milk production
and reproductive efficiency are currently under investiga-
tion and general conclusions to date are that reproductive
performance is compromised during negative energy balance
and lowered body condition.
When cows respond to rbGH with increased milk yields,
there is an increased or prolonged negative energy balance
accompanied by increases in lipolysis, IGF-I secretion,
hyperglycemia, and hyperinsulinemia (Sections I and II).
These changes also occur during ascending lactation in the
absence of rbGH (Walsh et al. 1980; Flux et aL. 1984; Barnes
et al. 1985). There is evidence that reproductive performance
is compromised as milk yield increases (Spalding et al. 1975)
and that plane of nutrition and blood metabolites affect the
LH response to GnRH (Beal et al. 1918), positive feedback
loops elicited by estradiol (Staingmiller et aL. 1982), nega-
tive feedback loops elicited by Pa (Beal et al. 1978), and LH
pulse frequency and amplitude (Morgan and Williams 1989).
Therefore, use of rbGH could compromise reproduction
through effects on energy balance or plane of nutrition. To
further complicate the dilemma in separating true effects of
rbGH on reproduction from other metabolic effects, Schemm
et al. (1990) and Gallo and Block (1991) showed that the
Pa-increasing effects of rbGH during estrous cycling
occurred only during the hrst two cycles postpartum when
milk yield and negative energy balance peaked, suggesting
that hormonal signals (e.g., IGFJ) may eventually overcome
metabolic signals (i.e., energy balance). More research is
needed to verify whether rbGH has such indirect effects on
reproduction (Olds et aL.7979; Martinez and Thibier 1984;
Berghorm et al. 1988).
Summary
Treatment of lactating dairy cows with rbGH results in a
series of orchestrated changes in the metabolism of body
tissues which favour elevated milk output and overall adap-
tation such that ketosis and liver disease do not occur. On
the basis of available information, there are no reasons to
suspect that cows injected with rbGH, or the developing
foetus, will suffer unusual metabolic complications which

programs will have to place greater emphasis on managing

treated cows. Based on responses in milk yield to rbGH, a
ration formulated for high production may have to be fed
for a longer time to sustain a response and allow for regain

Individual long-term studies have shown tendencies for
longer calving intervals, increased number of services to con-
ception, and a lower conception rate in rbGH-treated versus
control cows. These trends could be related to the magni-
tude of the milk increase in rbGH-treated cows. since avail-

do not impinge negatively on the regularity or length of
estrous cycles. However, rbGH treatments may impact on
reproductive management because mounting behaviour can
be altered and increases in milk yield during prolonged nega-

reproductive effrciency. For example, depending upon
overall milk yield and the type of production response to
rbGH exhibited by a herd, producers could elect to with-

the period ofnegative energy balance has subsided, to avoid
increases in services per conception. Alternatively, they may
delay first breeding by 30 d if rbGH is commenced very early
in lactation. This alternative appears viable because the cost
of an increased calving interval would be offset by the
increase in milk yield with greater persistency of produc-
tion for a longer lactation. Because the shape of the lacta-
tion curve changes with rbGH, current accepted economic
ideals of 12- to 13-mo calving intervals and 305-d lactations
may also require reevaluation.

Effects of Growth Hormone on Occurrence of Mastitis
Effects of rbGH on the duration and incidence of clinical
mastitis are of special interest. Several groups have summa-
rized data from studies of similar design to allow for detec-
tion of subtle effects on mastitis-related variables (Phipps
1989; Ferguson 1990; Craven 1991; McClary et al. 1991;
Monsallier 1991; Moore and Hutchinson1992). These sum-
maries indicated that treatment with rbGH had no signifi-
cant effect on mastitis-related variables. Incidence of mastitis
is related to many factors, especially environment and
milking management practices, and these effects are equally
evident in both control and rbGH-treated groups within a
herd. In herds where a small but consistent positive relation-
ship between milk yield and incidence of mastitis has been
observed, treatment with rbGH does not alter the relation-
ship (Craven 1991; Monsallier 1991). Results of a recent
experiment have shown that rbGH-treated and control cows
have the same ability to cope with mastitis induced by a large
experimental Staphylococcus aureus infection (Rasmussen,
Sejrsen, and Jensen, personal communication). Burvenich
et al. (1989b) showed that rbGH treatrnent is associated with
a quicker recovery of milk secretion in experimentally
infected mammary glands. Moreover, observations from a
series of recent papers by Burton et al. (1991a,b; I992a,b)
indicate that exogenous rbGH either enhances or does not
alter specific indices of bovine immune function. Therefore,
a slight increase in mastitis incidence may be observed in
rbGH-treated cows as a normal result of increased milk
production, but there is no evidence to suggest that rbGH
increases mastitis susceptibility by decreasing host immuno-
logical competence (see Section III, part (iii)).
Elevated milk somatic cell count (SCC) is commonly used
as an indicator of mastitis. Based on observations of both
occurrence of clinical mastitis and SCC, some of the studies
listed above indicate potential negative effects of high doses
of rbGH (20.6 mg d-1 or more) during early- to mid-
lactation on udder health. For example, SCC was elevated
during the ftrst 9 wk of a 38-wk. injection period for cows
treated with higher (20.6 mg d-'), but not lower (10.3 mg
d-r) doses of rbGH (McBride et al. 1990). This portion of
the treatment period coincided with peak milk yield and
effects of rbGH on SCC may have been confounded with
low or negative energy balance and (or) mammary gland
stress which normally accompanies this stage of early, heavy
milk production. However, Hemken et al. (1991) showed
that milk SCC was not influenced by either energy density
of the diet or by rbGH treatment (20.6 mg cow - ' d - ') in
a single lactation trial. In the study by McBride et al. (1990),
milk SCC was actually lower in the pretreatment period
for cows returning for a second treatment with rbGH than
for cows which had not received rbGH in previous lacta-
tions, and differences in SCC among currently treated and
untreated cows did not exist past the first 9 wk of treatment.

Observations from Non-bovine Species
Growth hormone deficient animals such as hypophysec-
tomized rodents, hypopituitary Snell-Bagg dwarf mice, and
some breeds of dogs have well characterized immunodefi-
ciencies (Fabris et aL. 7911a; Roth et al. 1984). These include
underdeveloped bone marrow (especially mononuclear cells),
reduced weights and cellularity of the thymus, spleen and
lymph nodes, decreased lymphocyte and polymorphonuclear
leukocyte numbers in peripherai blood, deficient cell-
mediated immune responses (e.g., allogenic graft rejection
time, graft versus host reaction, and delayed hypersensitivity
induced by haptenic chemicals), and decreased antibody
responses that are T cell-dependent (Fabris et al. 1971a; van
Buul-Offers et al. 1981; Roth et al. 1984; reviewed by Berczi
1986; Kelley 1989). Observed effects of GH deficiency on
the human immune system have been less consistent (Crist
et al. 1987; Rapaport et al. 1987; Bozzola et al. 1988b 1989;
Kiess et al. 1988; Spadoni et al. 1989).
In most instances of GH deficiency-related immuno-
deficiency, exogenous GH treatments are capable ofrecon-
stituting the immune system. Thymus and spleen weights
reverted to normal when Snell-Bagg mice (van Buul-Offers
et al. 1981) and dwarf Weimaraner dogs (Roth et al. 1984)
were treated with GH. Similarly, the deficient cellular
responses of lymphocytes from hypophysectomized rodents
were reversed to normal levels by GH administration (Berczi
et al. 1985). Thymic involution (which occurs normally
during aging) was reversed in aged rats implanted with GH-
secreting GH3 pituitary cells (Kelley et al. 1986) and
exogenous ovine GH augmented both spontaneous and
mitogen-driven proliferation of splenocytes from aged female
rats @rief et al. 1987). Increased Bursal weights and primary
antibody response titres to sheep erythrocytes were observed
in GH-treated, sex-linked dwarf White Leghorn chickens
(Marsh et ai. 1983). Both earlier (Fabris et a|. 1977b;, Arren-
brecht and Sorkin 1973) and more recent studies (Briefet al.
1987) have implicated a thymic dependency for immune
reconstitution elicited by exogenous GH.
Pioneering studies designed to detect GH receptors on
lymphocyte plasma membranes used a transformed human
cell line called the IM-9 lymphocyte (Fahey et al. 1977).
Cultured IM-9 cells were found to bind GH with high affinity
and specificity (Lesniak et al. 1974, 1977), and the GH
receptors were down-regulated by increasing the concentra-
tion of GH in support medium (Lesniak and Roth 1976).
Receptor-bound GH is internalized and found in lysosomal
association within the cell @arazzone et al. 1980), suggesting
at least one mechanism by which actions of GH are regu-
lated at the cellular level. Hydrophilic, GH-specific BPs are
also present in culture medium supporting IM-9 cells
(McGuffin et al. 1976; Trivedi and Daughaday 1988) and,
like those found in human (Baumann et al. 1986, 1989;
Herington and Ymer 1986) and rabbit serum (Leung et al.
1987), they are derived from the extracellular domain of the
GH receptor. There is some indication that GH BPs may pro-
tect bound GH from degradation via the normal process of
endocytosis in lymphocytes (McGuffin et aL. 1976). Produc-
tion of GH BPs may also regulate the action of GH on
lymphocytes by acting as a transport protein and by extending
the half-life of this hormone.
Clejan et al. (1982) and Kiess and Butenandt (1985)
showed specific binding of GH to human peripheral blood
mononuclear cells, but this receptor-ligand binding has been
associated with variable biological responses. In one study,
mitogen-stimulated cells showed. increased proliferation at
low doses of GH (450 ng mL-') while higher test doses
(up to 8500 ng ml-r) induced a progressive decrease in
lymphocyte proliferation (Bozzola et al. 1988a). In another
study (Schimpff and Repellin 1989), physiological concen-
trations of GH added to the cultured cells inhibited mitogen-
induced lymphocyte proliferation but the conditioned medium
from these cells enhanced proliferation of subsequent cul-
tures. Wolf et al. (1987) observed no effect of GH (15 or
150 ng mL r) on a murine one-way mixed lymphocyte
reaction when GH was added to the culture with allogenic
target cells but GH inhibited incorporation of tritiated thymi-
dine when it was added prior to addition of the target cells.
Blood serum from various sources (GH-deficient, GH-
deficient GH-treated, normal, or acromegalic patients) has
also been used to determine the GH requirements for mitosis
of human blood lymphocytes. In these studies. mitogen-

incorporation of tritiated thymidine (Thieriot-Prevost et al.
1981; Salvatoni et al. 7984;Bozzola et al. 1985). There has

contribute to the production and subsequent action of GH

1990). Guarcello et al. (1989) have demonstrated GRF
receptors on thymocytes and splenocytes, which when treated

Summary
In summary, IM-9 cells, untransformed lymphocytes from
peripheral blood, splenocytes, polymorphonuclear and
mononuclear leukocytes, and tissue macrophages from
several species in various physiological states have been
shown to bind and respond to GH and IGF-I. Furthermore,
the receptor-hornone binding characteristics of GH and IGF-
I on lymphocytes are similar to those described for cells of
nonJymphoid origin.
Immune cells not only bind GH and IGF-I but also produce
mRNA transcripts for these hormones. Production of these
transcripts is increased early during local inflammatory
responses, suggesting possible autocrine or paracrine actions
of these locally produced peptides. GH bound to its BPs may
serve as a reserve source of the hormone allowing it to be
available locally for rapid stimulation of IGF-I production
by activated lymphocytes or during periods of GH dehciency.
Since in vitro studies have shown that lymphocyte GH and
IGF-I receptors are acutely down-regulated with increasing
local concentrations of these hormones, it could be specu-
lated that endocytosis of these ligand-receptor complexes acts
as a rapid shut-down mechanism to control over-active local
inflammatory responses. On the other hand, circulating GH
(and possibly IGF-I) may provide more subtle and long-term
regulation of immune function, possibly during times of

(iv) GROWTH HORMONE AND MILK SAFETY
The issue of presence and bioactivity of rbGH and IGF-I in
milk of rbGH-treated cows has been thoroughly studied and
reviewed (McBride et al. 1988; Groenewegen et al. 1990;
Juskevich and Guyer 1990; Collier et al. 1997; Bauman and
Vernon 1993). The concentration of rbGH in milk is only
increased above the endogenous concentration when provoca-
tive doses of exogenous rbGH are administered to dairy
cows. Therefore, at recommended milk-yield enhancing
doses, rbGH concentration in milk is not elevated (Juskevich
and Guyer 1990). Furthermore, pasteurization eliminates
85-90% of immunoreactive rbGH in milk (Groenewegen
et al. 1990). Beyond this, research to date indicates that rbGH
is not active in primates (including humans), even at provoca-
tive doses, regardless of whether it is ingested or injected
(reviewed by Juskevich and Guyer 1990; Bauman and
Vernon 1993).
On the other hand, the IGF-I content of milk of an
individual cow can be increased by up to twofold with rbGH
treatment, but concentrations still remain within the range
of normal values and similar to levels found in human breast
milk (Juskevich and Guyer 1990). The IGF-I content of colos-
trum is unusually high and a major component (approximately
50%) of it is des-IGF-I. Des-IGF-I is an IGF-I variant which
can be more bioactive under some situations. Recent studies
have characterized the IGF-I fraction of milk from rbGH-
treated cows and shown that des-IGF-I is a minor component
of the somatomedins fraction and does not contribute sig-

Growth hormone and IGF's are present in milk from
untreated cows (Daughaday and Barbano 1990). Both

et al. 1989).

Pasteurization of milk is carried out either by moderate

CONCLUSION
Technical assessments of the biological effects of rbGH on
dairy cows have consistently demonstrated that it is
efficacious in increasing milk production and induces no
catastrophic effects on the physiological systems involved
in reproduction, disease resistance, and animal well-being.
