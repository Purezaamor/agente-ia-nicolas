Nutrient ﬂuctuations during the fetal stage affects fetal development, which has long-term impacts on the
production efﬁciency and quality of meat. During the early development, a pool of mesenchymal progenitor
cells proliferate and then diverge into either myogenic or adipogenic/ﬁbrogenic lineages. Myogenic progenitor
cells further develop into muscle ﬁbers and satellite cells, while adipogenic/ﬁbrogenic lineage cells develop
into adipocytes, ﬁbroblasts and resident ﬁbro-adipogenic progenitor cells. Enhancing the proliferation and myo-
genic commitment of progenitor cells during fetal development enhances muscle growth and lean production in
offspring. On the other hand, promoting the adipogenic differentiation of adipogenic/ﬁbrogenic progenitor cells
inside the muscle increases intramuscular adipocytes and reduces connective tissue, which improves meat
marbling and tenderness. Available studies in mammalian livestock, including cattle, sheep and pigs, clearly
show the link between maternal nutrition and the quantity and quality of meat production. Similarly, chicken
muscle ﬁbers develop before hatching and, thus, egg and yolk sizes and hatching temperature affect long-term
growth performance and meat production of chicken. On the contrary, because ﬁshes are able to generate new
muscle ﬁbers lifelong, the impact of early nutrition on ﬁsh growth performance is expected to be minor, which
requires further studies.

1. Introduction
Forage and feed deﬁciency is a common occurrence in the livestock
industry in many areas of the world. Such deﬁciency could be due to
drought which results in a signiﬁcant reduction in forage production
subjecting cows and ewes to nutrient deﬁciency during gestation
(Du et al., 2013). In addition, nutrient deﬁciency could also be due to
the lack of feeds and the poor quality of feeds, rendering sows and
hens to nutrient deﬁciency (Altmann et al., 2012).
Fetal programming, also called developmental programming or fetal
developmental programming, is the response to a speciﬁcchallengeto

In these studies, both malnutrition and over-nutrition during gestation

2004; Bieswal et al., 2006; Bispham et al., 2003; Desai, Gayle, Babu, &

& Stephenson, 2004; Vonnahme et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2013; Zambrano

development is also important to maximize the growth potential of
animals.
Meat animals are raised for their skeletal muscle. The fetal stage
is crucial for skeletal muscle development in mammalian livestock, be-
cause there is no net increase in the muscle ﬁber number after birth
(Stickland, 1978; Zhu, Ford, Nathanielsz, & Du, 2004). A decrease in
the number of muscle ﬁbers due to fetal programming permanently
reduces muscle mass and negatively affects animal performance.
Marbling (intramuscular fat) is crucial for meat palatability and
the fetal and neonatal stages are major stages for generation of intra-
muscular adipocytes (Tong et al., 2008), which provide the sites for in-
tramuscular fat accumulation or marbling formation during fattening.
Hence, fetal programming also affects the marbling in offspring cattle.
In addition, the overall fat accumulation in offspring is also affected by
maternal nutrition (Zhu et al., 2006).
In this review, we summarize recent progress in the impact of
maternal nutrition on fetal development, focusing on myogenesis and
intramuscular adipogenesis, as well as the impacts of fetal programming
on the growth performance and meat quality of offspring of mammalian
livestock. In the end, as a comparison, we also discuss the possible
impacts of fetal programming on poultry and ﬁsh production.
2. Programming of skeletal muscle and adipose tissue development
2.1. Fetal programming of skeletal muscle development
Muscle cells, adipocytes and ﬁbroblasts are all derived from
the mesoderm. Speciﬁcally, skeletal muscle cells are developed from
dermomyotome during the early embryonic development, while adipo-
cyte progenitors are derived from lateral mesoderm (Fig. 1). Mesoderm
cells undergoing myogenic commitment initially become Pax3+ and
then Pax7 +; and the subsequent expression of Myf5 commits these
cells to the myogenic lineage. Myogenic differentiation involves the se-
quential expression of MyoD, myogenin and MYF-4 (Buckingham et al.,
2003; Collins et al., 2009). These myogenic transcription factors then
initiate the expression of myogenic speciﬁc genes including embryonic
myosin heavy chain. Interestingly, brown adipocytes can be developed
from Myf5 + cells during fetal development (Seale et al., 2008). Recently,
there is a new pool of brown-like adipocytes identiﬁed in adipose tissue,
so called beige adipocytes. These cells also express UCP-1, generating
heat (Harms & Seale, 2013). Selected markers for satellite cells, muscle
ﬁbers, ﬁbroblasts, brown and beige adipocytes are listed in Table 1.
Ontogenetically, the early skeletal muscle development can be sepa-
rated into primary myogenesis and secondary myogenesis. Primary
myoﬁbers form ﬁrst during the embryonic stage, followed by the forma-
tion of secondary myoﬁbers which accounts for the majority of muscle
ﬁbers in adults (Beermann, Cassens, & Hausman, 1978). There is no fur-
ther increase in muscle ﬁbers after birth, and postnatal muscle growth is
mainly due to increase in muscle ﬁber size without forming new muscle
ﬁbers (Karunaratne, Ashton, & Stickland, 2005; Stickland, 1978). Satel-
lite cells are derived from fetal myoblasts, which become quiescent
and locate surrounding mature muscle ﬁbers in postnatal muscle. The
asymmetrical proliferation and myogenic differentiation of satellite
cells, and their fusion with existing muscle ﬁbers are crucial for postna-
tal muscle growth (Kuang, Kuroda, Le Grand, & Rudnicki, 2007).
Since the majority of muscle ﬁbers, if not all, form during the fetal
stage and myogenesis requires large amount of nutrients (Rehfeldt &

size but not the number (Du et al., 2010). However, based on rodent
studies, maternal nutrient restriction during late gestation reduces
satellite cell density, which may affect postnatal muscle growth and
muscle regeneration (Woo et al., 2011).
2.2. Fetal programming of fat and connective tissue development
Intramuscular fat (i.e., marbling) is crucial for the palatability of beef
because marbling contributes to both ﬂavor and juiciness. The amount
of intramuscular fat is determined by the number and size of intramus-
cular adipocytes. Fetal programming has a major effect on the number
of adipocytes in offspring. Adipocyte progenitors are derived from later-
al mesoderm, and likely have an endothelial origin. These progenitor
cells also have ﬁbrogenic capacity and named ﬁbro/adipogenic progen-
itors (FAPs) (Joe et al., 2010; Uezumi, Fukada, Yamamoto, Takeda, &
Tsuchida, 2010; Uezumi et al., 2011). Platelet derived growth factor re-
ceptor α (PDGFRα) positive cells are progenitors for adipogenesis
(Table 1)(Catalano & Hauguel-De Mouzon, 2011; Joe et al., 2010;
Uezumi et al., 2010; Uezumi et al., 2011). Stem cell antigen-1 (Sca-1)
is another marker for adipogenic progenitors, and PDGFRα+/CD45 −
and Sca-1 +/CD34 +/CD45− mark the similar pool of progenitor cells
possessing high adipogenic capacity (Joe et al., 2010; Tan, Eminli,
Hettmer, Hochedlinger, & Wagers, 2011; Uezumi et al., 2010; 2011),
with more than 85% cells identical (Joe et al., 2010). Progenitors and
preadipocytes account for about 15 to 50% of stromal vascular cells in
adipose tissue (Catalano & Hauguel-De Mouzon, 2011; Sepe, Tchkonia,
Thomou, Zamboni, & Kirkland, 2011). These cells reside in the adipose
vasculature. The source of these cells remains to be established, but a
large portion of FAPs are formed during fetal development (Yang
et al., 2013). During the fetal stage, it appears that mesoderm-derived
precursors with the capacity to differentiate into both endothelial and
mesenchymal lineage cells, called mesenchymoangioblasts, are formed
ﬁrst, and mesenchymal cells further develop into FAPs (Fig. 1). Indeed,
adipose development in the fetuses initiates with the formation of cap-
illary vessels in the spaces between mesenchymal tissues (Hausman &
Dodson, 2013).
During postnatal development, a portion of FAPs remain in the stro-
mal vascular fraction of adipose tissue, which becomes the source of
preadipocytes and adipocytes during later adipose tissue development
(Du et al., 2013; Huang, Das, Yang, Zhu, & Du, 2012a). Adipogenesis
can be separated into two stages, adipogenic commitment and
adipogenic differentiation, which are regulated by Zfp423 and peroxi-
some proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) respectively (Farmer,
2006; Gupta et al., 2010; 2012; Rosen & MacDougald, 2006; Spiegelman
& Flier, 1996; Vernochet, Peres, & Farmer, 2009).
Adipogenesis and neovascularization are temporally and spatially
associated processes (Cao, 2007). During embryogenesis, the primitive
fat organs are formed at the perivascular site (Hausman & Richardson,
1982). Preadipocytes migrate toward developing capillary network
to develop adipose tissue, a process regulated by αvβ3integrin
and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1, which are expressed in
both preadipocytes and capillary endothelial cells (Crandall, Busler,
McHendry-Rinde, Groeling, & Kral, 2000). Maternal food restriction
inhibits the expression of vascular epithelial growth factor (VEGF) in
microvascular and aortic endothelial cells during fetal development,
which decreases angiogenesis (Khorram et al., 2007). As adipose tissue
development is closely associated with capillary network development,
impaired neovascularization negatively affects fetal adipose develop-
ment. However, because adipose tissue has high plasticity, compensatory
growth of adipose tissue often result in fatter offspring born to nutrient
restricted mothers. Such growth is frequently associated with increased
feed intake and adipocyte hypertrophy (Gonzalez-Bulnes et al., 2012;
Zhu et al., 2006).
The initial events of adipose tissue development, including the for-
mation of a vascular network in future adipose tissue, initiates at a com-
parable time with primary myogenesis (Feve, 2005; Gnanalingham,
Mostyn, Symonds, & Stephenson, 2005; Muhlhausler, Dufﬁeld, &
McMillen, 2006). Roughly, the stage of adipogenic commitment over-
laps with the period of secondary myogenesis, and the major formation
of adipocytes occurs during late gestation to early weaning stages. Thus,
maternal nutritional management during this period affects adipocyte
hyperplasia, which alters overall adipose tissue development, as well
as intramuscular adipocyte density and thus marbling (Du et al., 2013).
The adipose tissue development is inseparable from the connective
tissue formation. Actually, adipose tissue is considered as a specialized
connective tissue. Accompanying adipogenesis, ﬁbrogenesis is also on-
going, together forming the adipose tissue and associated connective
tissue. Because both adipogenesis and ﬁbrogenesis share a common cell
lineage, a positive correlation between adipogenesis and ﬁbrogenesis is
expected. Consistently, in genetically high marbling Wagyu cattle, both
intramuscular adipogenesis and collagen accumulation are higher than
relatively low marbling Angus cattle (Duarte et al., 2013). Fibroblasts
also synthesize connective tissue surrounding muscle bundles, together

2.3. Competitive relationship between myogenesis, adipogenesis and
ﬁbrogenesis
As previously discussed, both skeletal muscle cells and adipocytes
are derived from the mesoderm during the embryonic stage, early
myogenesis and adipo/ﬁbrogenesis can be considered as a competitive
process. As much, enhanced muscle growth, such as double muscling
cattle, is associated with lower marbling. Interestingly, in our studies
with Wagyu cattle, we found that myogenesis was lower but intramus-
cular adipogenesis was higher in Wagyu cattle compared to Angus
cattle (Duarte et al., 2013).
Because adipogenic and ﬁbrogenic cells share immediate common
progenitor cells, it provides an opportunity for us to manipulate the
differentiation of progenitor cells to favor adipogenesis. Enhancing adi-
pogenesis in developing muscle increases intramuscular fat, thereby
improving beef ﬂavor. On the other hand, less ﬁbrogenic differentiation
reduces intramuscular connective tissue, thereby decreasing back-
ground toughness of beef. In combination, the eating quality of beef
can be improved. However, the mechanisms determining adipogenic
or ﬁbrogenic differentiation remain to be deﬁned. In our previous
studies, we found that Zfp423 is a critical regulator of adipogenic
differentiation. Over-expression of Zfp423 in stromal-vascular cells
separated from beef cattle enhanced adipogenic differentiation, while
knockdown of Zfp423 attenuated adipogenic capacity. Furthermore,
higher adipogenic differentiation due to enhanced Zfp423 expression
reduces ﬁbrogenesis (Huang et al., 2012a). Therefore, Zfp423 may pro-
vide a molecular target for enhancing intramuscular adipogenesis and
marbling in beef cattle, while reducing intramuscular collagen accumu-
lation and toughness of beef. Factors which can strengthen Zfp423
expression or enhance its function as a transcription factor are expected
to enhance adipogenesis and marbling in beef cattle. For example, vita-
min A supplementation can increase Zfp423 expression (Reichert et al.,
2011; Yasmeen, Jeyakumar, Reichert, Yang, & Ziouzenkova, 2012), and
its supplementation during a critical stage for intramuscular adipose
development is expected to enhance marbling.
2.4. Long-term effect of fetal programming on offspring
Fetal programming has long-term effects on offspring performance.
Lambs from dams subjected to nutrient restriction during mid-gestation
were fatter and had a lower lean to fat ratio than their counterparts
(Zhu et al., 2006). Piglets with lower birth-weight compared to their
littermates are fatter and have a low growth efﬁciency compared to
normal littermates (Wigmore & Stickland, 1983). Restriction in protein
intake during pregnancy reduces the size of progeny pigs (Pond, Yen,
Mersmann, & Maurer, 1990). Similarly, in sheep and beef cattle, nutrient
deﬁciency impairs muscle growth and increases overall fatness
(Funston, Larson, & Vonnahme, 2010; Funston & Summers, 2013;
Greenwood & Cafe, 2007). Maternal nutritional status may inﬂuence
fetal adipose tissue development by changing the prevailing glucose en-
vironment (Symonds & Lomax, 1992; Symonds, Pope, Sharkey, & Budge,
2012). During the early and middle gestation, maternal nutrition
restriction enhances glucose exchange capacity (Dandrea et al., 2001;
Heasman, Clarke, Firth, Stephenson, & Symonds, 1998)andmayin-
crease sensitivity to insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) to promote
adipose development (Symonds et al., 2012). Once the maternal dietary
intake is restored in the late stage of gestation, fetal glucose supply is in-
creased and lead to fetal adiposity (Bispham et al., 2005; Zhu et al.,
2006). In addition, the reduced muscle mass due to maternal nutrient
deﬁciency reduces nutrient consumption in the muscle, and the exces-
sive energy is diverted to adipose development and lipid accumulation,
increasing the overall fatness of offspring.
Up to now, most studies in livestock focus on the effect of maternal
nutrient deﬁciency on the performance of offspring. In addition, increas-
ing evidence also points the impact of maternal over-nutrition on fetal
development. Over-feeding of sows from conception to day 50 of
gestation increased offspring fatness at birth, and the muscle ﬁber
type composition of progeny was altered due to maternal nutritional
management (Bee, 2004). Rats born to mothers fed a high fat diet either
during gestation alone or during both gestation and lactation exhibited
a 25% reduction in muscle cross-sectional area with approximately 20%
fewer ﬁbers compared with pups born to mothers fed a balanced chow
diet (Bayol, Simbi, & Stickland, 2005). In sheep, maternal over-nutrition
enhances intramuscular adipocyte density of fetuses at the late gesta-
tion (Yan et al., 2010) and also the resulting lambs (Huang et al.,
2012b; Yan et al., 2011). Besides enhanced adipocyte development
due to maternal obesity, maternal over-nutrition or obesity increases
fetal circulatory leptin levels (Kleiblova et al., 2010; Uzelac et al.,
2010), which may induce leptin resistance in the offspring and lead to
hyperphagia (Rooney & Ozanne, 2011).
The maternal impact on offspring growth and development is not
only limited to the fetal stage, and increasing evidence points to the
importance of lactation nutrition on offspring adipose development.
Maternal high energy intake during lactation affects milk composition,
which has long-term effect on the offspring metabolism (Vogt et al.,
2014). Maternal milk composition is altered by maternal diet, providing
another opportunity to alter neonatal nutrition and thus, offspring
development and subsequent meat production.
2.5. Application of fetal programming to meat production
In many parts of the world, drought is a recurring meteorological
problem, which reduces forage production. As a result, sheep and cattle,
which primarily depend on forage, undergo periodic nutrient deﬁciency.
Due to the seasonal nature of cow and sheep reproduction, they fre-
quently experience nutrient deﬁciency during pregnancy due to limited
forage availability. Maternal nutrient deﬁciency potentially reduce mus-
cle ﬁber numbers, decrease marbling and increase overall fatness of off-
spring animals (Du et al., 2013). To avoid negative effects of nutrient
deﬁciency on meat production of offspring, nutrient supplementation
is needed. Nutrient supplementation during the second trimester
in ruminant animals promotes muscle development, which increase
muscle mass and overall production efﬁciency of offspring (Du et al.,
2010), while supplementation at late gestation only increases muscle
ﬁber sizes without change in muscle ﬁber number (Du et al., 2011;
Greenwood & Cafe, 2007). On the other hand, nutrient supplementation
at middle to late gestation, a stage corresponding to adipose tissue
development, promotes adipogenesis (Underwood et al., 2008).
Marbling is correlated with the number and size of adipocytes inside
the skeletal muscle. Increased adipogenesis in fetal muscle leads to
an increase in the number of intramuscular adipocytes. These intramus-
cular adipocytes accumulate fat during post-natal growth, forming
marbling. For calves which experienced nutrient deﬁciency during late
gestation and lactation stages, there are less intramuscular progenitors
formed, which limits marbling formation during the ‘ﬁnishing’ phase.
Therefore, maternal nutritional management to increase adipogenesis
in fetal muscle is an effective method for promoting marbling
(Du et al., 2013). The approximate timelines for muscle and adipose

3. Epigenetic mechanisms of fetal programming
While the genotype associated with high efﬁciency and quality of
meat production can be improved through genetic selection, the growth
efﬁciency of animals is also profoundly affected by epigenetic changes.
Environmental factors, especially nutrients, available for fetuses shape
the epigenetic landscapes of cells and tissues relevant to meat produc-
tion. During animal early development, stem cells and progenitor cells
maintain their pluri- or multi-potency through reversible inhibition of
lineage-speciﬁc genes while allowing genes for self-renewal to express
(Meissner et al., 2008a,b; Mohn et al., 2008). Polycomb repression com-
plexes (PRCs), including PRC1 and 2, are mainly responsible for revers-
ible inhibition of genes. One core component of PRC2 is EZH2 (Enhancer
of Zeste 2), which catalyzes histone 3 lysine 27 trimethylation
(H3K27me3) (McCabe et al., 2012; Qi et al., 2012), a marker for gene
silencing (Bernstein et al., 2006). One the other hand, trithorax group
(trxG) catalyzes H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3), which activates
gene transcription (Schuettengruber, Martinez, Iovino, & Cavalli,
2011). Interestingly, H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 co-exist, forming a
‘bivalent state’, which poise genes for activation or inhibition
(Meissner et al., 2008a,b; Mikkelsen et al., 2007). Most divalent genes
are key developmental genes; during differentiation, non-induced biva-
lent developmental genes lost active H3K4me3 but kept repressive
H3K27me3 mark (Schuettengruber & Cavalli, 2009), leading to largely
permanent inhibition of gene expression by inducing DNA methylation
(Mohn et al., 2008).
In our previous studies, we observed that adipogenic differentiation
was enhanced in fetuses of obese dams (Yan et al., 2010; Zhu et al.,
2008), and increased intramuscular fat content in offspring (Yan et al.,
2011), as well as overall adiposity (Samuelsson et al., 2008; Tong
et al., 2011). Our bioinformatics analyses show that the Zfp423 promot-
er presents exceptionally rich CpG sites and islands, meeting the charac-
teristics of “key developmental gene” with high CpG density promoters
(Meissner et al., 2008a,b). We observed that maternal obesity reduced
DNA methylation in the Zfp423 promoter by about 50% (Yang et al.,
2013), which aligned with the higher expression of Zfp423 in obese
fetal tissue. Our data has been independently conﬁrmed by another
study in rats (Borengasser et al., 2013). In addition, the H3K27me3
and EZH2 levels in the Zfp423 promoter were lower in obese compared
to control fetal tissue, consistent with the lower DNA methylation and
the high expression of Zfp423. We further observed that the DNA meth-
ylation changes in adipogenic progenitor cells during the fetal stage has
long-term impacts on the DNA methylation and adipogenic capacity of
adipogenic progenitor cells separated from offspring fat (Unpublished
data). Similar epigenetic changes likely occur in other tissues and organs
(Sebert, Sharkey, Budge, & Symonds, 2011; Simmons, 2011). In summary,
epigenetic changes during early fetal development alters epigenetic
signature in progenitor cells and likely differentiated cells in offspring
adipose tissue, providing a key mechanism linking maternal nutrition to
long-term performance of offspring.
4. Fetal programming in poultry and ﬁsh
Up to date, studies on fetal programming mainly focuses on
mammalian species, and the impact of maternal nutrition on poultry
is far less studied. Similar to mammals, the muscle development in
chicken is mainly accomplished during the embryonic stage (in ovo)
(Al-Musawi, Lock, Simbi, Bayol, & Stickland, 2011; Velleman, 2007),
and environmental factors affect embryo development. A recent study
compared the effect of exposure to high hatching temperature on the
growth performance of chicken. Brieﬂy, eggs were challenged with
39.5 °C for 12 h/d between E7 to E16 compared to 37.8 °C. Chicken
were harvested at day 35 and day 70, and found that the exposure to
a high incubation temperature reduced abdominal fat pad by 8% but in-
creased breast muscle yield (Loyau et al., 2013), and the feed conversion
ratio was also improved (Piestun, Druyan, Brake, & Yahav, 2013), clearly
showing the programming effect of early development on the subse-
quent performance of chicken. In another study, the impact of incuba-
tion temperature (37.5 versus 38.5 °C) on muscle development of
chicken embryos of laying hens and broilers was compared, and found
that high incubation temperature enhanced muscle growth only in
laying chicks (Al-Musawi, Stickland, & Bayol, 2012). In a recent study,
the effect of eggshell temperature on the hatchability of chicks and
their growth performance was compared and found that hatching
temperature affects the feed efﬁciency and body weight (Ipek, Sahan,
Baycan, & Sozcu, 2014). Interestingly, low-protein diet to hens reduced
the hatching weight of chicks; however, these chicks demonstrated
faster post-hatch growth and resulted in heavier chicken at harvest
(Rao et al., 2009). Besides, hatching temperature, the egg sizes
determine the nutrients available for embryo development, which is
expected to impact the growth performance of chicken. In summary,
despite limited studies, available evidence strongly support that envi-

5. Conclusions
Available studies clearly show that maternal nutrition affects the de-
velopment of fetal skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, which exert long-
term effects on the growth performance and meat quality of offspring.
In mammalian livestock and poultry, maternal under-nutrition during
fetal development reduces skeletal muscle ﬁber number, increases
fatness and reduces growth performance of progeny. Proper nutrient
supplementation, however, improves fetal skeletal muscle develop-
ment and adipogenesis in fetal and pre-weaning skeletal muscle,
enhancing marbling in offspring. Increasing evidence also demonstrates
the programming effects of hatching temperature on the growth perfor-
mance of chicken. Up to now, very few evidence is available regarding to
the ﬁsh. More studies on the programming effects of chicken and ﬁsh
are apparently needed.
