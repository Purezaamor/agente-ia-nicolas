II. GH Actions in Vivo and in Vitro
A. Effects  of GH on muscle growth in whole animals
1. Effects on muscle mass. Studies on effects of GH in muscle
have been done on a variety of models, from intact and
hypophysectomized rats to dairy cattle, and from cultured
cells to whole animals. It has been known for a long time that
administration of GH to hypophysectomized animals causes
an increase in muscle mass, at least in part because of in-
creased activity (and higher yields) of the polyribosomes
from skeletal muscles (19), although exogenous GH has little
or no effect on rapidly growing intact young rats. A strong
indication that GH is the primary pituitary controller of
skeletal muscle growth was provided by the observation that
treatment of intact rats with a polyclonal antiserum to rat GH
for 3 or 8 weeks substantially reduced the weight, total pro-
tein, and RNA content of hind limb muscles (20), probably
because there was a 80-90% decrease in IGF-I levels in se-
rum. Loughna and Bates (21) found that hypophysectomy
reduced, and GH treatment partially restored, the levels of
mRNA for all forms of myosin heavy chain (MHC), and the
increase in type 2B and neonatal MHC was dramatic. Type
1 (slow) MHC mRNA was not elevated by GH treatment in
ad libitum fed animals, but it was elevated in animals on a
restricted diet.
Apparently intact (i.e. nonhypophysectomized) farm ani-
mals are more sensitive to exogenous GH than are laboratory
rats. The effects of GH in stimulating increased muscle mass
in pigs were dose-dependent, with an optimum at 50-100
ju-g/kg of body weight per day (22). Even in  utero, GH had
some effect; treatment of pregnant sows early in gestation
(day 10 to 24) with porcine somatotropin induced formation
of significantly more muscle fibers in skeletal muscles in the
fetus, generally accelerated its development, and gave higher
body weights at birth (23). Time of administration of GH had
no effect on the response of pigs to GH administration (24).
However, Ramsay et al. (25) reported that pGH treatment of
swine gave different effects in different tissues at different
times and suggested that some conflicting reports of GH
effects might result from different tissue sampling or sample
timing. Pell and Bates (26) studied effects of GH on lambs and
demonstrated that GH caused an increased protein synthetic
capacity (i.e. increased RNA content) of the muscle, and the
rate of protein synthesis per unit RNA also increased; they '
concluded that GH was the only anabolic agent known to act
by increasing rates of protein synthesis rather than decreas-
ing rates of protein degradation. In parallel measurements of ,
effects of GH on protein synthetic rates in steers, there were
increases in protein synthetic rates in GH-treated steers
(measured as  [14C]leucine incorporation appropriately cor-
rected for the specific radioactivity of the intracellular labeled
precursor) (27), and they also found increased nucleic acid
levels. Thus, it appears that our early results (19) with hy-
pophysectomized rats have more recent parallels in much '
larger animals.
2. Relationship to  exercise. A number of studies report that  v -
circulating levels of GH increase in individuals after exercise.
For example, there were significant increases in serum GH
(as well as testosterone, cortisol, |3-endorphin, and lactate)
after snatch lift exercise in elite male Olympic-style weight
lifters (28), and this kind of increase could be demonstrated
even in older subjects aged 69.6 ± 1.1 yr (29). As indicated
elsewhere in this review, these increases in GH are accom-
panied by similar (or greater) increases in circulating IGFs,
which may be the proximal stimulators of muscle growth.
However, GH did not increase muscle growth and strength
when administered to exercising young (30) or older men (31,
32). Thus, the use of GH as a "performance-enhancing" treat-
ment may be ineffective as well as dangerous (33) and illegal.
The space program has made very clear the importance of
regular exercise to maintenance of bone and muscle  mass. A
widely used earthbound model of weightlessness is the hind-
limb suspension system in which the rear legs of the rat are
suspended from the upper part of the cage, thus preventing
their being used in any weight-bearing process. A number of
studies have attempted to assess the possible value of GH
treatment in ameliorating the atrophy associated with  H
weightlessness. For example, administration of GH to ani-
mals in simulated microgravity (hindlimb suspension) main-
tained the weights of fast extensor and flexor muscles but not
of slow muscles (34). This followed up and extended an
earlier report (35) that GH treatment did not prevent soleus
atrophy during actual space flight. A combination of exercise
and GH treatment gave the best attenuation of loss in un-
weighted fast-twitch skeletal muscles (36), and GH treatment
attenuated the degenerative effects of limb immobilization,
even in aged rats (37).
3. Effects in  GH-deficient adults. It is well known that adults
with long-standing GH deficiency have reduced skeletal
muscle mass (38), and this includes essentially all skeletal
muscles (39). Treatment of such patients with GH increases
lean tissue and skeletal muscle mass (40), but there are some
potential drawbacks to long-term treatment of such patients
(41). The loss of pulsatile secretion of GH with aging (42,43)
and the associated lowering of circulating IGF levels (43)
suggests that "replacement" therapy of the aged might have '

beneficial effects. A number of publications indicate that this
may indeed be the case. For example, Sonntag et al. (44) found
that treatment of old rats with GH restored protein synthetic
rates, and others showed that GH also remedied the low
membrane conductance associated with aging in rats (45).
Hypophysectomy in young animals caused a substantial de-
crease in fast type 1 fibers in both soleus (primarily slow) and
extensor digitorum longus (primarily fast) muscles, and
treatment with GH reversed this effect (46). We (47) found
that the previously reported age-related decreases in fast
fibers did not occur in specific pathogen-free (SPF) rats
housed in a barrier colony; i.e. the age-related changes in fiber
composition of muscles appeared to result from a series of
unrecorded disease states typical of a conventional animal
colony, not from the process of aging  itself.  This lack of
age-related change in fiber composition was independently
confirmed by others (48) who also used SPF animals, and it
is a dramatic example of the problems that can arise in aging
studies done in conventional animal colonies.
Nevertheless, there is a good deal of evidence that there
might be beneficial effects of GH or IGF treatment of aging
subjects. The initial report of Rudman et  al. (49) of good
responses to GH in elderly subjects has been followed by a
number of other investigations (50,51), which concluded that
long-term trials were worth serious consideration. Even in
young subjects, the potential usefulness of IGFs and GH in
reversing catabolic states was reviewed positively by Clem-
mons and Underwood (52), and the postoperative nitrogen-
sparing effect of GH treatment  (53,  54) also encourages fur-
ther consideration of such treatments, but there seems to be
a considerable variation in responsiveness among individual
patients (55).
There is little doubt that GH treatment can have beneficial
effects on the musculature of GH-deficient adults, even after
puberty; beneficial effects of extended GH treatment on the
musculature of such patients have been reported (56,57). GH
treatment of GH-deficient adults for an extended period of
time led Beshyah et  al. (58) to conclude that there was in-
creased muscle strength and decreased body fat, as well as
"improved psychological well-being." Thus it is clear that
GH has the same kinds of effects on human muscles that it
does on hypophysectomized rats, but these studies do not
demonstrate that the effects do or do not involve elevation
of circulating or muscle-expressed IGFs.
B. Effects ofGH in  isolated muscles
The older literature, mostly from the 1960s, reports a num-
ber of studies of effects of GH on isolated diaphragm mus-
cles, nearly always from hypophysectomized rats. Our ear-
lier reviews (13, 14) cover these studies in some detail (and
express some reservations about the rather high levels of GH
used in them). A recent paper (59) on amino acid uptake and
protein synthesis in isolated diaphragm muscles is of greater
interest because it uses lower concentrations of GH and com-
pares the 20K variant (more growth-promoting) to the 22K
native GH (more insulin-like). The conclusion, that "the
rapid stimulatory effects of GH on amino acid transport and
protein synthesis are expressions of the insulin-like action  of
GH and are not components of the response of target cells to
its growth-promoting action" is an important clarification of
an area that has been difficult to understand. This conclusion
might also apply to the results in perfused forearms that are
mentioned below; i.e. the increased amino acid uptake and
protein synthesis might result from early insulin-like effects
and do not necessarily presage longer-term growth re-
sponses. [It is interesting that these early insulin-like effects
may involve IRS-1 phosphorylation (60).] This might also
explain the observation by Zhao and Donovan (61) that com-
bined GH and IGF treatment gives a better recovery from
neonatal malnutrition in rats,  i.e. GH might have some ben-
eficial direct insulin-like effects in addition to its induction of
IGF and IGFBP gene expression. Zhao and Donovan suggest
several other possibilities not dependent upon an insulin-like
effect of GH.
C. Direct effects of GH on skeletal muscle
A central tenet of the somatomedin hypothesis is that the
actions of GH on muscle are mediated by the IGFs, and a
great deal of evidence supports the view that IGFs are im-
portant myogenic agents that certainly  could  mediate the
actions of GH. But this does not demonstrate that GH has no
direct effect on skeletal muscle. An implied assumption in
many studies on intact animals is that GH is acting directly
on the muscle, but this is not necessarily true. Indeed, the
extensive evidence of effects of IGFs on isolated skeletal
muscle cells, and the general lack of such evidence for GH,
requires that the relationship between GH, IGFs, and skeletal
muscle growth be considered carefully. Unfortunately, a
"cast-in-concrete" conclusion cannot be reached in this area.
There are many negative reports of studies in which ad-
dition of GH to isolated muscle cells in culture had no effect.
Effort was made to show effects (62-66) with only negative
results. In a number of studies in which other agents gave
positive responses, GH was found to have no effect (67-70).
However, most myoblast lines (and to some extent, newly
activated satellite cells) appear to correspond more closely to
embryonic cells  (e.g.  L6 cells express only the embryonic
myosin isoform; J. R. Florini, unpublished observations) than
to mature muscle, and it is generally believed that GH has
little if any effect on embryonic growth (see below).
There have been two reports of direct effects of GH on
isolated muscle cells in culture. The suggestion by Nixon and
Green (71) that myoblasts formed by azacytidine treatment
of 10T1 / 2 cells were induced to form myotubes when treated
with GH attracted a great deal of attention because it sug-
gested that GH induced the differentiation of muscle cells,
adipocytes, and other cell types. Surprisingly, IGF did not
have this effect in these cells. However, the same study
reported that GH had no effect on myogenesis by established
lines such as L6E9 and C2. The other report of a direct effect
of GH on muscle cells is by Adamafio  et al. (72), who found
that BC3H1 cells express specific and functioning GH recep-
tors and exhibit metabolic responses to GH  (i.e. the insulin-
like stimulation of glucose metabolism). This report did not
indicate any effect on either growth or myogenesis in these
cells.
Thus neither of these reports, although interesting for
other reasons, really establishes a direct effect of GH in stim-

ulating the growth or differentiation of typical muscle cells.
Both the converted 10T1/2 and BC3H1 cell lines are rather
different from either embryonic myoblasts or postnatal sat-
ellite cells; for example, cells of the BC3H1 line do not fuse
to form myotubes under low-serum "differentiation" con-
ditions. But a direct effect of  GH cannot simply be dismissed.
Some observations suggest a direct effect: Fryburg  et al. (73)
found that local infusion of GH (in human forearms) stim-
ulated protein synthesis, and Fong et  al. (74) observed that
GH enhanced MHC mRNA accumulation. Of course, these
clinical studies do not exclude the possibility that GH might
be acting by inducing expression of IGF-I in the musculature.
A more convincing indication of a direct effect of GH on
skeletal muscle has recently been provided by Smith and
co-workers (75). They showed that the tryosine phosphory-
lation of JAK2 (maximum at 5 min) and STAT5 (maximum
at 15 min) occurred in skeletal muscle (as well as liver) of rats
after intravenous administration of GH at 1.5 mg/kg body
weight. There was no stimulation of tyrosine phosphoryla-
tion of IRS-1, She, or other STAT proteins in either tissue. This
appears to be strong evidence for some direct effect of GH on
skeletal muscle, but it does not demonstrate that an effect on
growth or myogenesis is or is not mediated by induction of
IGF-I expression in skeletal muscle.
D. Effects of GH on expression of the IGF-I gene in muscle
The role of IGF gene expression in any direct effects of GH
on skeletal muscle growth is not clear. It has been known for
more than a decade that cultured myoblasts release IGFs (76),
but the relationship of this effect to GH treatments has been
ambiguous. There are a number of reports of GH-independent
expression of IGF-I mRNA in skeletal muscle. Perhaps the
most persuasive is the demonstration (77) that levels of IGF-I
mRNA in extrahepatic tissues in the GH-deficient sex-linked
dwarf chicken were close to those of normals; in this case,
there was clearly not a direct relationship to GH availability.
Pell et al. (78) found that muscle IGF-I mRNA in sheep was
insensitive to GH treatment as well as to nutritional status.
Similarly, treatment of pigs with porcine GH increased IGF-I
mRNA levels in liver and adipose tissue, but not in skeletal
muscle, although there was a substantial increase in muscle
growth (79). In a third species of farm animal, cattle, there
was a similar lack of correlation between GH levels, growth,
and muscle IGF-I mRNA levels (80). In exercised rats, the
anabolic response to training involved both GH-dependent
increases in liver IGF-I mRNA and GH-independent in-
creases in muscle IGF-I mRNA; in this case, it was possible
to distinguish GH dependence on the basis of the exon der-
ivation of the mRNA (81). Finally, endogenous production of
IGF-I protein in regenerating skeletal muscle of hypophy-
sectomized rats was essentially unimpaired (82). Thus it
seems very clear that IGF-I gene expression  can  occur in
muscle without stimulation by GH.
There is an equal abundance of evidence that GH  does
affect IGF-I mRNA gene expression in muscle. For example,
mRNAs for both IGF-I and IGF-II accumulated in skeletal
muscle of female rats implanted with GH-secreting GH3 cells
(83). In an investigation of the effects of continuous and
pulsatile intravenous GH infusion into hypophysectomized
rats, Isgaard et al. (84) found that the more "natural" pulsatile
treatment was more effective than continuous infusion in
increasing IGF-I mRNA levels in skeletal muscle, as well as •
in rib growth plate. Pell and Bates (85) showed that tissue
IGF-I content was increased more by GH than by IGF-I treat-
ment of Snell dwarf mice, although the muscle IGF-I could «
possibly have come from the circulation rather than local
synthesis. Subsequently, this group (86) showed that GH
treatment of hypophysectomized rats caused an increase in
IGF-I mRNA content of the gastrocnemius muscle, both in ad y
libitum and in pair-fed (paired with hypoxed) animals. A
review by some members of the same group (87) concluded
that "IGF-I may mediate growth in muscle in response to a *'
variety of stimuli by autocrine/paracrine action or in re-
sponse to certain stimuli possibly by endocrine action."
It seems reasonable to conclude that muscle IGF-I levels '
can be elevated by GH treatment (although they can also be
elevated in the absence of GH treatment). Therefore, the
possibility remains that at least some direct effects of GH 4
might be mediated by autocrine/paracrine actions of IGF-I
induced by GH. For that to be true, there must be functioning
GH receptors on skeletal muscle cells, which is not a very
easy point to evaluate.
E. Presence of the GH receptor in skeletal muscle A
Attempts to demonstrate the presence of a GH receptor in
skeletal muscle have given generally negative results in mea-
surements of specific binding of labeled GH to membrane
fractions from either intact muscles or from cultured cells,
but there may be experimental reasons for this difficulty.
Even in situations in which there is a clearly established
response to GH [as in the induction of pyruvate dehydro-
genase activity in human mononuclear cells  (88)],  specific  A
binding has been demonstrated by only one laboratory (89),
although apparently many unsuccessful attempts have been
made (90). In contrast to the general failure to find saturable
binding of GH in muscle of most mammals, Yao et  al. (91)
reported that trout tissue preparations from ovary, testis, fat,
skin, cartilage, gill, blood pellet, brain, spleen, kidney, and
muscle showed significant saturable binding; it is not obvi-
ous why GH receptors should be more readily detectable in
fish than in mammalian tissues. But, to our knowledge, these
are the only two reports in which saturable binding has been ^
demonstrated. Specific binding of ovine GH to microsomal
membranes from fetal sheep liver or muscle could not be
detected (92). Martini et  al. (90) summarize the situation by •
stating that "Specific binding of GH to nonhepatic cells has
been very difficult to demonstrate." In other areas (such as
interactions of insulin with the type II IGF receptor) such
results have been interpreted as meaning that no binding
occurred.
In many cases, investigators unsuccessful in demonstrat-
ing direct binding of GH to cell membranes instead mea-
sured GH receptor mRNA levels. For example, Martini  et al.
(90) measured GH receptor mRNA in human liver and mus-
cle by in situ PCR and stated that "This technique allows
direct measurement of GH receptor gene expression in hu-
man tissues and represents a valuable tool, particularly for
tissues such as muscle, in which the receptor protein cannot <

be measured using conventional binding assays." There have
been many reports, particularly during the past year, of
apparently controlled expression of the two proteins known
to bind GH in skeletal muscle and in other tissues.
It is now well established that liver and adipocyte cells
have a typical membrane receptor that binds GH in the
conventional fashion, although it is clear that binding of one
molecule to two adjacent receptors is required for optimal
activity (93). In addition, many tissues also contain a second
GH-binding protein (GHBP) that is formed either by pro-
teolytic cleavage in humans and rabbits (94,95) or alternative
splicing in mice and rats (96-98). All of the recent studies of
rodent tissues examined the levels of both transcripts, but
none reported a demonstration of saturable binding of la-
beled GH to either of the protein products specified by these
mRNAs.
For example, in situ hybridization studies in rats showed
that both the receptor and the binding protein genes are
expressed in a number of tissues including liver, kidney,
adrenal, skin, muscle, heart, and pituitary, with lower levels
in spleen, thymus, hypodermis, adrenal medulla, and pos-
terior pituitary (99). At least five variant forms of the GHR
mRNA (differing in the 5'-untranslated region) were found
in rats; their expression varied in a complex tissue-specific
manner, and the V3 variant was associated with the cyto-
plasmic protein found in skeletal muscle (100). Although
reports of direct effects of  GH on muscle are rare (see above),
the receptor mRNA is sufficiently abundant that a GHR
construct could be isolated from an ovine skeletal muscle
cDNA library; its structure adds to the emerging complexity
of the 5'-upstream region of the GHR gene (101).
As mentioned above, the GHBP is apparently formed in
human tissues by posttranslational processing of the GHR
(101), so a separate mRNA for the GHBP is not found in man.
The mRNA for GHR is expressed in virtually all human
tissues studied, with greater expression of GHR transcript in
liver, muscle, kidney, heart, and skin epidermis, and less in
thymus, mammary gland, testis, ovary, and dermis/hypo-
dermis (102), measured by in situ hybridization and North-
ern blot; this is the only report we found in which GHR
mRNA was detected by the relatively insensitive latter tech-
nique. The quantitative PCR methodology of Mertani et  al.
allowed demonstration that human skeletal muscle contains
GHR at low and variable (among individuals) levels, from
4-33% of the level found in human liver  (102). Expression of
the GHR occurs in many cell types and suggests that they
might all be responsive to GH, although there are very few
reports of such direct effects. In another report published at
about the same time, both forms of the GH-binding proteins
(GHR and GHBP) were found (by immunolocalization) in a
number of human tissues with higher levels of expression in
the liver, anterior and intermediate pituitary lobes, outer
kidney medulla, adrenal cortex, skin epidermis, heart and
muscle, but lower levels in spleen, thymus, hypodermis,
adrenal medulla, and posterior pituitary lobe (99). Wickel-
gren et al. (103) found that the differences in relative expres-
sion of the two isoforms of the GH receptor are greater
among individuals than they are among different tissues in
the same patient.
Expression of the GHR and GHBP are apparently under
both tissue and developmental controls. For example, there
is a developmental switch from a nonbinding variant of the
GHR to a functioning form shortly before term in sheep (92),
in agreement with the general view that GH has little or no
effect on fetal growth (see below). As a physiological control,
hepatic levels of expression were examined by Mertani and
Morel (99) during human development, and the two forms
of mRNA (GHR and GHBP) were present at low levels in
fetal liver, increasing considerably after birth. These studies
permit the identification in the adult male rat of cells that
might be directly responsive to GH.
Kaji et al. (104) found a transient increase in GHBP mRNA
levels in fasting rats after  1  day of GH treatment in muscle,
heart, and liver but not in fat tissues. The liver GHR mRNA
level was significantly increased after  1  day (but not after 5
days) of bovine GH treatment in fed rats. Both the liver GHR
mRNA level and the net increment of plasma IGF-I markedly
decreased after 5 days of bGH administration in fasting rats.
These findings suggest that GHR and GHBP mRNAs in the
liver are differentially expressed and that the expression of
GHBP mRNA is regulated differently in individual tissues,
at least in rats. The results also suggest that refractoriness to
GH in a sustained fasting state might be beneficial in pre-
venting anabolic effects of GH under circumstances in which
maintenance of brain metabolism is of crucial importance to
survival.
Dauncey et al. (105), in studies of the effects of temperature
and nutrition on the levels of GHR mRNAs in pigs, found
that the only significant effects were those of diet on liver and
of temperature on muscle. They concluded that levels of liver
GHR mRNA probably have a direct influence on growth of
the animals; they were positively correlated with plasma
IGF-I and growth rate, while muscle GHR mRNA may con-
trol metabolism when energy supplies are limited.
Jennische and Andersson (106) reported that GHR mRNA
levels were elevated in regenerating skeletal muscles early
after ischemic injury, but the levels decreased as the regen-
erated muscles matured. The increase in GHR mRNA was
delayed in hypophysectomized compared with normal an-
imals. They concluded that GH affects primarily the early
phase of muscle regeneration in normal animals and that it
was at least partially required for this response.
At the protein level, immunohistochemical studies (107)
have indicated that both the GHR and the binding protein
can be detected in a number of rat tissues, including skeletal
muscle. Based on its wide distribution, they suggested that
the (cytoplasmic) GHBP may have functions other than bind-
ing GH in the blood. In spite of this widespread expression
of GH binding proteins, our literature survey revealed only
one report of specific binding of  [125I]GH to skeletal muscle
membranes, that of Louveau and Etherton (108), who used
"crude membrane preparations containing sarcolemma of
pig skeletal muscle."
Based on these results, it seems likely that at least some
GHR is present in skeletal muscle, although this has not been
clearly and consistently demonstrated by the methods that
have worked unequivocally with many other hormones in
many other tissues. Like so much of the GH/muscle system,
there are reasonable arguments on both sides of the question
and no compelling reason to accept or reject the suggestion

that the GHR gene is expressed in skeletal muscle and there-
fore might stimulate myogenesis either directly or by induc-
ing autocrine expression of the IGFs. New experimental ap-
proaches that might clarify this situation are not obvious.
F. Effects of GH on fetal development of skeletal muscle
Most investigators have concluded that GH has little or no
effect in the fetus (as mentioned in most endocrinology text-
books and reviewed in Ref. 109). However, there is evidence
that the GHR gene may be expressed relatively early in fetal
development. For example, Mertani and Morel (99) mea-
sured hepatic levels of expression of GHR and GHBP during
development, and the two mRNAs were detectable at low
levels in fetal liver, increasing considerably after birth. Ed-
mondson et al. (110) demonstrated a distinct distribution of
GHR/BP and IGF-I mRNAs in the developing rat fetus with
coordinate expression in connective tissues and their pre-
cursors. This suggests that GH or a GH-like peptide may play
a direct or indirect (via IGF-I) role in fetal growth and de-
velopment. The possibility that the "GH-like peptide" might
be placental lactogen is enhanced by the report (111) of sat-
urable binding sites for ovine placental lactogen (but not GH
or pituitary PRL) in liver microsomal fractions of sheep.
Spence et  al. (112) reported that administration of GH to
pregnant or lactating rats (at doses that had significant effects
on maternal body weight and IGF-I levels) did not affect
embryonal or fetal development or rate of preweaning
growth; i.e. GH from the maternal circulation reached neither
the fetus nor the nursing offspring. These observations agree
with a previous report (113) that human GH does not cross
the placenta in a pregnant rat.
A recent review (114) considers the general subject of
hormone interactions between the placenta and fetus, rea-
soning from the results of IGF gene knockout experiments
that at least this member of the GH-IGF system plays an
important role in regulation of fetal growth. However, Pratt
and Anthony (92) found little or no specific binding of ovine
GH to microsomal membranes from fetal rat liver, which
might mean that GH stimulation of that organ does not
contribute to circulating IGF levels in the fetus. But the wide-
spread difficulties of demonstrating specific  GH binding (see
above) weaken this conclusion.
It is tempting to accept the general view that GH is not
important in fetal growth, but reports that fetal body weights
were lower after intraamniotic administration of an antibody
to GHRH make that conclusion uncomfortable (115). Al-
though it is clear that IGF can be secreted in the absence of
any GH (77), it is hard to explain such results if GH is
completely without effects during fetal development.
G. Effects ofGH on circulating levels of IGFs and IGFBPs
The stimulation by GH on liver secretion of IGF-I has been
widely reported and can certainly be considered to be well
established. It has been discussed in a number of previous
reviews (6, 7) and specific studies of control processes  (116—
119) among many others. There is a general consensus in the
field that the liver is the primary source of IGF-I (and IGFBP-
3), but there is considerable uncertainty about the extent to
which circulating IGFs (in contrast to autocrine/paracrine
secretions) affect specific tissues in specific situations. We
will sample the rapidly accumulating literature on auto-
crine/paracrine roles of IGFs, but the secretion of IGF-I by
liver in response to GH is not discussed here.

A. Biological significance  of the IGFs demonstrated by
knockout experiments
The biological significance of the IGFs was most strikingly
demonstrated when their expression was "knocked out" by
homologous recombination techniques. The general effects
of knockouts of portions of the various ligands, receptors,
and binding proteins were reviewed recently by Wood (120).

knocked out, and the results demonstrate that the IGFs are
very important in muscle growth and development. Indeed,
a common observation in mouse lines lacking IGF-I (and/or
its receptor) is that embryonic development is impaired but
embryos are viable. However, the pups die immediately after
birth because they cannot breathe (121, 122). The central
importance of IGFs to muscle development is emphasized by

knocked out show the same result (123, 124); i.e. there is
essentially no functional muscle development in the absence

Braxton et  al. (122) were "characterized by underdevelop-
ment of muscle tissue," and Liu et  al. (121) found a "gener-
alized organ hypoplasia... including the muscles." The
essential role of IGFs in cell growth is shown by the report
(125) that fibroblasts from IGF-I receptor knockout mice grew

B. Actions of IGFs in muscle
•i
1. Effects on cell growth and  proliferation. The stimulation of
myoblast proliferation by the IGFs was first noted in our
early experiments with multiplication-stimulating activity
secreted by Buffalo rat liver cells (62). The results of those
studies agreed with the original somatomedin hypothesis
(11, 12) that the growth-promoting effects of pituitary GH
^ were mediated by somatomedins; none of our experimental
results in the subsequent two decades prompt us to change
that view. In addition to increases in cell number, increased
protein levels and  [ 3H]thymidine incorporation were ob-
served in L6 myoblasts treated with what we now call rat
IGF-II. Merrill et al. (63) showed that amino acid uptake was
JI also stimulated by multiplication-stimulating activity, both
in myoblasts and myotubes. All three members of the IGF
family (IGF-I, IGF-II, and insulin) stimulate many anabolic
processes in skeletal muscle cells (129,130); this is consistent
with their effects on cell proliferation. We have recently
found indications that IGF-I cannot by itself stimulate myo-
blast proliferation; the presence of a serum component we
have tentatively designated Mitogenic Competence Factor is
also required (131). We are currently attempting to charac-
terize the nature of this agent and are investigating the spec-
ificity of its requirement.
The major anabolic effects of  IGFs on cultured muscle cells
from a number of sources have been demonstrated by many
other investigators. Among others, rat muscle satellite cells
(132, 133), ovine satellite cells (134), the RMo cell line (135),
chicken muscle satellite cells (136), turkey embryonic myo-
blasts and satellite cells (137,138), as well as myotubes from
porcine myoblasts (139) and from chick myoblasts (140),
have all exhibited anabolic responses to  IGFs. Activated sat-
ellite cells exhibit many properties in common with embry-
onic myoblasts, but McFarland et  al. (141) found that a sat-
ellite cell-derived turkey myoblast line exhibited higher
protein synthesis rates than did embryonic myoblasts, al-
though IGFs and insulin stimulated protein synthesis and
decreased protein degradation in both kinds of cells. The
active field of hormonal regulation of muscle cell growth in
agricultural animals has been reviewed recently (142), so it
need not be detailed here.
Our early observations indicated that some cell lines are
more sensitive to exogenous IGFs than others, and we sub-
sequently found (143) that this most often results from dif-
ferences in their autocrine expression of the IGFs. This varies
over a wide range from very high levels secreted by Sol 8 cells
to relatively modest secretion by the L6A1 subline, which
was initially selected on the basis of its relatively high re-
sponsiveness to insulin at high levels (144). L6E9 cells are
somewhat more active in autocrine expression of IGFs and
thus less responsive to exogenous IGF (see autocrine expres-
sion of IGFs below).
One of the anabolic actions of  IGFs in muscle is associated
with the suppression of proteolysis, as initially shown by
Ballard and Francis (145) and extended by observations of
many others (129, 139, 146). Effects of IGFs on uptake of
amino acids and glucose have been documented in consid-
erable detail by several investigators, but those effects are not
the subject of this review.
The stimulation of myoblast proliferation by the IGFs is
now regarded as a well established effect that is not dra-
matically different from similar effects on other tissues; much
more recent attention has been given to effects on differen-
tiation and the signaling pathways that lead to both re-
sponses (see below). Giorgino and Smith (147) have reported
that 1 /XM  dexamethasone substantially increases the [3H]thy-
midine incorporation effect of IGF in the 10-100 nM range,
apparently acting by increasing and prolonging the phos-
phorylation of She and, surprisingly, decreasing phosphor-
ylation of IRS-1 and associated PI3-kinase activity. These and
other aspects of IGF signaling are discussed below.
Although we did not realize it at the time, our early report
(148) of stimulation of myoblast proliferation and differen-
tiation by dexamethasone was in agreement with these ob-

2. Effects on differentiation. De la Haba et al. (149) reported
three decades ago that high levels of insulin stimulate dif-
ferentiation of muscle cells. We (150) later showed that IGFs
are more potent stimulators of terminal myogenic differen-
tiation than is insulin, and we concluded that insulin really
acts as an IGF-I analog in this system (151). Similar obser-
vations of IGF stimulation of myogenesis were observed in
a number of cells other than the rat L6 myoblasts we use
routinely. Allen and Boxhorn (133) demonstrated that IGFs
stimulate differentiation in rat satellite cells, Schmid et al.
(151) found increased myogenesis in IGF-treated chick cells,
and Pinset et al. (152) separated two variations of mouse C2
myoblasts that differed widely in their requirement for ex-
ogenous insulin for differentiation. In contrast, we (143)
could detect no stimulation by exogenous IGF-I of terminal
differentiation in Sol 8 cells, but this could be attributed to the
very high level of expression of the IGF-II gene and accu-
mulation of IGF-II in the medium of these cells when they
were incubated in low-serum "differentiation" medium.
The stimulation of muscle cell differentiation by IGFs ex-
hibits a strikingly biphasic concentration dependency curve
(153). There is significant stimulation of differentiation at
relatively low IGF concentrations, but at higher concentra-
tions (>100 ng/ml IGF-I or 300 ng/ml IGF-II) the extent of
differentiation is progressively decreased, essentially disap-
pearing at very high IGF concentrations. This biphasic effect
is evident not only when differentiation is assessed morpho-
logically as the fusion of myoblasts into myotubes, but when
other markers of myogenesis such as elevation of CK activity
or the expression of myogenin or MHC are measured. The
lower stimulation of differentiation at higher IGF concen-
trations is not a simple result of increased proliferation, since
there is no further increase in cell number. Furthermore,
Quinn et  al. (154), who studied a muscle cell line overex-
pressing the IGF-I receptor, found no correlation between the
secretion of IGFBPs by these cells and the progressive de-
crease in terminal differentiation. One explanation could be
that intracellular proliferative signaling molecules such as
c-Fos, c-Jun, or c-Myc, which have been shown to negatively
affect the myogenic program (155), are induced in increasing
amounts at the high IGF concentrations (154). It is also pos-
sible that optimal stimulation of differentiation may involve
binding of one IGF molecule to two receptor a-subunits, as
has been shown for GH (see above).
For some time, there was reluctance to accept the reports
that a mitogen such as IGF-I could stimulate myogenesis;
there were very good reasons to believe that mitogens acted
on muscle cells to block differentiation by forcing the cells to
reenter the cell cycle, as initially suggested by Konigsberg
(156) and supported by many studies with fibrobast growth
factor (FGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and platelet-
derived growth factor (PDGF), among other mitogens (re-
viewed in Ref. 15). Indeed, it is still true that many people
refer to the change of medium to give low-serum differen-
tiation conditions as representing the "removal of mitogens."
However, the observation that IGFs stimulate myogenesis
has been repeated in so many laboratories that it can hardly
be treated as unreliable. In fact, we found (157) that IGF-I
does not stimulate differentiation while  it is stimulating pro-
liferation; there is a temporal separation between these ef-
fects (see below). Under most cell culture conditions, there is
a proliferative response to IGF-I that lasts 24-36 h, and this
is followed by myogenic differentiation.
a. Induction of  myogenin expression.  The discovery of the
family of myogenesis genes MyoD, myogenin, myf-5, and
MRF4 revolutionized our views of myogenic differentiation.
This family encoding helix-loop-helix proteins was first de-
scribed in terms of their ability to transform other cell types
to the myogenic lineage; i.e. 3T3 fibroblasts stably transfected
with a vector that gave high levels of MyoD expression
became myoblasts in all measurable ways (158). It has now
become clear that these genes (which are expressed only in
skeletal muscles—not even in cardiac or smooth muscle) are
transcription factors that (in the form of heterodimers with
other proteins such as E12 or E47) interact with CAnnTG
E-boxes in the genes for many muscle-specific proteins.
These genes and their actions have been very actively studied
during the past decade (reviewed in Refs. 159-163), and their
discovery gave us an initial explanation for the stimulation
of differentiation by the IGFs.
We (164) found that IGF actively induces elevation of
myogenin mRNA in treated cells; knockout animals have
indicated that myogenin is the member of the MyoD family
most directly associated with terminal myogenic differenti-
ation (123, 124). Our tissue culture experiments with anti-
sense oligos complementary to myogenin mRNA indicated
that myogenin is an obligatory part of the stimulation of
myogenesis by IGFs, an interpretation strengthened by the
observation that these oligos did not affect other responses
to IGFs in myoblasts (130). The levels of myogenin mRNA at
49 h closely paralleled differentiation (quantitated as CK
levels) at 72 h through a wide range of IGF-I concentrations,
including the biphasic decrease at higher IGF levels (164).
However, more recent experiments in which control cultures
also exhibit elevated myogenin mRNA levels (but not accel-
erated differentiation) have made clear that other processes
must also be involved in the effects of  IGF on myogenesis (J.
R. Florini, D. Z. Ewton, and S. L.  Roof, unpublished obser-
vations). In addition, the apparently normal differentiation
of myoblasts prepared from myogenin knockout mice (but
their failure to differentiate in the animal) (123) makes a
simple correlation between myogenin gene expression and
terminal differentiation untenable. This is not the only sur-
prising result that has been seen with knockouts of members
of the MyoD family; different strategies to knock out the gene
designated MRF4 (or myf-6 or herculin) gave dramatically
different phenotypes (165).
b. Effects on other myogenesis  genes. L6 cells do not express
MyoD, and MRF4 appears only relatively late in differenti-

antisense oligos complementary to myf-5 inhibited differen-

(167) have shown that myf-5 protein can induce myogenin
expression, thus supporting this interpretation.
3. Mitogenic vs. myogenic actions of IGFs. It is well established
by now that the IGFs, unlike other mitogenic growth factors,
stimulate both proliferation and differentiation of muscle
cells in culture (168). However, this fact has puzzled many
researchers because proliferation and differentiation are two
opposing processes, and it was difficult to perceive how one
family of growth factors (IGF-I, IGF-II, and insulin) acting
through a single receptor (the type I IGF-I receptor) could
send a signal to a cell to proliferate and to differentiate. We
had earlier shown that the increase in differentiation of L6A1
myoblasts was not secondary to increased cell number in
IGF-treated cultures (153), and that IGFs still stimulated dif-
ferentiation even in the absence of proliferation (169). In the
early studies we usually measured proliferation as increase
in cell number 1 to 2 days after IGF addition, and differen-
tiation as elevation in creatine kinase activity or fusion of
myoblasts into myotubes after 3 days. After the myogenesis-
regulatory genes were discovered, we showed that IGF-stim-
ulated differentiation was associated with an increase in
steady state levels of myogenin mRNA (154) measured 2
days after IGF addition. We also demonstrated that several
muscle cell lines express IGF-II, and the extent of autocrine
expression of IGF-II correlated with the rate of spontaneous
differentiation of the various cell lines (143). Furthermore,
results of experiments with antisense oligos directed against
myogenin (130) and IGF-II (143) supported our hypothesis
that the IGFs stimulated muscle differentiation by inducing
the expression of myogenin, and that autocrine expression of
IGF-II stimulated myogenesis in the absence of exogenous
IGFs.
We had consistently observed that, although IGF-I was
more potent (i.e. effective at lower concentrations) than
IGF-II in stimulating L6A1 myoblast differentiation, the ex-
tent of differentiation was much greater in cultures that had
been incubated with IGF-II than in those with IGF-I. In more
recent studies we determined that the lesser myogenic ac-
tivity of IGF-I correlated with a greater initial mitogenic
response to IGF-I, thus resulting in delayed differentiation
(157) (Fig. 3). In essence, the stimulation of differentiation by
IGF-I was as great as that by IGF-II; it merely followed the
latter by about 24 h. Rosenthal and Cheng (170) examined
effects on several early markers of proliferation and differ-
entiation in L6E9 cells and provided further evidence that
IGF-I was initially mitogenic and only subsequently differ-
entiation promoting. They studied IGF-I effects on the reti-
noblastoma gene product (Rb), a nuclear regulator of cell
cycle progression that is present in the hyperphosphorylated
state during cell proliferation and hypophosphorylated state
during differentiation. They found that IGF-I treatment re-
sulted in persistence of  Rb hyperphosphorylation for at least
24 h before hypophosphorylated Rb became the dominant
form. In addition, during the first 24 h after IGF-I addition,
mRNA levels for cyclin Dl and cdk4, the regulatory and
catalytic components of a holoenzyme that plays a principal
role in mediating phosphorylation of Rb, were expressed at
high levels normally associated with proliferating cells. In
contrast to cyclin Dl, there was marked inhibition of myo-
genin gene expression up to 24 h after IGF-I addition, and
only after 48 h did IGF-I show stimulation of myogenin
mRNA. In addition, Rosenthal and Cheng found that cells
incubated with IGF-I in the presence of cytosine arabinoside,
an agent that inhibits DNA synthesis, had markedly lower
levels of myogenin mRNA and concluded that the early
IGF-induced inhibition of differentiation can occur through
a process that is independent of mitogenic effects. However,
the action of ara-C occurs at a relatively late point in mito-
genesis, at the level of DNA synthesis, and it seems to us that
early markers of the mitogenic response such as  c-fos, c-jun,
cyclin Dl, and hyperphosphorylated Rb could still be up-
regulated, and thus negatively affect the myogenic program.

C. IGF receptors
1. Characterization of IGF receptors in skeletal muscle cells. The
IGFs bind with high affinity to two cell surface receptors, the
IGF-I (or type I) receptor, which has a high degree of ho-
mology to the insulin receptor, and the IGF-II (or type II)
receptor, which has been shown to be identical to the cation-
independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (for reviews see
Refs. 171 and 172). The type I receptor binds IGF-I with the
highest affinity but also binds IGF-II and insulin with ap-
proximately 10-  and 100-fold lower affinity, respectively. The
type II receptor binds IGF-II with the highest affinity, binds
IGF-I with much lower affinity, but does not bind insulin.
The IGFs are also bound by the insulin receptor but with
much lower affinity. Both IGF receptors have been found in
skeletal muscle cells from humans (173) and rodents (174-
177), while in avian muscle cells  IGF binding has been found
only to the type I receptor (136,178,179). The type I receptor
is a heterotetrameric transmembrane protein consisting of
two identical extracellular a-subunits containing a cysteine-
rich IGF-binding site, and two j3-subunits that contain an
ATP-binding site and a cluster of three tyrosines at positions
1131,1135, and 1136,  which upon  IGF binding undergo rapid
phosphorylation and are required for activation of tyrosine
kinase activity. Additional tyrosine residues in the C-termi-
nal domains of the /3-subunit are also important in signal
transduction by the IGF-I receptor. The type II receptor, on
the other hand, consists of a monomeric polypeptide chain
that is primarily extracellular, has no tyrosine kinase activity,
and is involved in the trafficking of lysosomal enzymes (180).
Variants of the IGF-I receptor not having the structure of
the typical type I IGF-I receptor have also been described.
Due to the close homology of the receptors for IGF-I and
insulin, hybrid receptors consisting of one IGF a/3-half re-
ceptor and one insulin a/3-half receptor occur in many tissues
including skeletal muscle (181). In addition, in skeletal mus-
cle there appear to be developmentally regulated changes in
IGF receptors. In the rat, IGF-II receptors are more abundant
than IGF-I receptors in fetal muscle, but at birth IGF-II re-
ceptors decline rapidly, while IGF-I receptor number re-
mains steady for about 1 month before declining in the adult
(182). Furthermore, while the IGF-I receptor in adult human
muscle contains 95-kDa j3-subunits, fetal muscle contains a
novel form with 105-kDa /3-subunits in addition to receptors
with 95-kDa /3-subunits (183). These novel receptors undergo
increased tyrosine phosphorylation when compared with
receptors from adult muscle and may play an important role
in early muscle development. IGF-II receptors were found to
be more abundant also in human fetal muscle and declined
after birth (184).
Studies with muscle cell cultures have provided further
evidence for the presence of different IGF receptor forms.
Inducible C2 myoblasts, a subclone of  C2  cells that does not
express MyoD and requires IGFs or insulin for differentia-
tion, contain a 106-kDa /3-subunit, which, when myoblasts
differentiate into myotubes, is replaced by 95- and 101-kDa
isoforms (185). In addition, the a-subunit of the myoblast
IGF-I receptor is also slightly larger (140-kDa), has a 10-fold
higher affinity for IGF-II than IGF-I, and is also lost upon
differentiation. Overexpression of MyoD in the inducible C2
cells reduced IGF-II binding to this atypical receptor (186).
2. Regulation of IGF receptors in muscle cells. Cell cultures have
also been used to study expression of IGF receptors  as muscle
cells differentiate, but no general trend has been found, pos-
sibly because results were normalized by different methods.
Beguinot et al. (176)  reported that in rat  L6 muscle cells there
was a decrease in both [125I]IGF-I and [125I]IGF-II binding as
a result of a 3- to  4-fold reduction in receptor number. In spite
of this decrease in receptor number, stimulation of amino
acid and glucose uptake by IGFs increased during differen-
tiation. Using a different clone of  L6  cells, we found little or
no change in  [ 125I]IGF-I binding upon differentiation (187).
Shimizu et al. (173) had reported earlier that there was no
change in IGF-I or IGF-II binding in human muscle cultures
upon differentiation. In contrast, in mouse C2 cells,  Tollefsen
et al. (188, 189) found that there was a dramatic increase in
IGF-II binding with differentiation, whereas there was only
a transient increase in IGF-I binding. However, in turkey
satellite cells Minshall et al. (137)  reported a decrease in IGF-I
binding as myoblasts fused to form myotubes.

reported that preincubation with IGF-I or IGF-II resulted in
the down-regulation of IGF-I receptors without affecting
type II receptors. Rosenthal and colleagues (190, 191) have
extended these studies to show that IGF-II causes down-

cells (192). Since FGF treatment also resulted in inhibition of
IGF-II gene expression and subsequent IGF-II secretion, they
suggested that the mechanism by which FGF up-regulates
IGF-I receptor is by inhibiting autocrine IGF-II secretion.
Glucocorticoids have been shown to potentiate the mitogenic
action of IGF-I in many cells. In rat skeletal muscle the potent
glucocorticoid dexamethasone caused an increase in IGF-I
receptor expression (193). Similarly, dexamethasone treat-
ment of L6 myoblasts resulted in an increase in IGF-I receptor
protein content (147).
3. Role of IGF receptors in mediating IGF actions. The majority
of biological actions of both IGF-I and IGF-II appear to be
mediated by the type I IGF-I receptor in most cell types
(reviewed in Ref. 171). The type II IGF-II receptor appears to
serve primarily as a sink for IGF-II, removing excess IGF-II
from the extracellular environment. However, there have
been several reports showing potential signaling by the type
II receptor through coupling and activation of G i2-proteins
(194), and in Na+/H+ exchange and the production of ino-
sitol triphosphate in kidney proximal tubular membranes
(195). The type I receptor also seems to mediate most actions
of IGFs in skeletal muscle, although some recent studies
suggest a possible role for the IGF-II receptor. The type I
receptor appears to mediate amino acid and hexose uptake
in rat soleus muscle (175) and in  BC3H1 muscle cells (174),
DNA synthesis in chick muscle satellite cells (136), and the
down-regulation of the IGF-I receptor by IGF-II in BC3H1
cells (191). In our study with rat L6 myoblasts, we concluded

that the IGF-I receptor mediated several anabolic actions of
IGF-I and IGF-II: stimulation of amino acid uptake,  prolif-
eration, and differentiation, and inhibition of protein deg-
radation (129). Most of the conclusions in the above studies
were based on the observation that the relative potencies of
the IGFs and insulin, IGF-I>IGF-II>insulin, reflected the
relative affinities of these growth factors for the type I re-
ceptor. In addition, a blocking antibody to the type II receptor
had no effect on IGF-II-stimulated amino acid or glucose
uptake or on leucine incorporation into protein in L6 myo-
blasts (196), further implicating the type I receptor as the
mediator of these actions.
IGF-II analogs with normal affinity for the type II receptor
but reduced affinities for the type I and insulin receptors
(197-199) have recently been used to help to define the role
of the type II receptor in biological signaling. One such an-
alog, [Leu 27] IGF-II, binds with high affinity to the type II
receptor but has a 100-fold or greater reduction in affinity for
the type I IGF and insulin receptors in placental membranes
(198). We (157, 200) found that this analog, when added at
concentrations up to 100 ng/ml, had no effect on L6A1 myo-
blast differentiation measured as elevation in creatine kinase
activity or fusion of myoblasts into myotubes; however, it did
have some stimulatory activity when added at  300 ng/ml, a
concentration that was shown to have some cross-reactivity
with the type I receptor in placental membranes (198). How-
ever, we did not perform any [Leu 27] IGF-II binding studies
in the L6A1 cells to verify that this analog showed cross-
reactivity to the muscle type I receptor identical to that in
placental membranes. When compared with native IGF-II,
[Leu27] IGF-II also was shown to have a very diminished
effect in stimulating glucose uptake in a human muscle-fiber
strip preparation (201). These results further supported the
conclusion that IGF-II-stimulated L6A1 differentiation was
mediated by the type I receptor.
However, other studies have found that these IGF-II an-
alogs with reduced binding to the type I receptor had sig-
nificant biological actions in muscle cells. Magri et  al. (202)
found that the IGF-II analogs with reduced affinity for the
type I receptor had some activity at low concentrations in
inhibiting the expression of IGF-II mRNA in L6A1 myoblasts.
Rosenthal et al. (203) demonstrated that [Leu 27] IGF-II stim-
ulated two markers of myogenesis, myogenin gene expres-

ml) at which the analog did not significantly bind to the type
I receptors in BC3H1 cells; this was verified by affinity cross-
linking. In contrast, this analog had only minimal effects in
down-regulating the expression of IGF-I receptors in BC3H1
myoblasts, suggesting mediation of this action of IGF-II
through the type I receptor. Bach et al. (204) in similar studies
using L6A1 cells found that [Leu 27] IGF-II also had some

required for these actions of IGF-II, it may be  capable of me-

views on the role of the type II receptor in skeletal muscle
myogenesis.
A possible role for the type II receptor has also been shown
in metastatic rhabdomyosarcomas, embryonal tumors that
are thought to arise from developing skeletal muscle-form-
ing cells (reviewed in Ref. 205). A crucial step in the metas-
tasis of tumors is cell locomotion, and IGF-II was shown to
stimulate motility of rhabdomyosarcoma cells (206). How-
ever, the [Leu 27] IGF-II analog also stimulated rhabdomyo-
sarcoma cell motility at concentrations as low as 10~ 10 M, a
level at which it does not bind to the type I receptor. Fur-
thermore, an antibody to the type II receptor completely
inhibited IGF-II-induced motility, while an antibody to the
type I receptor had no effect. Both IGF-I and IGF-II receptors
are present in these tumors, they contain abnormally high
levels of IGF-II mRNAs, and cells derived from rhabdomy-
osarcomas secrete IGF-II into the medium, suggesting that
the IGFs may play an important role in the regulation of
proliferation of these tumors. A blocking antibody to the
IGF-I receptor (aIR-3), but not an antibody to the type II
receptor, inhibited the proliferation of the human rhabdo-
myosarcoma cell line, RD (206, 207), suggesting that the
proliferation of these cells is mediated by the type I receptor.
Additional evidence supporting these findings was obtained
by in vivo studies demonstrating that the aIR-3 antibody
suppressed the growth of human rhabdomyosarcoma in re-
cipient mice (208). The tumors removed from aIR-3-treated
mice also exhibited a marked decrease in levels of p34 cdc2, a
kinase involved in cell cycle control, indicating that suppres-
sion of tumor growth by blocking the IGF-I receptor was
associated with arrest in cell proliferation. These results
strongly indicate that the proliferative response of rhabdo-
myosarcoma cells is mediated by the type I receptor, while
IGF-II-stimulated cell motility is mediated by the type II
receptor.
4. IGF receptor knockouts. Additional information concerning
the potential roles of the IGF-I and IGF-II receptors has been
obtained from knockout experiments. Mice lacking a func-
tional IGF-I receptor die immediately after birth of respira-
tory failure and exhibit a severe growth deficiency (45%
normal size) characterized by general organ hypoplasia in-
cluding the muscles (209). Mice lacking functional IGF-II
receptors die around birth but are approximately  30%  larger
than their normal siblings (210, 211). The mutants exhibited
elevated levels of circulating IGF-II (210), findings support-
ing the theory that one function of the IGF-II receptor is to
remove excess IGF-II from the circulation (212). However,
half of double mutant mice, which not only lacked a func-
tional IGF-II receptor but also lacked IGF-II, survived at birth
(211, 213). It appears that loss of the IGF-II receptor and its
IGF-II degradative function resulted in the accumulation of
toxic levels of IGF-II. Studies with mouse embryos carrying
null mutations of the genes encoding IGF-I, IGF-II, and the
IGF-I receptor, alone or in combination, suggested that be-
tween embryonic days 11.0 and 12.5, the IGF-I receptor me-
diated mitogenic signaling only from IGF-II. However, from
day 13.5 on, both IGF-I and IGF-II functioned through the
IGF-I receptor, while IGF-II interacted with an additional
unknown receptor (XR) (214). Whether XR may correspond
to some of the variant receptor types found in muscle as
discussed earlier is not known.
5. Overexpression of the type I IGF receptor in skeletal myoblasts.
Quinn and colleagues (154, 168, 215) used a different ap-
proach to investigate the role of the type I receptor in stim-
ulating myoblast proliferation and differentiation. Using a
retroviral expression vector they developed several lines of
muscle cells that overexpressed the type I receptor. In L6
myoblasts overexpression of the type I receptor caused li-
gand-dependent stimulation of proliferation and differenti-
ation: there was normal differentiation at low IGF-I concen-
trations but greatly enhanced proliferation and inhibition of
differentiation at high IGF levels. When switched to low
serum medium, IGF-I receptor overexpressing C2 myoblasts
differentiated extremely rapidly and expressed myogenin at
very high levels even in high serum conditions. However,
when these cells were cultured with high levels of IGF-I and
transforming growth factor-j3 (TGF/3), they proliferated be-
yond confluence, exhibiting properties similar to trans-
formed cells. These studies thus showed that the type I re-
ceptor could mediate both proliferative and myogenic
signaling by the IGFs.

onset of differentiation (measured by myogenin gene ex-

way. And while significant gains have been made elucidat-

has been performed in cell types other than muscle (reviewed
^ by Refs. 9, 171, and 222). Therefore, to present a more com-

will be included with specific mention given to results ob-

1. Cytoplasmic signal transduction pathways. The IGF receptor
is a member of  the tyrosine kinase growth factor receptor
* family that becomes activated upon ligand binding. This
ligand binding results in a conformational change leading to
autophosphorylation of several critical tyrosine residues that
serve as  recruiting sites  for  specific cytoplasmic proteins
containing SH2 (src homology 2) domains. However, unlike
other receptor tyrosine kinases, the IGF and insulin receptors
} interact with intermediate signaling  proteins, notably insulin
receptor substrate (IRS-1) and Src homology containing pro-
tein (She).
IRS-1 interacts directly with the insulin and IGF-I receptors
(223-225) and serves as a mediator between these receptors
and many downstream signaling molecules. IRS-1  is a pro-

(224,226). Six of these phosphorylation sites are organized in

multicomponent docking platform, binding multiple SH2

Syp, a protein tyrosine phosphatase (SH-PTP2) (230), and the
p85 subunit of  PI3-kinase (231),  all of which contain SH2
domains; this latter interaction has been shown to occur  in

Grb-2 is an adaptor protein containing SH2 and SH3 do-
mains known to be involved in the activation of the Ras
pathway of growth factor signaling (236, 237). Its SH2 do-
mains function to bind IRS-1 while its SH3 domain has been
shown to associate with mSOS (son of sevenless), a guanine
nucleotide-releasing protein involved in the exchange reac-
tion converting the inactive form of Ras (Ras-GDP) to its
active, Ras-GTP form (238,239). The serine/threonine kinase
Raf-1 is the primary target of activated Ras after localization
of Raf-1 to the plasma membrane (240). Raf-1 in turn activates
a phosphorylation cascade resulting in the phosphorylation
of mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase kinase, (MEK,
also designated as MKK1 and MKK2) followed by activation
of a family of MAP kinases termed extracellular regulated
kinase (ERK) (241,242), Jun kinase (JNK) (243), and p38 MAP
kinase (244, 245). It has been shown that overexpression of
Grb-2 in L6 muscle cells increases MAP kinase activity in
response to  insulin stimulation (227), although the same
group has recently demonstrated that IRS-1 /Grb2 is not suf-
ficient for activation of the ERK family and suggested that
binding of Grb2 to She "may be the predominant mecha-
nisms whereby insulin... receptors activate Ras." (246).
ERK1 and ERK2 have also been reported to be activated in
L6 and Sol 8 myoblasts in response to a combination of IGF
and insulin (234); the amounts  of  ERK1 and ERK2 were
roughly the same in myoblasts and myotubes. JNKs phos-
phorylate the amino terminus of  c-Jun, increasing its ability
to activate transcription (243). Derijard et  al. (247) have re-
cently shown that a MKK isoform, MKK4, was present in
high quantities in muscle and could phosphorylate JNK in
vitro. MKK3, also highly expressed in muscle, specifically
phosphorylated p38 MAP kinase. In other cell types, several
downstream targets of MAP kinases have been identified
including ribosomal S6 kinase (Rsk 90), MAPKAP, and phos-
pholipase A2, as well as several transcription factors (248).
The phosphotyrosine phosphatase (PTPase), Syp, pos-
sesses two SH2 domains that have been shown to interact
directly with activated PDGF and EGF receptors resulting in
phosphorylation of Syp, thereby increasing its catalytic ac-
tivity (249, 250). Although Syp has not been shown to be
directly phosphorylated by the insulin or IGF-I receptor,
phosphorylation of IRS-1 results in the recruitment and bind-
ing of Syp with  IRS-1  via its SH2 domain, resulting in acti-
vation of phosphatase activity (229,  251, 252). Syp has been
demonstrated to function as a positive rather than negative
regulator of growth since expression of dominant negative
Syp blocked the stimulation of MAP kinase by insulin (253).
Other phosphatases may also be involved in IGF or insulin
signaling. Kenner et al. (254) have shown that IGF-I and
insulin stimulation of L6 myoblasts results in an increased
protein tyrosine phosphatase activity. Using antibodies spe-
cific to PTPase IB, they found that this increase in PTPase
activity was accompanied by an enhanced expression of PT-
Pase IB mRNA and protein. Another PTPase, PP-2A, has also
been shown to be activated by IGF-I (255). Thus, PTPases
may play an important role in modulating phosphorylation
states, thereby regulating growth factor-induced signaling.
Many growth factors activate PI3-kinase through direct
association with their receptor. IGF-I and insulin receptors,
on the other hand, stimulate PI3-kinase activity through both
association with IRS-1 and direct receptor association (147,
225). PI3-kinase  is composed of  two subunits,  a 110-kDa
catalytic subunit and two isoforms of the 85-kDa regulatory
subunit, p85a and p85/3, both of which can complex with the
pi 10 catalytic subunit (256). Two specific YMXM motifs in
IRS-1 mediate association with the 85-kDa regulatory sub-
unit through its SH2 domains, resulting in the activation of
its catalytic subunit (257, 258). Activation of PI3-kinase cat-
alyzes the phosphorylation of inositol phospholipids on the
3'-hydroxyl position of the inositol ring. Elevation of these
phosphatidyl-3-inositides is  believed to  regulate cellular
growth and metabolism (259). Giorgino and Smith (147)
demonstrated that IGF-I stimulation  of  L6 myoblasts re-
sulted in a 2-fold increase in PI3-kinase activity associated
with the IGF-I receptor but a dramatic 42-fold increase in
PI3-kinase activity associated with  IRS-1.  Dexamethasone
pretreatment resulted in an approximately 4-fold increase in
DNA synthesis and was accompanied by a decrease in IRS-
1-associated PI3-kinase but an increase in IGF-I receptor-
associated PI3-kinase activity. They concluded that the IRS-
l/PI3-kinase pathway was unlikely to be involved in the
potentiation of IGF-I action by glucocorticoids. They further
showed in a preliminary report (260) that this decrease in
IRS-1-associated PI3-kinase activity was due to an increase in
the a-isoform of the p85 regulatory subunit. They suggested
that these increased levels may act to compete with p85a-
pi 10 and p85/3-pllO complexes for association with IRS-1
and may act as a novel mechanism in regulating cell signal-
ing.

lethal, so  a compensatory pathway must exist. In 32D my-

Another early phosphotyrosine substrate of the IGF-I re-
ceptor is She. The She family is composed of three proteins
of 46, 52, and 66 kDa, each containing a single SH2 domain
(270). She has been known to associate with the activated EGF
(270) and PDGF (271) receptors and has recently been shown
to interact directly with the phosphorylated insulin (272) and
IGF-I receptors (273). Like the insulin receptor, the IGF-I
receptor contains a phosphotyrosine NPEY motif that can
interact with both She and  IRS-1.  Sequence analysis also
indicates potential recognition regions for interaction be-
tween She and IRS-1, although direct association between the
two remains to be shown (227,274,275). Giorgino and Smith
(147) reported that IGF-I treatment of  L6 myoblasts resulted
in a marked stimulation of the tyrosine phosphorylation of
p52 and to a lesser extent of p66 and p46 isoforms of She; this
reached a maximum 5 min after IGF-I addition and then
slowly declined up to 90 min. In dexamethasone-pretreated
cells the level of IGF-I-stimulated tyrosine phosphorylation
of all three She isoforms was significantly higher. Giorgino
and Smith concluded that since the increase in IGF-I-stim-

phosphorylation, IGF-I stimulation of the She pathway may
play an important role in the control of L6 muscle cell pro-
liferation. In our studies with L6A1 cells, we also found that
She was rapidly phosphorylated in L6 myoblasts after IGF-I
stimulation (235). Phosphorylated She has been shown to
directly interact and activate Grb-2 /SOS (276, 277). Yamau-
chi and Pessin (276) have shown that She may actually com-

insulin-activated MAP kinase and c-fos transcription. There-

mechanism leading to the Ras/MAP-K signaling pathway.

Crk (278). The Crk family of proteins contains SH2 and SH3
domains, has some sequence homology with Grb-2 and Nek,
and has been shown to associate with mSOS (279,280). More
recent studies with NIH 3T3 cells overexpressing Crk have
shown that Crk also associated with IRS-1 but that stimu-

from IRS-1  (229). This suggests that Crk may be involved in
IGF-I receptor activation of the Ras-signaling pathway, but
no studies with muscle cells have been reported.
In addition to IRS-1, She, and Crk, other tyrosine-contain-
ing proteins may serve as substrates of the IGF-I receptor.
Using the yeast two-hybrid system, Laviola  et al. (281) have
identified a 50-kDa protein that contains both SH2 and pleck-
strin homology consensus sequences and interacts with the
insulin and IGF-I receptor. Its role in IGF-signaling pathways
remains to be elucidated. PKC, PKA, and phospholipase C
have also been implicated in IGF action in a number of cell
types, but their role in muscle remains to be determined.
2. Specificity of  mitogenic vs. myogenic signaling. Many of the
pathways subsequent to IGF-I receptor activation overlap
those pathways reported for other growth factors such as
EGF and PDGF. Divergences in the signaling pathways must
exist at some point to specify the individual growth factor
responses. Mastick et al. (282) have noted differences in pro-
tein phosphorylation patterns in cells stimulated by IGF-I or
insulin, suggesting that qualitative differences may be im-
portant. In addition, using the yeast two-hybrid system, Tar-
tare-Deckert et al. (283) have shown that the insulin and IGF-I
receptors interact with different regions of the p85 regulatory
subunit of PI3-kinase. Using CHO cells overexpressing the
human insulin and EGF receptors, Okada et  al. (284) found
that insulin stimulation caused tyrosine phosphorylation of
the 52-kDa She isoform, with relatively low phosphorylation
of the 46-kDa isoform, while EGF stimulation resulted in the
tyrosine phosphorylation of both 52- and 46-kDa She iso-
forms equally.
Exactly how these differences affect downstream signaling
and what role they play in contributing to the appropriate
biological response after ligand activation remains to be de-
termined. In addition, in muscle cells, IGF-I stimulates both
proliferation and differentiation, two mutually exclusive
processes. Where and how in the signaling pathways are
these distinctions made?
In recent experiments, we have found that an inhibitor of
MEK activation, PD098059 (285), inhibits the proliferative
response of L6 cells to IGF-I but enhances the myogenic
response up to 3-fold (our unpublished observations). These
observations suggest that the signaling pathways to  prolif-
eration and differentiation may branch at some point before
MEK activation, with the MAP kinase cascade being required
only for the proliferative response. It has been shown (285)
that c-Raf  activity is activated and remains activated longer
in Swiss 3T3 cells treated with this inhibitor, and it has been
suggested (286) that Raf but not MEK or ERK is required for
differentiation of hippocampal neuronal cells. The possibility
that enhanced and prolonged activation of Raf accounts for
the enhancement of the IGF stimulation of differentiation by
PD 098059 is currently under investigation.
We have also found that rapamycin abolishes both the
stimulation of differentiation by IGF-I and its enhancement
by PD 098059. The best established action of rapamycin is the
prevention of elevation of p70 S6k activity (reviewed by Ref.
261). Systems insensitive to PD 098059 and sensitive to rapa-
mycin have been reported in studies of the increase in protein
synthesis and activation of glycogen synthase by insulin in
isolated rat diaphragm muscle (262), and in elevation of
levels in Swiss 3T3 cells of the phosphorylated heat- and
acid-stable protein (PHAS-1) that is involved in the activa-
tion of the mRNA cap-binding protein, eIF-4E  (287). At least
insofar as these inhibitor responses reveal, it appears that the
stimulation of differentiation by IGF-I involves signal path-
ways similar to those increasing protein synthesis and gly-
cogen synthase activation by insulin, and it apparently
branches from the mitogenic pathway at some point before
the activation of the MEK cascade.

De Meyts et al. (288) have suggested that ligand-induced
receptor dimerization provides a signaling device with both
a switch and a timer; specificity then can be maintained
through the signaling network despite the convergence of
multiple signals on common downstream pathways. They
cite evidence that the IGF-I molecule has two (or possibly
three) binding domains in different parts of the molecule.
These two binding surfaces can cross-link the two a-subunits
of the IGF-I receptor, creating high-affinity binding and a
long-lasting association and signal transmission. Cross-link-
ing may be required for a sustained activation of the receptor
kinase to induce the appropriate signal. In the case of effects
of IGFs on myoblasts, the concentration dependency curves
would suggest that the two-faced binding [which would
predict the biphasic concentration dependency curve that we
have consistently observed (153)] is most likely to be in-
volved in the stimulation of differentiation, while the stim-
ulation of proliferation (which shows simple saturation ki-
netics) may well result from a single interaction between
IGF-I and the a-subunit of the receptor (153). We look for-
ward to the development of IGF analogs that may allow
unequivocal evaluation of these possibilities.
3. Role of  nuclear transcription factors in IGF action. Growth
factors regulate the transcription of a number of genes en-
coding proteins involved in growth and metabolism. Several
of the immediate early genes, such as  c-fos and c-jun, are
associated with proliferation and are activated upon IGF-I
stimulation (289-291). Members of the Fos and Jun protein
families dimerize to form the AP-1 complex, which mediates
cellular responses by binding to a DNA consensus sequence
known as the 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetat e re-
sponse element (292).
Several nuclear transcription factors have also been found
to be activated by IGF treatment of muscle cells. Ong et al.
(289) demonstrated that IGF-I treatment of L6 myoblasts
resulted in an 18-fold increase in steady state levels of  c-fos
mRNA. Peak stimulation occurred 30 min after IGF-I addi-
tion, and c-fos mRNA levels remained elevated for up to  2 h.
Giorgino and Smith (147) reported an increase in both  c-fos
and c-jun mRNA levels within 30 min after IGF-I addition to
L6 cells, and the response had diminished after 2 h. Pre-
treatment with dexamethasone resulted in a higher and ear-
lier, but more transient, peak of c-fos mRNA but had no effect
on c-jun mRNA levels. Elevated  c-fos levels were associated
with increased phosphorylation of the IGF-I receptor, acti-
vation of receptor-associated PI3-kinase, and increased phos-
phorylation of She. These events may represent part of the
signaling pathway linking the IGF-I receptor to nuclear tran-
scription factors. We have also observed a rapid induction of
c-fos and c-jun mRNAs in  L6A1 myoblasts after IGF-I treat-
ment but found little, if any, increase in either under incu-
bation conditions favoring differentiation rather than pro-
liferation (235). Rahm et al. (293) examined  c-fos  mRNA
expression during spontaneous differentiation of  L6J1 myo-
blasts. In the absence of stimulation by exogenous IGFs,
differentiation of these cells reached maximal levels only
after 10 days;  c-fos transcripts were most abundant at day 4,
2 days before the appearance of markers for myogenic dif-
ferentiation. They further showed that transfection of L6J1
myoblasts with a  c-fos expression vector resulted in inhibi-
tion of differentiation without affecting proliferation. These
observations are compatible with the demonstration that Fos
and Jun proteins directly antagonize the function of MyoD
and myogenin and inhibit myogenesis (294,  295). Therefore,
it appears that stimulation of  c-fos and  c-jun, similar to the
activation of intracellular mediators such as IRS-1 and She,
is associated with the proliferative and not myogenic re-
sponse of  L6A1 muscle cells to IGFs.
Sarbassov et al. (296) compared nondifferentiating NFB4
mutant muscle cells to parental C2C12 cells under prolifer-
ation and differentiation conditions. When serum was re-
moved (which would induce differentiation in the parental
cells), c-jun levels remained elevated in the NFB4 cells, as
they would during proliferation. Proteins associated with
differentiation, such as myogenin, IGF-II, and IGFBP-5, were
undetectable. In addition, a unique pattern of tyrosine-phos-
phorylated proteins was seen in the mutant cells, suggesting
that the signal transduction pathways that control differen-
tiation may be incorrectly regulated. Differentiation could be
partially restored by exogenous IGF-II or by transfection
with a myogenin expression vector, but the levels of  c-jun
mRNA remained elevated even under these conditions.
IGF-I has been shown to stimulate the transcription of c-fos
and c-jun through specific IGF response elements within the
promoter regions of these genes. Using PC-12 cells, Monnier
et al. (297) showed that IGF-I stimulates  c-jun transcription
via a ds-acting element 132 bp from the transcription start
site, possibly at the AP-1 like site, TGACATCA. IGF-I stim-
ulated c-fos  transcription by acting through the serum re-
sponse element at position -317 of the  c-fos promoter. Ad-
ditional regulatory regions may also be important during
IGF-I stimulation, functioning to coordinate muscle cell pro-
liferation and differentiation.
E. IGFBPs
The actions of the IGFs are modulated by a family of six
high-affinity IGFBPs, designated  IGFBP-1 to -6 (reviewed in
Ref. 172). The IGFBPs function not only as carrier proteins for
the IGFs in the circulation, protecting them from degradation
and transporting them to specific tissues, but also as mod-
ulators of IGF action. Most IGFBPs can either inhibit or po-
tentiate IGF-stimulated actions, depending on cell type and
incubation conditions, but IGFBP-4 thus far has been shown
to have only inhibitory functions. In addition, IGF-indepen-
dent actions have been shown for IGFBP-1, -3, and a fragment
of IGFBP-5, all of which have been shown to bind to cell
surfaces. Whereas IGFBP-3 functions primarily as the major
carrier of the IGFs in the circulation, all six IGFBPs have been
shown to be produced by individual tissues, each cell type
expressing a specific combination of binding proteins.
1. IGFBPs in skeletal muscle  cells. McCusker and colleagues
(298,299) first described the secretion of two IGFBPs (24 and
31 kDa) from rat L6 myoblasts and later identified them as
IGFBP-4 and -5 (300). We later reported that  L6A1 cells also
secreted IGFBP-6 in addition to IGFBP-4 and -5 (10). In con-
trast, the mouse C2 cell line was shown to secrete only a
29-kDa binding protein (188), which was later purified from

conditioned media and its cDNA cloned and identified as
IGFBP-5 (301). However, C2C12 myoblasts (a different sub-
line of C2 cells) were reported to secrete primarily IGFBP-2
plus two unidentified binding proteins (302). Turkey satellite
cells also secreted IGFBP-2 (303). In subsequent studies com-
> paring turkey embryonic myoblasts and adult satellite cells,
McFarland et al. (138) found that both common (34 and 36
« kDa) and different (30.8, 37, and 41 kDa) IGFBPs were se-
creted by the two cell types, but specific IGFBPs were not
identified. A human rhabdomyosarcoma cell line was re-
ported to produce IGFBP-2 (304), while mouse BC3H1 cells,
a nonfusing muscle cell line, secreted only IGFBP-5 (300).
IGFBP-1 or -3 were not secreted by any cultured muscle cell
types examined in these studies. Thus, it seems that multiple
IGFBPs are expressed by different muscle cell types.
In vivo, IGFBP-5 mRNA was highly expressed in gastroc-
nemius muscle of the mouse and could also be detected in
muscle from 7-week post-hatch chicks (301). Major sites of
^ IGFBP-5 mRNA expression were found in muscle precursor
cells in the embryonic rat (305), suggesting that this protein
- may play a role in muscle development; in contrast, IGFBP-2
was not detected in embryonic skeletal muscle. Northern
analyses showed that IGFBP-5 was the major IGFBP ex-
pressed in skeletal muscle from 3-month-old rats. Levels of
IGFBP-5 mRNA were severely depressed after hypophysec-
** tomy and could only be partially restored by IGF-I infusion,
while GH infusion resulted in a 4-fold stimulation (306).
Another study in rats demonstrated that IGFBP-4 mRNA
levels in skeletal muscle were decreased by diabetes and
fasting and could be restored by feeding (307); IGFBP-2
mRNA was not detected in skeletal muscle. However, a
transcript for IGFBP-2 was detected in embryonic skeletal
muscle of the chick (308).
2. Regulation of  IGFBP expression in muscle cells. IGFs and
insulin have been shown to increase the levels of IGFBPs in
the conditioned media of several cell types, and the stimu-
lation apparently is not only due to increased transcription
but is also the result of posttranscriptional effects, such as
stabilization of the protein (309, 310). McCusker et al. (299)
reported that proliferating L6 myoblasts secreted more
•• IGFBP-4 than IGFBP-5, whereas differentiated myotubes se-
creted primarily IGFBP-5. There was a preferential increase
in the accumulation of IGFBP-5 after IGF-I or insulin stim-
ulation, but insulin was less effective than IGF-I in stimu-
lating this increase.
We confirmed these observations and further explored the
effects of the IGFs and insulin by comparing the IGFBP levels
in conditioned media with the steady state levels of IGFBP
mRNA (10). We found that levels of IGFBP-4 and -5 in con-
ditioned media increased with IGF treatment, and insulin
caused a much smaller stimulation. There were dramatic
differences in mRNA for the three IGFBPs: IGFBP-4 mRNA
levels were very high in proliferating myoblasts but de-
. creased as differentiation proceeded in the presence of the
IGFs or insulin. We could detect no IGFBP-5 mRNA by
northern blots of RNA from proliferating myoblasts, but
levels rose dramatically as the cells differentiated in the pres-
ence of IGF-II or insulin; the effect of IGF-I was smaller.
•> IGFBP-6 mRNA levels decreased upon IGF or insulin-stim-
ulated differentiation but increased in growth factor-free
control cells. The lack of correlation between IGFBP-mRNA
and accumulated protein levels suggests that the effects of
the IGFs were due both to elevation in mRNA (as a result of
increased transcription or stabilization of mRNA) and to
subsequent stabilization of secreted IGFBPs in the media,
whereas the effects of insulin were only at the level of IGFBP
mRNA, since it does not bind to secreted IGFBPs. These
results suggested that IGFBP-4 expression was associated
with L6A1 myoblast proliferation, whereas IGFBP-5 expres-
sion is a major event during muscle differentiation; IGFBP-6
may play a role in quiescence (10). A study by Silverman et
al. (311) using the related L6E9 muscle cell line also reported
secretion of IGFBP-4 and -6, both of which increased in re-
sponse to IGF-I treatment. However, in contrast to our results
with L6A1 cells, they found very little IGFBP-5 accumulation
in the conditioned medium. The conflicting results could be
due to differences in L6 cell lines or to less stimulation of
differentiation of the L6E9 cells by the IGF-I since it was
added in the presence of 2% serum, which enhances the
proliferative response and delays the myogenic response
(131).
In C2 muscle cells James et al. (301) found a dramatic
increase in the steady state levels of IGFBP-5 mRNA during
terminal differentiation; this increase paralleled the rise in
the myogenic regulatory factor myogenin. In addition, they
also reported a differentiation-dependent rise in levels of
IGFBP-5 in the conditioned culture medium within 12 h of
the onset of differentiation, which was nearly identical to the
increase in IGFBP-5 mRNA. Differentiation in these C2 myo-
blasts was induced by the transfer of cells from high serum
containing growth medium into low serum; under these
conditions, these cultures express high levels of IGF-II and
differentiation proceeds in the absence of exogenous IGFs or
insulin. Rotwein et  al. (312) reported that in the C2I muscle
cells, a subclone of C2 cells that are dependent on exogenous
insulin or IGF-I for initiation of terminal differentiation (152),
IGFBP-5 was also the sole IGFBP expressed. They further
showed that rapid IGFBP-5 transcription accounted for the
dramatic increase in IGFBP-5 mRNA and protein upon C2I
differentiation after the addition of insulin. The time course
of accumulation of IGFBP-5 mRNA paralleled the increase in
myogenin mRNA. Almost no IGFBP-5 mRNA was expressed
in proliferating cells in growth medium or in quiescent cells
cultured in the absence of insulin. The increase in IGFBP-5
mRNA appeared to be due to activation of transcription
rather than to an increase in stability of IGFBP-mRNA. De-
letion analyses of the IGFBP-5 gene promoter region fused to
a luciferase reporter gene transiently transfected into C2I
myoblasts indicated that a fragment containing only 156
nucleotides of 5'-flanking DNA retained more that 70% of
maximal activation of IGFBP-5 gene transcription. Several
potential binding sites for muscle transcription factors are
located in this region. These results suggest that one function
of IGFBP-5 is to modulate IGF-induced muscle differentia-
tion.
The general correlation between enhanced secretion of
IGFBP-5 and muscle differentiation was further supported
by a study in the NFB4 mutant muscle cell line (296). NFB4
cells are nondifferentiating mutants of C2C12 cells in which

none of the markers of myogenesis such as MyoD are ex-
pressed. Removal of serum, which induced the parental
C2C12 cells to withdraw from the cell cycle, did not induce
differentiation in NFB4 cells, and there was no increase in
secretion of IGFBP-5 as in the parental cells. However, upon
treatment of the NFB4 cells with IGF-I or IGF-II at concen-
trations that stimulated differentiation in the parental cells,
the myogenic phenotype was partially restored; the IGF-
treated mutant cells expressed myogenin, MHC, IGF-II, and
IGFBP-5.
Several factors other than the IGFs or insulin have also
been reported to alter the secretion of IGFBPs by muscle cells.
TGF/3, a potent inhibitor of myogenesis (153, 313, 314),
caused a concentration-dependent decrease in IGFBP secre-
tion from L6 and  BC3H1 muscle cells  (300). The same study
showed that bFGF, another potent inhibitor of muscle dif-
ferentiation (reviewed by Ref. 315), also inhibited IGFBP-5
secretion from BC3H1 cells but stimulated IGFBP-4 and -5
secretion from L6  myoblasts. The effects of  bFGF on L6 cells
were surprising since no other actions of bFGF have been
reported in these cells, and they have been shown to lack FGF
receptors (316, 317). It is possible that bFGF acts through a
receptor-independent mechanism, somehow stabilizing IG-
FBPs from proteolytic degradation, but we have not seen any
reports of such studies. EGF, which blocks  BC3H1 differen-
tiation but has no effect in L6 cells, also inhibited IGFBP-5
secretion by BC3H1 cells.
Suramin, an agent that prevents binding of growth factors
to cell surface receptors, has been used to block autocrine
effects of endogenously produced factors. In the studies by
McCusker and Clemmons (300), suramin markedly stimu-
lated secretion of IGFBPs in both L6 and BC3H1 cells and
reversed the inhibitory effects of added cytokines, suggest-
ing that the secretion of IGFBPs from these cells is under
negative control by endogenously produced factors. Agents
that elevate intracellular cAMP levels also stimulated secre-
tion of IGFBP-4 and -5 in L6 myoblasts; conversely, agents
that decrease cAMP levels inhibited IGFBP secretion (318).
Dexamethasone, a potent glucocorticoid that increases IGF
receptor binding, inhibited IGFBP-4 and -5 secretion from L6
and BC3H1 cells (298).
3. Actions of  IGFBPs on muscle cells. The studies described
above demonstrated that of the six IGFBPs, four have been
shown to be secreted by various skeletal muscle cell lines:
IGFBP-2, -4, -5, and -6. Although there is no one specific
IGFBP common to all muscle cells, there is general agreement
that IGFBP-4 secretion appears to be associated with  prolif-
eration, and IGFBP-5 is induced during differentiation. Since
IGFBPs can either inhibit or potentiate IGF actions, it is of
considerable interest to define a function for each of these
IGFBPs in modulating IGF action in muscle cells. The de-
velopment of IGF analogs with alterations in the N-terminal
portion of the protein, which have normal affinity for the
type I receptor but greatly reduced affinity for IGFBPs (319,
320), has provided a tool for indirectly exploring effects of
IGFBPs on IGF actions. In rat L6 myoblasts, several such
IGF-I analogs with alterations in the N-terminal portion of
the B domain of the protein were more potent than native
IGF-I in stimulating protein and DNA synthesis and inhib-
iting protein breakdown (320). Similar results were also re-
ported by Tomas et ah (321), who used these analogs in
dexamethasone-treated rats.
We found that all of the IGF-I analogs with reduced af-
finity for IGFBPs that we tested were approximately 10  times
more potent than native IGF-I in stimulating L6A1 myoblast
proliferation but almost 100 times more potent in stimulating
differentiation (10,  200). In addition, these analogs were also *
much more potent in inhibiting the autocrine expression of
IGF-II mRNA in L6A1 cells (202). One would expect such
increased potency of the analogs if the IGFBPs secreted by the
cells bound to the IGF-I and prevented it from binding to and
activating the type I receptor. Thus we concluded that IG- *
FBPs secreted by L6A1 muscle cells inhibited both prolifer-
ation and differentiation of these cells. Using one such an-
alog, des(l-3) IGF-I, Rosenthal et ah (220) also concluded that
IGFBPs secreted by L6E9 myoblasts inhibited IGF-I-induced
myogenesis. Similarly, Rotwein et  ah (312), also using the
same analog, concluded that IGFBPs secreted by C2I myo-
blasts inhibited differentiation of these cells. Furthermore,
since they had shown that C2I cells secreted only IGFBP-5, •
they concluded that IGFBP-5 inhibited C2I muscle differen-
tiation.
It is more difficult to interpret the results with L6 myo-
blasts since they secrete three IGFBPs, and it is impossible to
assign a specific function to any one of them by using ana-
logs. More information could be obtained by treating cells
with purified IGFBPs or by modulating endogenous expres-
sion of individual IGFBPs. Bach et ah (322, 323) showed that
recombinant human IGFBP-6 (when present in a molar ex-
cess) inhibited L6A1 proliferation and differentiation in-
duced by IGF-II but not by IGF-I. The lack of effect of IGFBP-6
on IGF-I actions is consistent with the much lower affinity of
IGFBP-6 for IGF-I (323). In more recent studies with L6A1
cells, we found that purified IGFBP-4 inhibited IGF-I- and
IGF-II-stimulated proliferation and differentiation (Ref. 235
and D. Z. Ewton, S. A. Coolican, S. Mohan, S. D. Chernausek,
and J. R. Florini, manuscript in preparation). These results are
consistent with reports by others showing that IGFBP-4 has
only inhibitory actions (reviewed in Ref.  172). We found that
IGFBP-5 also inhibited proliferation and differentiation of
L6A1 myoblasts when added in the presence of IGF-II. How- -
ever, when IGFBP-5 was added in a 1- to 4-fold molar excess
in the presence of IGF-I, IGFBP-5 inhibited proliferation (and
associated elevation in  c-fos mRNA) but stimulated differ-
entiation (increase in myogenin mRNA, elevation in creatine
kinase, and fusion). IGFBP-5 has been shown to potentiate
actions of the IGFs in other cell systems (172), and it has been
suggested that its ability to associate with cell surfaces con-
tributes to its stimulatory action. We also found some cell
surface-associated IGFBP-5 in L6A1 cells (our unpublished
observations) but the significance of this is yet to be deter-
mined.
James et ah (324) have investigated the role of autocrine
IGFBP-5 secretion by transfecting IGFBP-5 cDNAs in the .
sense and antisense orientation under control of a constitu-
itively active promoter into C2 myoblasts. They found that
differentiation was inhibited in cells expressing sense
IGFBP-5, whereas the cells expressing the antisense IGFBP-5
differentiated prematurely and more extensively. These find-

ings are in agreement with our results with L6A1 cells; we
also showed that IGFBP-5 inhibited IGF-II-stimulated dif-
ferentiation, and IGF-II expression has been shown to in-
crease in C2 cells during differentiation upon serum with-
drawal (143, 189). Thus we can now draw a preliminary
conclusion that IGFBP-4, -5, and -6, binding proteins secreted
by muscle cells, can all act to inhibit muscle differentiation
but that IGFBP-5 has the additional capability of stimulating
myogenesis under the proper conditions.
Exogenous IGFBPs have also been shown to modulate
muscle IGF action in vivo. In the absence of prolonged muscle
activation (e.g.  paralysis), expression of IGF-I mRNA and
protein in muscle fibers was increased transiently and cor-
related with stimulation of interstitial cell proliferation and
nerve sprouting as part of a coordinated process to prevent
degeneration (325,  326). The application of IGFBP-4 blocked
the neurite-promoting activity of IGF-I, and both IGFBP-4
and -5 prevented the stimulation of interstitial cells in par-
alyzed muscle. These studies provide evidence that the IGFs
and IGFBPs play a major role in neuromuscular develop-
ment. Figure 7 illustrates the changes known to occur in
expression and action of IGFBPs during IGF-induced myo-
genesis in L6A1 myoblasts.
F. Autocrine expression of the IGFs
1. In skeletal muscle. It is apparent that a significant component
of IGF action in muscle is due to autocrine and paracrine
actions by these hormones. Tollefsen et al. (188,189) were the
first to demonstrate that, after removal of high serum-con-
taining growth medium, spontaneous differentiation of C2
muscle cells was accompanied by increased expression of the
IGFs. They found that although both IGF-I and IGF-II mRNA
and protein secretion increased during differentiation, the
expression of IGF-II was at least 1 order of magnitude greater
than that of IGF-I and suggested that the IGFs may be au-
tocrine growth factors for these muscle cells. In a collabora-
tive study with members of that group, we subsequently
showed that the rate of spontaneous differentiation in several
sublines of muscle cells correlates with their level of auto-
crine secretion of IGF-II (143). C2 and Sol 8 cells secrete high
levels of IGF-II and are relatively insensitive to exogenously
supplied IGFs, whereas L6 lines, which exhibit lower levels
of IGF-II gene expression, are more sensitive to exogenous
IGFs. Furthermore, an antisense oligodeoxyribonucleotide
complementary to the first five codons of  IGF-II inhibited C2
myoblast differentiation. This inhibitory effect could  be over-
come by the addition of exogenous IGF-II, providing strong
evidence that autocrine action of IGF-II plays a major role in
stimulating differentiation of skeletal muscle cells.

nonfusing muscle cell line,  BC3H1,  Rosenthal and Brown
(191) showed that IGF-II mRNA accumulated when cells
were incubated under low serum differentiation conditions.

myogenin and IGF-II mRNAs during differentiation of
BC3H1 cells and found that elevated IGF-II expression oc-
curred after activation of the myogenin gene and suppres-
sion of the Id gene, which encodes a myoblast protein known
to inhibit myogenesis (328). They suggested that endog-

The effects of IGF overexpression have been studied in
both cultured cells and transgenic  mice. Mathews et al. (127)
overexpressed a hIGF-I cDNA in transgenic mice resulting in
increased IGF-I plasma concentrations and IGF-I overexpres-
sion in a broad range of organs. These mice exhibited an
increase in body weight and organ size but only a slight
increase in muscle mass. To target IGF-I effects specifically
to skeletal muscle, Coleman et  al. (330) overexpressed an
a-actin /IGF-I hybrid gene in both C2C12 muscle cells and in
striated muscle of transgenic mice. Cultured C2C12 cells
showed enhanced myotube formation as well as increased
mRNA levels of the myogenic factors MyoD and myogenin
500 FLORINI, EWTON, AND COOLICAN Vol. 17,  No. 5
and elevated mRNA for several contractile proteins. In fact,
muscle-specific mRNAs were higher in transfected cells than
in normal cells treated with exogenous IGF-I, suggesting that
sustained release of IGF-I in an autocrine / paracrine manner
is more effective as a myogenic stimulus than a bolus of
exogenous IGF-I. Expression of this a-actin/IGF hybrid gene
in transgenic mice resulted in a 47-fold increase in IGF con-
centrations in skeletal muscle leading to hypertrophy of all
myofiber classes without an increase in general body weight
or circulating IGF-I concentrations. This is one of the most
dramatic effects of IGFs on muscle growth that we have seen.
It is also striking that there was apparently little or no "leak-
age" of the IGFs from the muscle into the circulation.
Stewart et al. (331) reported that C2C12 cells overexpress-
ing a mouse IGF-II cDNA underwent enhanced differenti-
ation compared with control cells when switched to low
serum differentiation media. This was also associated with
an increased expression of myogenin mRNA and protein as
well as increased levels of the active, hypophosphorylated
form of the retinoblastoma protein, Rb. In contrast to these
results, Minniti et  al. (332) have recently shown that over-
expression of IGF-II in C2C12 cells resulted in enhanced
proliferative growth but decreased differentiative ability.
This was defined by lack of myotube formation and de-
creased myogenin expression. No difference was seen in
either number or affinity of IGF-I receptors. Stewart et al.
suggest that the differences observed between their results
and those seen by Minniti et al. may be due to clonal selection.
Clones selected with an increased fraction of hypophosphor-
ylated Rb lead to growth suppression and terminal myoblast
differentiation, whereas clones containing high concentra-
tions of the inactive, hyperphosphorylated Rb result in a
highly proliferative state as seen by Minniti et  al. Other fac-
tors may also contribute to the different phenotypes seen by
these groups.
Recently, Stewart and Rotwein (333) suggested an addi-
tional role of autocrine secretion of  IGFs: they showed that
endogenous IGF-II also serves as a critical survival factor in
muscle cells. Stable transfections of C2 cells expressing an
antisense construct to IGF-II rapidly undergo apoptosis
when incubated in low serum differentiation media. Cell
death could be blocked by addition of exogenous IGF-I,
IGF-II, or insulin at doses corresponding to their affinity for
the IGF-I receptor. FGF-2 and PDGF-bb also prevented ap-
optotic cell death but did not promote differentiation. Thus
the authors suggest that autocrine secretion of IGF-II is an
early event in muscle differentiation and may mediate myo-
blast survival, at least in  vitro. This is a much more sophis-
ticated version of the "happy cell" explanation proposed by
some to explain our initial reports of IGF stimulation of
myogenesis before we demonstrated that IGFs stimulated
differentiation even in the presence of  5% horse serum (153).
Autocrine secretion of IGFs has also been shown to stim-
ulate the growth of several tumors, including Wilm's tumor
(334, 335), neuroblastoma (336), and rhabdomyosarcoma
(RMS) (207, 337). RMSs, sarcomas of the soft tissue, are tu-
mors believed to be derived from skeletal myoblasts arrested
at an early step in the myogenic pathway. Tumor tissues and
cell cultures overexpress three transcripts of IGF-II mRNA,
6.0, 4.8, and 1.8 kb in size, whereas normal adult muscle
expresses lower levels (207, 304, 337). In fact, it has been
suggested that the overexpression of IGF-II by these tumors
may be used as a diagnostic tool when compared with nor-
mal tissues (338). In vivo treatment of rhabdomyosarcoma
with a specific IGF-I receptor-blocking antibody suppresses
RMS growth by decreasing p34 cdc2, a cellular protein in-
volved in cell cycle regulation (208). Minniti et al. (206), on the
other hand, have shown that antibodies specific for the IGF-II
receptor do not inhibit RMS growth. Expression of IGF-I
receptor antisense vectors results in decreased IGF-I surface
receptors causing reduced growth rates in  vitro, impaired
colony formation, and failure to form tumors in immunod-
eficient mice (339). These results illustrate the importance of
the IGF-I receptor in mediating autocrine actions of the IGFs
and suggest that agents interfering with receptor and IGF-
signaling pathways may provide a treatment for  RMS.  The
roles of autocrine IGF expression in many other tumors is
considered in the next section of this review.
In vivo, autocrine expression of IGF-I and IGF-II have been
shown to contribute to myotube formation in the developing
embryo (340, 341). Antisense oligonucleotides to IGF-II can
disrupt embryonic development at very early stages (342).
Plasma IGF-I levels also correlate with growth. Tall children
and adolescents have elevated IGF-I plasma levels (343)
whereas pygmies and Laron-type dwarfs are associated with
depressed levels (344, 345). To further illustrate the impor-
tance of IGFs in  vivo, transgenic mice overexpressing IGF-I
show a 50% increase in plasma IGF-I levels and a  1.3-fold
increase in body size, whereas IGF-I knockout mice reach
only 30% normal adult weight.
Autocrine secretion of IGFs also facilitates muscle regen-
eration after injury in both normal and hypophysectomized
rats (82, 346). Levinovitz et  al. (347) have shown that both
IGF-I and IGF-II mRNA and protein levels increase during
muscle regeneration, with IGF-I increases preceding those of
IGF-II. Their expression occurs at different stages of differ-
entiation, and IGF-II appears concomitant with the formation
of myotubes. To study the effects of IGF-I on muscle repair,
Lefaucheur and Sebille (348) injected neutralizing antibodies
against several growth factors including IGF-I into damaged
muscle. The neutralizing antibodies against IGF-I dramati-
cally reduced the number and diameter of regenerating myo-
fibers. These results illustrate the central importance of au-
tocrine IGFs during muscle regeneration.
2. Other cell  types.  To put the autocrine effects of IGF on
muscle in perspective (and to show that they are not unique
aspects of skeletal muscle growth), we have also reviewed
some of the recent publications on autocrine/paracrine ac-
tions of these hormones in other tissues. The role of autocrine
expression of IGFs in many cell types is becoming very
widely recognized; our literature review in this area found
more than 250 citations within the past 4 yr. It is beginning
to appear that all proliferative responses in cells of  all types
(with all kinds of stimulators) involve autocrine expression
of IGFs as a key step. Thus IGFs may be viewed as  secreted
"second messengers" for mitogenic actions of many and
possibly all growth-promoting hormones. This is hard to
prove conclusively, but the wide array of observations
prompting it are difficult to ignore; a small sampling of the
October, 1996 GH AND THE IGF SYSTEM IN MYOGENESIS 501
many reports is presented here. At the very least, our knowl-
edge in this area now reached the position that one studying
a proliferative response must seriously consider the likeli-
hood that autocrine IGFs play a key role in the response, and
any system in which they do not do this can be considered
unusual. This review is not centered around autocrine effects
in nonmuscle tissues, but we feel that we must call readers'
attention to the explosive growth of this area.
a. Cardiac and smooth muscle. The compensatory hypertro-
phy of cardiac muscle in response to aortic constriction was
associated with a 50% increase in left ventricular IGF-I
mRNA levels (349), and there was also an increase in IGF-I
mRNA in aortic smooth muscle (350) accompanying this
treatment. Acute myocardial infarction (resulting from oc-
clusion of the left coronary artery) led to increased IGF-I
mRNA levels in rats (351), and ventricular explants of human
cardiac tissue exhibited high IGF-I protein content (352). Rat
aortic smooth muscle (353) as well as aortic endothelial cells
(354) also exhibit responses to various stimuli that appear to
involve autocrine IGF actions. Therefore, all three forms of
muscle are capable of forming and responding to IGFs, as are
a number of myogenic cell lines (143).
b. Bone. It is repeatedly stated that IGFs are the most abun-
dant growth factors stored in bone (355-358), and it is very
clear that they play a major role in bone development and
maintenance (reviewed in Refs. 359-361). The induction of
new bone formation by osteogenic protein-1 (OP-1) has re-
cently been shown to be associated with an increase in IGF-I
mRNA levels and changes in IGFBP levels (362). In view of
the extensive literature on IGF involvement in bone forma-
tion in experimental systems, it is surprising that Strom et al.
(363) found that adult human osteoblast-like cells do not
synthesize IGF-I; it is not obvious why human bone precur-
sors should be so different from those of experimental ani-
mals.
c. Ovary and uterus. The role of IGFs in the female repro-
ductive system has been very actively studied for some time;
a review of some of the earlier work in this area is available
(364). Our attention to this area was attracted by the study
of Liu et al. (365), which reported a strikingly coordinated
increase in IGF-I and the IGF-I receptor in response to FSH
in ovarian follicles, accompanied by a secretion of a protease
that degraded IGFBPs (366). A recent review of hormonal
control of ovarian follicular growth and atresia is provided
by Moley and Schreiber (367). IGFs play an important role in
the function of fallopian tubes (368) as well as of the uterus
(369) and ovary. IGF-I from the uterine endometrium is cen-
tral to formation of the aromatization system at the time of
implantation (370). Thus it seems safe to conclude that au-
tocrine/paracrine IGFs are important regulators of repro-
duction as well as growth.
d. Testes. We found fewer publications on IGF autocrine
actions in testes than in ovaries, but there are a good many
indications that IGFs are also important in male reproductive
tissues. Moore and Morris (371) found high levels of IGF-I
protein (by RIA), and they demonstrated the presence of IGF
binding to interstitial cells from testes. Spiteri-Grech et al.
(372) concluded from a series of studies on effects of FSH on
spermatogenesis and testicular IGF-I content that "IGF plays
an important role as a paracrine and autocrine regulator
within the testis." Itoh et al. (373) demonstrated that FSH and
testosterone together gave larger increases in intratesticular
IGF-I levels than either separately. If the results with skin
fibroblasts (374) can be extrapolated to other testosterone
target tissues, the observation that IGF is an important reg-
ulator of steroid 5-a reductase is of major importance, as the
formation of 5a-dihydrotestosterone is a crucial event in all
androgenic actions of testosterone.
e. Thyroid gland. There appears to be a central role for the
IGFs in the TSH stimulation of thyroid gland growth; earlier
evidence for this was reviewed by Gartner (375). Transfected
FRTL-5 cells have been developed as a model system to
investigate some aspects of this effect (376); the TSH-stim-
ulated increase in DNA synthesis of these cells was blocked
by an antibody to IGF-I. A subsequent review (377) stated
that "the effects of TSH on IGF, IGFBP, and IGF receptor
production and the effects of IGFBPs on IGF action are good
examples of this amplified response." It appears that the
enhanced expression of the IGF-I gene is induced by a TSH-
induced cAMP pathway (378).
/. Other  endocrine tissues. Autocrine expression of the IGFs
has now been reported for many hormone-secreting cells
previously thought to be subject to other kinds of controls.
Olson et al. (379) found that IGF expression is elevated in an
immortalized hypothalamic cell line and that "insulin and
the IGFs can exert direct effects on... neurons"; they also
suggested that these agents may play a role in the early
development of GnRH-secreting neurons. In the pituitary,
Lue et al. (380) found detectable IGF-II mRNA (but not IGF-I
mRNA) and concluded that the IGFs probably affect pitu-
itary function, but not that of corticotrophs. IGF-I has im-
portant effects on gonadotropin release (381), and those ac-
tions are modulated by IGFBPs within the cow pituitary; the
first conclusion was supported by cell culture studies (382).
Autocrine IGF-I also seems to be involved in the secretion of
PRL (383). According to the results of Michels et al. (384),
circulating estrogen levels have important effects on auto-
crine IGF-I, the IGF-I receptor, and IGFBP-2 in pituitaries of
cycling female rats. Further down the endocrine line, ACTH
had major effects on the adrenal distribution of bFGF and
IGF-I mRNA; the authors concluded that these two growth
factors at least partly mediate growth and differentiation of
the adrenal cortex in response to ACTH (385). Thus it seems
that many parts of the endocrine system induce autocrine
IGF expression in processes not previously considered to
involve these molecules.
g. Brain and nervous system. This is an area of very active
current research, and many observations suggest that auto-
crine IGF effects are important in the central nervous system.
Gosteli-Peter et al. (306) showed that IGF-I (and a number of
IGFBP) mRNAs were detectable in brain samples from hy-
pophysectomized rats, and Bondy and Lee (386) suggest that
the specific localization of individual IGFBPs within the
brain indicate "a role for IGFBPs in the targeting of IGF action
to specific cell addresses during brain development." Both
astroglial cells (387) and Schwann cells (388) have been re-
ported to express the IGF-I gene, and these cells are respon-
sive to exogenous IGFs.
h. Other cell types. There have been reports of detectable
IGF-I or IGF-II mRNAs in a wide variety of tissues, partic-
502 FLORINI, EWTON, AND COOLICAN Vol. 17,  No. 5
ularly during development. These include lung (389), kidney
(390), retina (391), thymus (392), spleen (393), and pancreas
(394), among others. Aron (395) reviewed the extensive lit-
erature on IGF-I and erythropoiesis and presented five ar-
guments for a key role of IGFs in red blood cell formation by
the bone marrow, by either an endocrine or a paracrine
mechanism (involving IGF release by bone marrow stromal
cells). Antisense oligos have been used (396) to demonstrate
an essential role of autocrine IGFs in DNA synthesis in WI-38
cells, a line of normal diploid fibroblasts of lung origin.
Compensatory hypertrophy of the remaining kidney after
unilateral nephrectomy is GH dependent in adult but not in
immature rats; this hypertrophic response in young animals
was associated with increased renal IGF mRNA and en-
hanced specific binding of IGFs to renal membranes, thus
suggesting autocrine/paracrine expression of IGFs as medi-
ating the GH-independent response (397).
i. Tumors of all kinds. Autocrine expression of the IGFs has
been reported for a wide range of kinds of tumors and cancer
cell lines (398). The report (128) that IGF-II-overexpressing
transgenic mice exhibited a much higher incidence of a wide
variety of tumors after  18  months of age suggests that IGF-II
might be more closely associated with tumors than is IGF-I
(because extensive tumor formation is not associated with
IGF-I overexpression; see above), and there are many exam-
ples of this. Autocrine expression of IGF-II has been reported
in tumors of childhood (Wilms tumor) (334, 335) and of old
men (prostate cancer or hyperplasia) (399, 400). Tumors of
neural origin have been ascribed to an autocrine loop in-
volving IGF-II, including neuroblastomas (401-403), menin-
giomas (404), and other tumors of the central nervous system
(405). The involvement of autocrine IGF-II expression in rh-
abdomyosarcomas is discussed in the preceding section of
this review. Autocrine IGF-II loops have also been described
for liver cancers (reviewed in Ref. 406), colon carcinomas
(reviewed in Refs. 407 and 408), and breast cancer (reviewed
in Ref. 409), where it has been suggested to play a role in
malignant progression (409). Surprisingly, spontaneously
transformed rat mesothelial cells used IGF-II as an autocrine
stimulator of proliferation, but asbestos-transformed cells of
the same origin did not exhibit elevated IGF-II expression
(410).
IGF-I has also been implicated in a number of tumors.
Thyroid cancer cells (411), pancreatic cancer (412, 413), me-
tastases (to brain) of non-small lung cell carcinomas (414),
gliomas (415), and metastatic carcinomas (416) have all been
reported to have IGF-I autocrine or paracrine components.
Some tumors such as sarcomas may involve both IGF-I and
IGF-II (417). Observations by Baserga's group that agents
such as SV40 large T antigen (418), overexpressed Ras protein
(418), or bovine papillomavirus (419) fail to transform fibro-
blasts from mouse embryos with targeted disruptions (i.e.
knockouts) of the IGF-I receptor gene are striking indications
that autocrine IGFs play a central role in uncontrolled growth
of all kinds of transformed cells.
Not surprisingly, attempts have been made to interrupt
these autocrine loops and thus suppress or prevent growth
of the tumor cells. For example, the use of peptide analogs
of IGF-I that compete for binding to the IGF-I receptor (420),
expression vectors encoding an antisense sequence to the IGF
receptor (339, 421, 422), antibodies to the receptor (423), and
suramin to block binding (424) have all been used, at least in
cultured cells. An LHRH antagonist "almost completely in-
hibited IGF-II release" from MCF-7 mammary tumor cells
(425), and it has been suggested that tamoxifen may exert its
anti-breast cancer actions by suppressing IGF-I formation
and inducing IGFBP-1 (426).
Thus, although the IGFs are not usually described as on-
cogenes, there is certainly a great deal of evidence that the
uncontrolled expression of IGF genes is closely associated
with tumors of a wide variety of types and origins. And we
can certainly conclude that the autocrine actions of IGFs in
muscle that we found somewhat surprising 5 yr ago (143) are
really not at all unusual.
G. Role of IGFs in control ofTGFfi expression in muscle
cells
The inhibition of myogenesis by TGF/3 was discovered
independently in three laboratories (153,313,314); Allen and
Boxhorn (133) subsequently showed that  TGF/3 blocked dif-
ferentiation in primary satellite cells from rat muscle. It has
little effect on proliferation and other anabolic responses to
IGFs in L6 cells (427). All three forms of TGF/3 are potent
inhibitors of myogenesis under most cell culture conditions,
but a stimulatory role has been found in some situations (428,
429). The inhibitory actions of TGF/3 occur, apparently in-
dependently, at several stages of myogenesis. Brennan  et al.
(430) showed that when myogenin protein was produced by
a constitutive expression vector, TGF/3 acted to inhibit the
transcriptional activity of the myogenin protein without af-
fecting its ability to bind to DNA. Autocrine expression of
IGF-II in myoblasts in "differentiation" medium is also in-
hibited by TGF/3 (202). Inhibition of myogenesis by autocrine
TGF/3 was suggested by Lafyatis et al. (431), who showed that
all of the mammalian  TGF/3  isoforms were expressed at the
mRNA level and secreted primarily as latent TGF/3 (L-TGF/3)
by C2C12 myoblasts. It was assumed that these L-TGF/3s
would be activated by an as yet to be defined mechanism in
an intact animal. Lafyatis snowed increases in the mRNA
levels of TGF/32 and TGF/33 mRNAs (1.7-fold and  7.9-fold,
respectively) in myotubes while TGF/31 mRNA in myotubes
decreased to 40% of the myoblast levels when the cells were
allowed to spontaneous differentiate in 1% horse serum (low
serum) medium. As myoblasts under these conditions se-
crete substantial amounts of IGF-II (143), we suspected that
expression of the TGF/3s might be IGF-dependent and pro-
portional to IGF concentration in the medium.
We (432, 433) found that the effects of IGFs on TGFj3 are
rather complex. In the first few hours after IGF-I adminis-
tration, there is an elevation of TGF/3 expression, particularly
of the j33-isoform. This is followed by a decrease to rather low
levels during the time that differentiation is occurring, with
a subsequent rise to higher levels as myogenic differentiation
is complete. In all conditions we devised, elevated TGF/3
mRNA levels correlated with a mitogenic response, while
low levels were closely associated with terminal myogenic
differentiation. This leads to the hypothesis that IGFs regu-
late the expression and secretion of TGF/3s as part of a co-
ordinated series of changes in growth factor gene expression

> that occur during cell proliferation and the initiation of dif-
ferentiation.
It has not been established that autocrine TGF/3 expression
affects myogenic differentiation, but parallel results with
FGF suggest that this is a real possibility. Swain and co-
, workers showed that although myoblasts contain relatively
low levels of FGF mRNA (434), transfecting the cells with an
*• FGF antisense expression vector increased their rate of dif-
ferentiation substantially (435).  Like TGF/3s, the FGF gene is
down-regulated during myogenic differentiation. This leads
us to suggest that there is a coordinated series of changes
when myoblasts are incubated in low-serum "differentia-
J tion" medium; inhibitors of myogenesis such as  FGF, TGF/3,
4 and IGFBPs-4 and -6 are down-regulated, and stimulators
such as IGF-II are up-regulated, facilitating myogenesis.
IV. Other Agents That Affect Myogenic
Differentiation
A. Stimulators
For some time, it seemed that the only agents that had a
positive effect on terminal myogenesis in cultured myoblasts
(in contrast to the numerous growth factors that inhibit the
process) were the IGFs. However, there are now reports of
at least four other well characterized entities (and one on-
cogene) that induce the processes associated with formation
of myotubes and expression of muscle-specific genes.
1. Thyroid hormones. The general myotrophic actions of thy-
roid hormones (TH) are routinely mentioned in endocrinol-
ogy textbooks, and the substantial effects of TH on muscle
fiber composition and MHC gene expression both in heart
(436) and skeletal muscles (437) are well established. Only
relatively recently, however, was it reported (434) that treat-
ment of C2 cells with T 3 induced MyoD expression and
myotube formation. This report has been independently ver-
ified by several other laboratories (439-441). Muscat and
associates (439) characterized TH response elements in genes
that are up-regulated during myogenic differentiation, in-
cluding a-actin and myogenin  (442). A review of the effects
of thyroid hormones on myoblast differentiation was pub-
lished last year (162). The importance of this TH effect is
indicated by the report (443) that constitutive expression of
the orphan receptor, Rev-erbAa: (which is transcribed from
the noncoding strand of the c-erb-Aa gene that encodes TRa)
blocks the stimulation of myogenesis by TH.
The presence of these typical TH response elements, cou-
pled with the observation that overexpression of the c-erbA
proto-oncogene (which encodes the TH nuclear receptor)
stimulates myogenesis indicates that the effects of TH are
mediated by the usual TH receptor system, but Marchall et
al. (441) suggest that there may be some involvement of a
cAMP pathway in primary quail muscle cells. They present
evidence for a pathway involving adenylate cyclase activa-
tion and consequent increased CREB transcriptional activity.
It appears that the quail system differs from the more fre-
quently studied murine cell lines in a number of ways, as
there is no increase in levels of the quail analogs of MyoD or
myogenin mRNA under conditions in which T 3 stimulates
fusion to form myotubes. Perhaps avian myoblasts differ
from their mammalian parallels in other ways in addition to
the failure of their mannose-6-phosphate receptors to bind
IGF-II.
It is difficult to fit the observations on thyroid hormone
effects together with the large body of evidence on stimu-
lation of myogenic differentiation by the IGFs. None of the
papers in this area considers any possible relationship be-
tween the effects of THs and  IGFs, and indeed most of them
do not cite any reports of this other stimulator of myogenesis.
In view of the relatively high rate of autocrine expression of
IGF-II in C2 cells (188,143, 327, 329), it is hard to understand
how Rev-erbAa can completely inhibit differentiation sim-
ply by interfering with the formation of the TR/retinoid X
receptor (RXR) heterodimer directly involved in TH stimu-
lation of myogenesis, as suggested by Downes et  al. (443).
Experiments in which effects of TH and IGF are compared
directly are badly needed to clarify this area.
2. Retinoic  acid.  There are a number of early reports that
retinoic acid (RA) suppresses rhabdomyosarcoma growth by
inducing terminal differentiation of the cells, but it has only
recently been demonstrated that RA stimulates differentia-
tion in normal myoblasts. Halevy and Lerman (444) showed
that 10~ 10 to 10~ 8 M RA stimulated fusion and myogenin
mRNA elevation and elevated CK levels (while decreasing
[3H]thymidine incorporation) in both primary chicken sat-
ellite cells and in C2 myoblasts; they concluded that the RA
receptor-a (RAR) mediated the effect, a view recently sup-
ported by others (445). Downes et al. (446) subsequently
concluded that the RXR was more likely to be involved,
because they found a substantial decrease in RAR (and an
increase in RXR) in C2C12 myoblasts incubated in the ab-
sence of serum, and it has been suggested that the RXR7
receptor has muscle-specific functions (447). This is too far
from our areas of expertise for us to evaluate this point with
any confidence. However, one aspect of the TH/RA actions
on myogenesis is clear; there is a dramatic difference in the
effects of RA and other stimulators of myoblast differentia-
tion. As reported by Albagli-Curiel et  al. (440), the suppres-
sion of differentiation by high serum cannot be reversed by
addition of T 3 to the medium (or by IGFs; our unpublished
observations), but RA can stimulate differentiation even in
the presence of 10% FBS. In addition to inducing MyoD and
myogenin expression, as well as cell fusion, RA under these
conditions also inhibits API activity, thus suppressing cell
proliferation. We know of no other agent that has been re-
ported to induce differentiation in cultured myoblasts in the
presence of the inhibitory effects of 10% FBS.
There is not universal agreement that RA stimulates myo-
genic differentiation. As pointed out by Xiao et  al. (448),
application of RA to limb buds, embryonic somites, and
neonatal limbs inhibited myogenic differentiation in a dose-
dependent way. From these and other observations using RA
and C2C12 cells, they concluded that there is a considerable
modulating effect of other factors in chick embryo extract on
the response of myoblasts to RA.
One possible mechanism for the stimulatory effects of  RA
in isolated cultured cells is an increased induction of auto-
crine expression of IGF-II, as 10~8 M RA caused a substantial
504 FLORINI, EWTON, AND COOLICAN Vol. 17,  No. 5
increase in IGF-II mRNA levels in L6 myoblasts (202). How-
ever, the time course of CK elevation observed by Halevy
and Lerman (444) is probably too rapid for sufficient IGF-II
accumulation to account for the entire effect, although it
might make some contribution to continuation of the process.
Other than this observation (202), there does not appear to
have been any consideration of possible interactions between
the IGFs and RA in affecting differentiation.
3. Interleukin-15. Quinn et al. (449) followed up the report that
the newly discovered interleukin-15 (IL-15) is expressed at
high levels in skeletal muscle by testing its effects on cultured
skeletal muscle cells. They found that IL-15 at concentrations
in the range of  1 to 10 ng/ml had a substantial stimulatory
effect on MHC levels (determined by Western blot) in both
C2 myoblasts and primary bovine myoblasts as they fuse to
form myotubes. Curiously, IL-15 did not affect the rate of
differentiation or the number of myotubes formed, but it did
give larger myotubes. This increase in MHC levels could also
be seen in differentiated myotubes as well as in differenti-
ating myoblasts. Whether the effects were due to increased
synthesis or decreased degradation of myosin was not de-
termined, but the larger myotubes formed in the presence of
IL-15 suggest that it stimulates overall muscle fiber hyper-
trophy rather than specifically increasing MHC gene expres-
sion. The effects of optimal levels of IL-15 were additive with
those of IGF-I in stimulating MHC gene expression. In view
of the general suggestion (155,163) that MRF4 is the member
of the MyoD family associated with later stages of myogen-
esis, it would be very interesting to investigate effects of IL-15
on MRF4 expression.
4. Neuregulins. We (450, 451) have recently discovered that
glial growth factor-2 (GGF2), a member of the neuregulin
family of neurotrophic agents, is a potent stimulator of myo-
genic differentiation, giving a maximal stimulation of CK
elevation at a concentration of 1 ng/ml or 18 pM (mol wt
55,000). GGF2 is one of a relatively recently recognized fam-
ily of closely related products of a single gene, made by
alternative splicing to give as many as 15 distinct protein
products (452-455). Most members of the family include
transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains; they are primar-
ily glycosylated, transported to the cell surface, and reside as
transmembrane proteins that can be released by proteolytic
cleavage. The members of this family are known by a number
of different names, including heregulin (HRG), neu differ-
entiation factor (NDF), acetylcholine receptor inducing ac-
tivity (ARIA), and glial growth factors  (GGFs). A new mem-
ber, sensory and motor neuron-derived factor (SMDF), has
recently been added to the family (456); like GGF2, it lacks
transmembrane and cytoplasmic regions and is presumably
secreted by cells that express it. This is a rapidly growing
field of research, and many new actions and interactions are
being reported, but most attention has been focused on the
actions of these agents in the nervous system. There are also
major effects of these agents on growth and development of
mammary epithelial cells (457), and gene knockouts make it
clear that they are essential for early cardiac development
(458).
There are two major reasons for our interest in the myo-
genic activity of GGF2. First, we believe it [and quite possibly
other neuregulins, which share a conserved sequence in the
most likely receptor-binding region (459)] is a strong candi-
date to fill the role of the long-sought (460) myotrophic agent
secreted by nerve cells. Identification of this agent could
clarify the nature of nerve-muscle interactions. Second, we
find that the effects of GGF2 are additive to those of IGFs
beyond the maximal effect that can be achieved with either
agent acting alone. This strongly suggests that the mecha-
nisms by which the two agents act may be different, or at
least, that distinctly different steps become rate limiting in
the two cases. This may help us in our attempts to distinguish
signals leading to the proliferative and differentiative re-
sponses to the IGFs.
5. Ski and c-erbA oncogenes. Colmenares and Stavnezer (461)
made the surprising observation that infection of quail
embryo cells with the ski oncogene, which stimulates
proliferation and induces the morphological changes and
anchorage-independent growth usually associated with
transformed cells, also formed clones capable of myogenic
differentiation. The formation of myogenic cells is remark-
ably high; at least 40% of the embryo cells were converted to
differentiating myoblasts. In a way, this effect of the ski
oncogene is analogous to that of the IGFs; an agent that
stimulates growth of cells also stimulates terminal differen-
tiation, two responses generally considered to be mutually
antagonistic. Colmenares and Stavnezer very thoroughly
demonstrated that the sfa'-infected cells exhibit numerous
aspects of myogenic differentiation, including fusion, for-
mation of myosin and a-actin proteins, elevation of creatine
kinase, a-actin, and troponin I mRNAs, etc. They considered
a number of possible trivial explanations of the apparent
myogenic effects of ski infection and rather convincingly
eliminated all of them. The obvious explanation of these
observations is that ski encodes a protein that induces ex-
pression of one or more members of the MyoD family, and
this is indeed the case (462). Unfortunately, this satisfyingly
simple explanation of the effects of  ski does not hold, because
a transformation-defective mutant of v-ski also causes ele-
vated expression of MyoD and myogenin (and other muscle-
specific mRNAs such as a-actin and muscle creatine kinase)
but does not stimulate fusion. As we have seen occasionally
in control cultures (our unpublished observations) elevation
of MyoD and myogenin mRNAs is not always sufficient to
induce complete myogenic differentiation. More information
on recent work on this interesting effect of the  ski oncogene
is provided in the paper by Engert et  al. (463), which con-
siders some other surprising aspects of the induction of myo-
genesis in sfo'-infected cells.
The only other oncogene known to enhance myogenic
differentiation is  c-erbA, which encodes the nuclear T 3 re-
ceptor (464). In this case, simple overexpression of the pro-
tooncogene is apparently not sufficient to trigger differen-
tiation, but it makes the C2 cells substantially more sensitive
to exogenous T3. By itself, c-erbA completely blocks activity
of AP-1 (Fos/Jun) even in the absence of T3. A mechanism by
which T3 interacts with c-/erbA, RXR7 and AP-1 to control
myogenesis is presented in a recent paper (465).
These myogenic effects of the nuclear transcription factors

c-erbA and ski are in sharp contrast to the inhibitory effects
of other genes including those associated with tyrosine ki-
nase (v-src),  G protein  (H-ras),  growth factor receptors (v-
erbB), and other transcription factors (Myc, Jun, and Fos)
(cited in Ref. 464).
B. Inhibitors
The inhibition of myogenesis by mitogenic growth factors
has been reviewed many times by many investigators in-
cluding us (15,17). It is not really relevant to the central focus
of this review to consider this in detail, but we should at least
mention that it is well established that basic FGF and all
TGF/3s are potent inhibitors of terminal differentiation; some
of the actions of TGF|3s are mentioned earlier. In cells that
respond to them (which does not include L6 cells) EGF and
PDGF are also inhibitory. In no case does IGF overcome the
inhibition of differentiation by these agents.
V. Summary and Conclusions
It is very clear that the GH-IGF axis plays a major role in
controlling the growth and differentiation of skeletal mus-
cles, as it does virtually all of the tissues in the animal body.
One aspect of this control is unquestioned: circulating GH
acts on the liver to stimulate expression of the IGF-I and
IGFBP3 genes, substantially increasing the levels of these
proteins in the circulation. It also seems that GH stimulates
expression of IGF-I genes in skeletal muscle, although there
are a number of cases in which skeletal muscle IGF-I ex-
pression is elevated in the absence of GH. It is substantially
less clear that GH acts directly on skeletal muscle to stimulate
its growth; the presence of GH receptor mRNA in skeletal
muscle is well established, but most investigators have been
unsuccessful in demonstrating any specific binding of GH to
skeletal muscle or to myoblasts in culture. It has been equally
difficult to show direct actions of GH on cultured muscle
cells; the only positive report concludes that the early insulin-
like effects of GH can result from direct interactions between
GH and isolated muscle cells.
The effects of the  IGFs on skeletal muscle are much clearer.
It is well established by studies in a number of laboratories
on a variety of systems that IGFs stimulate many anabolic
responses in myoblasts, as they do in other cell types. IGFs
have the unusual property of stimulating both proliferation
and differentiation of myoblasts, responses that are generally
believed to be mutually exclusive; in myoblasts, they are in
fact temporally  separated. The stimulation of differentiation
by IGF-I is (at least in part) a result of substantially increased
levels of the mRNA for myogenin, the member of the MyoD
family most directly associated with terminal myogenesis.
As levels of myogenin mRNA rise, those of myf-5 mRNA (the
only other member of the MyoD family expressed signifi-
cantly in L6 myoblasts) fall dramatically, although myf-5
expression is required for the initial elevation of myogenin.
The effects of IGFs are significantly modulated by IGFBPs
secreted by myoblaits in serum-free medium; inhibitory IG-
FBPs-4 and -6 are expressed and secreted by L6A1 myoblasts,
while expression of IGFBP-5 rises dramatically as differen-
tiation proceeds. Other myoblasts also secrete IGFBP-2.
Even if exogenous IGFs are not added to the low-serum
"differentiation" medium, myoblasts express sufficient
amounts of autocrine IGF-II to stimulate myogenesis after a
period of time; some myogenic cell lines (such as Sol 8) are
so active in expressing the IGF-II gene that it is not possible
to demonstrate effects of exogenous IGFs. This autocrine
expression of IGFs is by no means unique to skeletal muscle
cells; indeed, it is so widely seen in cells responding to
mitogenic stimuli that we suggest that IGFs can be viewed
as extracellular second messengers that mediate most, if not
all, such actions of agents that stimulate cell proliferation.
The component of serum that suppresses IGF-II gene ex-
pression under "growth" conditions appears to be the IGFs
themselves, which exhibit a very high potency in the feed-
back inhibition of IGF-II expression.
In addition, IGFs have effects on the expression of other
genes related to differentiation. Treatment of L6A1 cells with
IGFs suppresses their expression of the myogenesis-inhibit-
ing TGFj3s with a time course consistent with an initial pro-
liferative step followed by differentiation,  i.e.  expression is
first increased and then very substantially decreased. It is not
established that this plays a role in control of differentiation,
but experiments with FGF antisense constructs suggests that
this may well be the case.
Until recently, IGFs were the only circulating agents
known to stimulate myoblast differentiation, in contrast to
the relatively large number of growth factors that inhibit the
process. It is now clear that thyroid hormones and RA also
stimulate myogenesis, and that IL-15 enhances the stimula-
tory effects of  IGF-I. Infection of quail embryo cells with the
ski oncogene gives a high yield of cells that are essentially
indistinguishable from myoblasts. Thus it can no longer be
said that IGFs are the only known stimulators of myogenesis,
although it can be argued reasonably that they play a very
major role in the process.

at the Sanjay Gandhi Post Graduate Institute of Medical Sciences in Lucknow, India. Instead, this is the
