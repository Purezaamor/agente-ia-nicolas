ABSTRACT 
One hundred sixty crossbred steers were used to compare the effects of metabolizable 
protein and energy restrictions on subsequent compensatory growth. Diets were formulated 
to impose severe or mild growth restrictions for periods of 77 to 154 d. Steers were 
realimented on a high-cuncentrate diet and fed to achieve approximately 1 cm S.C. fat 
thickness. Finishing performance and carcass characteristics were evaluated relative to 
unrestricted control animals. AU restricted animals, with the exception of those on the mild, 
brief energy restriction, exhibited compensatory growth relative to controls. A single direct 
comparison of energy and protein restriction treatments indicated that finishing 
performance during realimentation was similar for these two groups. Evaluation of the 
relative effects of protein and energy restriction by multiple linear repssion indicated that 
compensatory growth was influenced by differences in duration or severity of nutrient 
deprivation to a greater extent for energy-restricted than for protein-restricted steers. Linear 
contrasts and repssion estimates indicated that compensatory growth was influenced more 
by differences in severity of restriction than by duration of the restriction period.

Previous investigations of the relative ef- 
fects of protein and energy deprivation (Win- 
chester et al., 1957; Abdalla et d., 1988) have 
focused on the effects of limiting digestible or 
crude protein intake in comparison to caloric 
restriction. Given the interdependency of pro- 
tein and energy metabolism within the rumen, 
restrictions of dietary CP or digestible protein 
may impair energy utilization. Restrictions 
imposed by limiting metabolizable (postnuni- 
nally digested) protein would circumvent prob- 
lems associated with interactions between 
ruminal protein and energy metabolism. Objec- 
tives of the present study were to evaluate the 
effects on compensatory growth of NE and 
metabolizable protein deprivations of differing 
severity and duration.

designed to last 77 or 154 d, thereby allowing

restriction, as well as interactions among these

a growing period of 77 d during which steers 
were fed the control growing diet (Table 1). At 
the conclusion of the restriction period steers 
were initially fed the cont101 growing diet for 4

gradually increased (four increments) during a 
3-wk period to achieve the final composition

Steers were fed in pens of five animals each 
(four pensbatment) in a partially enclosed 
facility (4 m2/animal) throughout the study. 
Mean monthly temperatures from May 1987, 
through January 1988 were 18.5, 22.7, 24.7, 
21.7, 18.2, 8.7, 5.0, -1.7 and -15.9.C respec- 
tively. Initial weights and weights at the end of 
the growing and restriction periods were the 
average of full weights measured on two 
consecutive days. Cattle were fed once daily, 
and adjustments in feed offered were made on 
the basis of weekly estimates of live weight in 
order to facilitate attainment of targeted rates 
of gain. During the growing and finishing 
periods, steers were offered diets in amounts 
sufficient to allow for minimal refusals. Feed 
refusals were recorded weekly. 
Pens of cattle were slaughtered at the U.S. 
Meat Animal Research Center slaughter lab 
ratory when the average backfat thickness of 
the pen was estimated (by visual appraisal) to 
be 1 cm. Fii live weight was calculated from 
hot carcass weight using the average dressing 
percentage (60%) of all cattle as the divisor to 
remove effects of differences in gastrointesti- 
nal tract fii. 
Statistical Analyses. Data were analyzed 
using least squares ANOVA (SAS, 1982) in a 
randomized complete block design. Dam line 
and location within the feeding facility were 
used as blocking criteria and were included in 
the statistical model as main effects. Pen (five 
animals) constituted the experimental unit. 
Interaction terms were not significant and 
were, therefore, not included in statistical 
models. Comparisons of treatment means were 
made using orthogonal hear contrasts. 
Analyses of covariance (class variables 
were protein and energy) also were performed 
on pen means using the General Linear Models 
procedure of SAS, (1982) to estimate the 
relative effects of protein and energy restric- 
tions on subsequent finishing gain and effi- 
ciency. Steers in the six restricted groups were 
classified as being either protein- or energy- 
restricted. Restriction class then was used as a 
ked effect in the covariance models. Gain 
during the restriction period and days of 
restriction were included as continuous vari- 
ables and were nested within the restriction 
class to obtain separate estimates (for protein 
and energy restricted groups) of the effects of 
duration and severity of restriction. Homogen- 
eity of slopes was tested in a second model 
containing main and crossed effects of restric- 
tion class, restriction gain and days of restric- 
tion.

Three of the 160 steers used in the study 
were removed prior of slaughter. A control 
finishing steer was diagnosed as having polio- 
encephalomalacia after several weeks of feed- 
ing and therefore was removed. A steer in the 
mild energy restriction treatment suffered a 
broken leg and subsequently was destroyed. 
The third was in the long duration, severe 
protein-restricted group. This steer continued 
to lose weight after 11 wk of restriction, and 
therefore was removed from the study. The 
majority of steers severely restricted in metab- 
olizable protein intake perfoxmed poorly dur- 
ing the first 5 to 6 wk of the study. 
Consequently, the severe protein restriction 
diet was reformulated to provide additional 
metabolizable protein, the SEV protein limit- 
ing diet in Table 1 reflects these changes. 
Performance of steers severely restricted in 
protein intake for the long duration was 
sufficiently poor to justify terminating the 
restriction period at 126 rather than 154 d.

Metabolizable Protein vs Energy Restric- 
tion. One of the primaty objectives of this 
study was to compare finishing performance of 
steers that had been restricted to similar rates 
of gain by limiting intake of metabolizable 
protein or energy. Performance (Table 2) of 
steers with limited protein intake was in 
general less than that of energy-restricted 
steers during the periods of growth restriction. 
Therefore, it was not possible to make several 
direct comparisons of protein and energy 
restriction treatments. Most of the following 
discussion of the comparison of protein and 
energy restriction is based on results of 
multiple linear regression analyses. 
Restriction gains of steers that experienced 
brief, mild protein restriction, though numeri- 
cally lower (P > .lo) were not significantly 
different from those of steers severely re- 
stricted in energy intake for the short duration. 
These treatment groups therefore provided a 
suitable direct comparison of metabolizable 
protein and energy restriction. Orthogonal 
comparisons of finishing performance (Table 
3) of these two groups indicated that gain, 
efficiency and feed intake were not signifi- 
cantly different. protein-restricted animals re- 
quired a longer (P c .01) feeding period than 
energy-restricted steers, but they tended to be 
lighter at the beginning (Table 2) and had 
heavier carcasses (Table 4) at the end of the 
finishing period. Quality grade, yield grade 
and liver weight were not different between the 
two groups.

protein and energy intakes covered a similar

Consequently, it was possible to take advan- 
tage of the variability in pen means and make 
suitable comparisons between protein and 
energy restriction using analysis of covariance.

The severe nature of the protein restrictions 
experienced by animals in this study may have 
imposed limitations on their ability to express 
compensatory growth. Hovell et al. (1987) 
observed compensatory N accretion when 
casein was infused into the abomasum of 
lambs previously subjected to low-protein 
treatments. In trials with steers, Drouillard et 
al. also observed a compensatory response to 
supplemental escape protein when realimenta- 
tion was preceded by a period of severe protein 
undernutrition (unpublished data). Protein rc- 
striction can result in depletion of body 
protein, as evidenced by studies of Fattet et al. 
(1984) and Hovell et al. (1987), which 
potentially could increase the animal's need 
for protein during repletion. 
No consideration was given to the possibil- 
ity of an increased protein requirement of 
protein-restricted steers when formulating the 
finishing diet in the present study. In disre- 
garding this possibility, we may have limited 
the oppomity for protein-restricted steers to 
express compensatory growth. In a companion 
study with lambs (Drouillard et al., unpub- 
lished data), supplemental escape protein was 
provided during realimentation, with the result 
that a similar expression of compensatory 
growth occurred in lambs previously restricted 
in either metabolizable protein or energy. 
Previous comparisons of the effects of 
protein and energy restriction have not taken 
into account the potentially negative influence 
of protein malnutrition on ruminal energy 
metabolism. Restrictions imposed by limiting 
digestible protein (Winchester et al., 1957) or 
CP (Carroll et al., 1963; AWalla et al., 1988) 
are undoubtedly important, because they repre- 
sent practical situations incurred in systems of 
livestock production. Perhaps it is incorrect to 
refer to restrictions resulting from these types 
of deficiencies as protein restriction; they more 
likely represent the combined influences of 
protein and energy deficiency. Results of the 
studies cited above are inconclusive with 
regard to comparison of the effects of protein 
and energy restriction on compensatory 
growth, because the extent to which limitations 
in energy metabolism may have influenced the 
results is unknown. 
Differences in gastrointestinal fill are of 
critical importance in assessing the compensa- 
tory response to previous nutritional regimens; 
estimates of body growth based on changes in 
live weight may be prone to considerable error. 
Evaluation of performance in the present study 
relied on measurements of live weight growth, 
and thereby is subject to criticism. Differences 
in gastrointestinal fill have not been dismissed
